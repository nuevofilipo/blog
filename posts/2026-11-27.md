# When the floor fights back

*2026-11-27*

Good Friday morning. Every walking policy you've trained so far quietly assumes one thing: the ground stays where it is. Today's main paper breaks that assumption. A humanoid learns to stand, balance and jump on a trampoline, using nothing but its own joint sensors, and the trick is a better model of the floor, not a better robot.

After that: what human takeovers teach a VLA (and what they quietly break), a puzzle benchmark that no VLA can solve, Volvo teaching humanoids by demonstration in Belgium, an RAI master's thesis in Zurich about robots improving their own training pipeline, and fish in the deep sea that are losing their genes.

## The big one: a humanoid on a trampoline

**[Riding the Wave: Sim-to-Real Humanoid Control on Elastic Terrain](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34569)** (Yinglei Zhu, Qin Li, Honghao Pan, ... Huichan Zhao, 13 authors), submitted 25 November. [Project page](https://fly-pigTH.github.io/wave_rider/). Code "coming soon".

Here's the problem. Standard locomotion training treats the ground as rigid: your foot hits it, it pushes back, done. Soft terrain (gym mats, foam, a trampoline, a sagging platform) breaks that in two nasty ways:

1. **The ground remembers.** It deflects, stores energy and rebounds, and that rebound is still happening when your next foot lands.
2. **Contacts are coupled.** Loading your left foot changes the support under your right foot, because they're on the same membrane.

Most sim-to-real pipelines handle "soft" ground by making the contact itself compliant (a springy foot-ground interaction). The authors show that's not enough: it models a squishy point, not a surface that moves as a whole.

Their framework, **RIPPLE**, adds **ElasticMembrane**: a pretensioned mass-spring mesh, basically a grid of point masses joined by springs, stretched like a drum skin. It captures coupled deflection, energy storage and rebound, and it's built into **MJLab**, so it trains GPU-parallel like any other terrain.

Training is **staged**: first learn to stand and move on rigid ground, then transfer those skills to elastic terrain. The policy sees **proprioception only**: no cameras, no force sensors. It deploys **zero-shot on a MagicBot Z1**, which balances, jumps repeatedly, and steps from a rigid platform onto a trampoline.

The numbers that matter:

- In simulation, membrane-trained policies stand successfully at least **98.7%** of the time (mean over three seeds) under 0.3 m/s pushes, across three terrains.
- Policies trained on rigid ground, or with compliant contact only, **transfer poorly to two of those three terrains**.
- One fixed policy tolerates stiffness from **0.35 to 2.8 times** the training value, and beds up to 7 m wide. So no per-surface calibration.

**What to take for your club.** You're not going to put your robot on a trampoline for its first steps. But three lessons carry over directly:

- **Your simulator's model of the world sets the ceiling.** When a policy fails on hardware, the reflex is to add more domain randomisation. Sometimes the real fix is that a whole physical effect is *missing* from sim, and no amount of randomising the parameters you already have will add it. Worth asking about your own setup: is the lab floor rigid? Is there a foam mat for fall tests? Does the robot have compliant feet or backlash that sim ignores?
- **Staged training works.** Rigid-ground skills first, then harder physics. That's the same curriculum logic you'll use going from "stand" to "walk" to "walk on rough terrain".
- **Proprioception alone carries a lot.** The policy figures out what the ground is doing purely from how its joints feel. That's the reason a history of joint states (or a learned latent estimated from it) in your observation is so powerful: it's how the robot "feels" the floor without a single extra sensor.

## Robot learning lesson: recovery data helps, then it hurts

**[FailPoint-X: A Policy-Matched, Failure-Anchored Benchmark for Offline VLA Post-Training from Human Takeovers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33769)** (Zirui Li, Yujie Yan, ... Chen Lv), submitted 25 November. [Code](https://github.com/yujieyan81/failpoint-x).

Yesterday's seed was "failure data is underpriced". This paper is that idea, measured. The setup: take a fixed π0.5 checkpoint, let it fail naturally on 10 LIBERO-X task families (629 failures), and have humans take over at the moment things go wrong. That gives **2,400 accepted recoveries**. Then post-train on those recoveries with different methods and evaluate on 384 held-out failure moments.

What they found:

- **Plain behaviour cloning of the recoveries** improves recovery success by **29.4 percentage points** over a control trained only on more normal demos.
- A fancier **value-based offline RL** learner adds just **1.5 points** on top. Simple won.
- The sting: when you start from *normal* situations instead of failures, the recovery-cloned policy is **30 points worse** than the base policy. Mixing normal demos back in cuts that loss to 16.5 points.

Why this happens: recovery data is all about weird states. Train only on that and the policy starts treating every situation like an emergency. It's catastrophic forgetting with a robotics twist.

The general lesson is one you'll meet again when you fine-tune your walker for tasks later: **always evaluate on the old distribution after training on the new one**. A policy that recovers from stumbles beautifully but no longer walks straight is not progress. Keep a fixed regression test of plain walking and run it after every fine-tune.

## Seed for your brain: robots that improve their own training

Two things this week that rhyme.

**[RoboARC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33390)** (Wang et al.) is a puzzle benchmark for robots. The best coding agent solves **86%** of tasks when it gets perfect state from the simulator, but only **43%** with real sensors. **Four zero-shot VLA policies solve none** of 18 one-arm puzzles. The gap between "knows the answer" and "can actually do it with noisy perception" is still huge, and that gap is where a lot of robotics value hides.

And the RAI Institute in Zurich has an open master's thesis on **Physical Recursive Self Improvement**: can an AI system take over parts of a robot's training pipeline? It would run many cheap sim-to-sim transfers and occasional sim-to-real ones, turn the lessons from sparse real trials into general training rules, and validate them in simulation. (Details in the internship tracker below.)

The seed: think about how much of your club's training loop is a human looking at a rollout video, saying "it's shuffling its feet", tweaking a reward weight and retraining. That loop is slow, it's undocumented, and it's exactly the kind of thing an LLM agent with access to logs, videos and config files could start to automate. A tool that watches training runs, diagnoses common locomotion pathologies (foot shuffling, dragging toes, jittery joints, exploiting a sim bug) and proposes reward or randomisation changes, with every suggestion logged and testable, is a real product. Every robot learning team does this manually today.

## Quick hits from the arXiv batch

- **[DexEngine](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33579)** (Yu et al.). Turns human hand-object videos into physically executable robot data: 65k trajectories, 116 hours. Policies trained on it beat those trained without the physics correction step. The pattern to notice: raw human data is cheap, but it's the *physics cleanup* that makes it usable.
- **[WISER-RL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34043)** (Wong et al.). Wake-sleep RL from a **single demonstration**, reporting 100% success on each of six LIBERO tasks and two real-robot tasks. Extraordinary claims, so read the evaluation details before believing it, but the one-demo framing is interesting.
- **[μMPC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34597)** (Nath, Chou). MPC on a Koopman-linearised model, with solve times as low as 23 microseconds and quadruped control in under 5 ms. Useful context: "classical control is too slow" is less and less true.
- **[HoloBrain-0.5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34449)** (Lin et al.). A cross-embodiment VLA that reports 70.2% mean task progress on Unitree G1 tasks after post-training, versus 11.0% for π0.5. A big gap, so worth watching whether others reproduce it.

## Industry: Volvo teaches humanoids by showing them

**Volvo Cars** has started training **Hexagon's AEON** humanoids at its Ghent plant in Belgium, its first factory to test humanoids. Staff teach the robot by **demonstration**: right now, placing parts into two fixtures with high precision. Next comes more parts, moving around the plant, and working next to existing machines. A larger pilot arrives in the first half of 2027, and production work is planned from mid-2027. ([The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/volvo-humanoid-robots-hexagon-aeon-ghent), [Automotive News Europe](https://www.autonews.com/volvo/ane-volvo-humanoid-robots-ghent-2027-1126/))

Two details worth noting. AEON is **wheeled**, not legged: factories are flat, so legs aren't worth the complexity yet. And its **10 kg payload** limits it to lighter jobs. The industrial humanoid that ships first is not the one that walks best, it's the one that fits the existing line. BMW is already piloting AEON in Leipzig.

Meanwhile in China, **Xpeng** showed its new **Iron** humanoid on 25 November. Mass production starts in **January**, store-assistant training in its car showrooms in Q1, and sales from Q2 2027. Co-president Brian Gu says homes are "maybe another one to two years" after commercial deployments, and that robotics is "much easier to be profitable" than cars. Notably, it runs on Xpeng's own driver-assist chips, so US export limits on Nvidia hardware matter less to them. ([CNBC](https://cnbc.com/2026/11/26/china-ev-xpeng-humanoid-robots-household-chores.html))

## Outside the bubble: a quiet extinction in the deep sea

The deep ocean is huge, so its animals should be safe, right? A study in *Nature Communications* ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261125045249.htm)) says maybe not.

A team led by Pedro Peres at Florida International University analysed **12 years of DNA** (2011 to 2023) from **486 specimens** of five species living hundreds of metres down in the Gulf of Mexico: three lanternfish and two deep-sea shrimp. **Every single species** showed genetic traits linked to higher extinction risk. One lanternfish, *Diaphus dumerilii*, lost about **8% of its genetic diversity in just 12 years**. Climate models suggest four of the five could lose up to 70% of their suitable habitat by the end of the century.

The subtle part is what "small population" means here. These animals aren't necessarily rare. Lanternfish are among the most numerous vertebrates on Earth. The issue is the **effective population size**: how many individuals actually pass on their genes. You can have billions of fish and still a shallow gene pool, which means less raw material to adapt when conditions change. "This challenges the idea that deep-sea species are naturally more resilient because they occupy such vast environments," says Peres.

Caveats: five species, one region, and the habitat losses are model projections, not observations. The study also found no direct link to the 2010 Deepwater Horizon spill.

Why it's worth your two minutes: "big number, therefore robust" is a reasoning trap that shows up everywhere. A dataset with a million samples can still have very little diversity. A policy trained on 4,096 parallel environments can still have seen only one kind of floor. Today's main paper, in a way, is about exactly that.
