# What to train on, and in what order

*2026-10-09*

A walking policy is shaped as much by the order in which it sees the world as by its rewards. Today's lesson is on curricula: how terrain and velocity commands are scheduled so PPO learns at all, and how to know whether the schedule is working. Plus a blind humanoid paper that walks rough terrain with a recurrent belief and a motion prior, a method for choosing system identification trajectories that was tested on a real K1 humanoid, a 60 million dollar bet on human motion data, and a fossil that pushes our own genus back 200,000 years.

## Robot learning lesson: terrain and command curricula

**The problem a curriculum solves.** At the start of training the policy is random noise. Put it on stairs with a 1.5 m/s command and almost every episode ends in a fall within a second, every reward is roughly the same, and the gradient carries no information. Put it on flat ground with tiny commands forever and it never learns to recover from a stumble. A curriculum keeps each robot in the band where it succeeds about half the time, which is where the learning signal is strongest.

**Terrain curriculum (the legged_gym recipe).** [Rudin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.11978) arrange the terrain as a grid: columns are terrain types (flat, rough, slopes, stairs up, stairs down, stepping stones), rows are difficulty levels. Each of the thousands of parallel robots is assigned a tile. At reset:

- If the robot walked more than half the tile length, it moves up one level.
- If it covered less than half of the distance its command asked for, it moves down one level.
- Robots that solve the hardest level are sent to a random level, so the population does not forget the easy terrain.

Nobody hand-tunes a schedule; the population sorts itself. Isaac Lab ships the same logic as the `terrain_levels_vel` curriculum term in its locomotion tasks, so for your robot this is mostly configuration, not code.

**Command curriculum.** The same idea applied to the velocity command. Start with a small range, say plus or minus 0.3 m/s forward, and widen it once the tracking reward for the current range passes a threshold, for example 80 percent of its maximum. [Margolis et al., Rapid Locomotion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.02824) go further with a grid adaptive curriculum: the command space (forward speed, yaw rate) is split into bins, and the neighbors of a bin are only unlocked once the policy tracks that bin well. This avoids a classic failure where the policy learns fast straight-line walking but never learns to turn, because the turning bins were sampled too early and always failed.

**What else gets scheduled.** Three more knobs are commonly ramped over training:

- **Penalty weights.** Start smoothness, torque and action-rate penalties near zero and increase them, so the policy first learns to walk and then learns to walk gently. Introduced too early, they teach the policy that standing still is optimal.
- **Randomization ranges.** Friction, mass, motor strength and push forces start narrow and widen. The 2 October posts covered this from the sim-to-real side.
- **Episode termination.** Some teams start with strict termination (any torso contact ends the episode) and later relax it so the policy can learn to stumble and recover.

**How to know it is working.** Log three things per iteration: the mean terrain level, the current command range, and the success rate per terrain column. A healthy run shows the mean level climbing steadily and then plateauing at a level you consider good enough. Warning signs: the level climbs to the top in a few hundred iterations (terrain is too easy, add harder tiles), the level stalls near zero (rewards or termination are broken, not the curriculum), or one column stays empty while the others climb (often stairs down, which needs foot clearance rewards).

**Practical recommendation for the first gait.** Flat ground plus mild rough terrain, two columns only, a command curriculum on forward speed, and penalties ramped up over the first third of training. Add slopes and stairs only after the flat gait has survived a sim-to-sim check in MuJoCo. A custom robot with unknown actuator limits does not need to climb stairs in simulation before it has stood on the real floor.

## New papers worth reading

### [DAMP: Humanoid Locomotion via Denoised Belief Learning and Adversarial Motion Priors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11505)
Submitted 8 October. A blind humanoid policy for rough terrain: a recurrent network builds an internal belief about the hidden state (terrain, contacts, the robot's own velocity) from proprioceptive history, and that belief is trained to be denoised and aligned with the task, while an adversarial motion prior keeps the gait human-like. Transferred from simulation to real hardware, with a [demo video](https://youtu.be/AkI7TZB2DDM). Why it matters for you: it combines two pieces you will need anyway, a memory-based estimator because your robot has no camera-based terrain map, and a style prior so the gait looks like walking rather than a shuffle. No code yet, but the method section is a good map of where these two ideas plug into PPO.

### [Informationally Decoupled Trajectory Design for Sim-to-Real System Identification](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10905)
Submitted 7 October. When you identify simulator parameters (masses, friction, motor gains) by matching recorded trajectories, a poorly chosen trajectory lets several parameter combinations explain the data equally well, and the estimate is meaningless. IDTD designs excitation trajectories from the Fisher information so that each segment exposes a different subset of parameters. It cuts identification error by about 40 percent over the best prior exploration baseline on simulated Go2, G1 and a quadrotor, and was validated on a real Booster K1 humanoid. Practical takeaway even without the method: before recording bench data for system identification, ask which parameters each motion actually excites. A slow sinusoid on one joint tells you about friction, not inertia.

### [Teaching a Robot Dog New Tricks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10601)
Submitted 6 October. Eight RL teachers, each trained on one narrow skill, are distilled into one student that performs 22 tasks on a Unitree B1, with RL and imitation mixed during distillation. The interesting piece is adversarial task selection: during distillation the task the student does worst on is sampled most often. This is the same idea as today's curriculum lesson, applied to tasks instead of terrain, and it is a plausible route for the club's later stage when walking, turning, standing up and simple tasks have to live in one policy. [Video](https://youtu.be/V9yX04EBcFA).

## Founder radar

### [Mecka AI raises 60 million dollars to record how humans move](https://americanbazaaronline.com/2026/10/08/mecka-ai-raises-60-million-to-teach-humanoid-robots-how-humans-move-489658/)
Announced 8 October. Sequoia led the Series B, with Nvidia and Microsoft's M12 participating. Mecka pays people to record themselves doing everyday tasks, making coffee or repairing cars, using smartphones and wearable body sensors, and sells the resulting motion data to robotics companies. Founded in 2024; an earlier report put the round near a 500 million dollar valuation.

This is the third human-data item in a week, after HumanVerse-500 and Workhorse. The pattern is clear: teleoperation data does not scale, so the market is shifting to capturing humans directly and solving the retargeting problem afterwards. The business insight is that Mecka sells the raw material while everyone else fights over models.

**Idea seed.** Data collection companies sell volume. The bottleneck one step later is retargeting quality: mapping a human recording onto a specific robot whose limb lengths, joint limits and torque limits differ, without producing foot sliding or physically impossible motions. A tool that takes any human motion clip and a robot description file, and returns a retargeted clip plus a check that the robot can actually track it in simulation (torque within limits, feet not sliding, balance kept), would be useful to every team that buys such data, including the club once it moves from walking to tasks.

## Outside your bubble

### [Homo erectus in southern Africa 200,000 years earlier than thought](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261007042113.htm)
Reported 8 October. A team led by Jesse Martin at La Trobe University described 18 previously unpublished hominin fossils from Drimolen in South Africa's Cradle of Humankind, excavated years ago but never formally studied. Among them is an adult Homo erectus skull fragment dated to about 2 million years ago, which places our genus's likely direct ancestor in southern Africa around 200,000 years earlier than expected. The paper is in Annals of Human Biology.

The more striking detail is the ratio. At Drimolen, Paranthropus robustus outnumbered Homo erectus by more than ten to one. Paranthropus bet on huge molars and powerful jaws for tough plant food, with a chimpanzee-sized brain; erectus bet on a bigger brain, smaller teeth and probably more meat. At that moment the specialist looked like the winner, and our lineage was the long-odds survivor. Paranthropus went extinct; erectus spread across the world. Two lessons travel well. A generalist strategy can look weak while conditions are stable and win when they change, which is the bet behind general-purpose humanoids over specialized machines. And a lot of discovery is sitting in drawers: these fossils were dug up long ago and nobody had written them up, much like the fall logs and bench data every lab collects and never analyzes.
