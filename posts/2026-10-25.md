## Robot learning lesson: teleoperation is a latency budget

**Why teleop matters for you.** Walking can be learned from a reward file. Tasks mostly cannot, at least not yet. The standard pipeline for stage two is: a human performs the task through the robot, you record what the robot saw and did, and you train a policy on that (or use it to seed RL). The quality of that data depends on one thing more than any other: whether the operator feels like they are *in* the robot or like they are steering it through a laggy video call. Lag makes the operator slow and hesitant, and slow, hesitant demos teach slow, hesitant policies.

**Where the time goes.** A typical whole-body teleop stack has three expensive stages between your wrist and the robot's:

- *Retargeting by optimization.* Solve an inverse kinematics problem every frame to turn human poses into robot joint angles. Accurate, but it costs compute and time.
- *Dense references.* Many tracking policies want full joint positions, every body-part transform, and often a window of *future* frames. Future frames sound harmless until you notice that in live teleop the future has not happened yet, so you have to buffer, which is just delay with a nicer name.
- *The tracking policy itself*, which turns the reference into motor targets.

**What [HuMiT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29903) does instead** (Cao, Xu, Chen and colleagues, Tsinghua IIIS and CUHK among others):

- *A compact reference target.* The policy sees root height, root linear and angular velocity, and the SE(3) poses of four keypoints, both wrists and both ankles, relative to the root, at the current frame only. No joint references, no other body parts, no future.
- *Closed-form scale calibration* replaces optimization-based retargeting. Human keypoints from the headset and trackers are mapped to robot body positions with a simple scaling step, so the IK solve disappears from the loop.
- *An asymmetric actor-critic.* The actor gets the compact reference plus proprioception. The critic, which only exists during training, gets privileged state and the full retargeted reference. The critic knows exactly what the motion "should" be, which makes value estimates good, while the actor learns to infer the rest of the body from four points. You saw this pattern on 18 October with teacher-student distillation; this is the cheaper version of the same idea, inside one PPO run.

**The numbers.** About 20 ms from headset streaming to policy output. A median of 65 ms from operator motion to robot motion, measured by running optical flow on 200 fps video of punching, squatting and waving. Under the same protocol, they report 120 ms for TWIST2, 145 ms for a low-latency SONIC variant and 245 ms for SONIC. On a real Unitree G1 that is fast enough to catch a tossed ball, which is a nice test because a ball does not wait for you. Code is up at [xiaohu-art/Track](https://github.com/xiaohu-art/Track).

**The honest gap.** Kneeling fails, and the authors blame it on too few training trajectories with ground contact. That is the general price of a sparse interface: whatever the four points do not specify, the policy has to fill in from its training distribution. Ask for something outside it and it guesses badly.

**Three things to steal.**

- *Draw the latency budget before you build.* Write down every stage from human to motor, with a number in milliseconds next to each, before choosing components. You will find that the network is rarely the slow part; buffering and solving are.
- *Ask what the policy really needs at runtime.* Yesterday's paper found that contacts alone carry most of what a motion clip says. Today's finds that four keypoints and a root carry most of what a teleop stream says. Same lesson, twice in two days: interfaces can usually be much smaller than you think, and smaller interfaces are faster and easier to retarget.
- *Hide the richness in the critic.* If you have information at training time that you cannot have at deployment, give it to the critic, not the actor. It is almost free and it often helps a lot.

## New papers worth reading

### [Jumping on the Moon with one policy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29586)
From RSL again (Müller, Fischer, Trentini, Tanaka, Hutter). In one-sixth gravity, walking is awkward and jumping becomes a real way to cross gaps and obstacles. They train one end-to-end policy for commanded point-to-point jumps under lunar gravity, with a multistage RL approach and only sensors a real mission would carry. In simulation it lands within less than one body length of the target. Two details worth your time: they analyze the torque and power a lunar jump demands, which is exactly the kind of sanity check you should run before asking your own actuators to do something athletic; and they stress-test with control freezes of up to one second mid-flight. On hardware, a vertical jump under lunar-gravity offloading matched the simulated torque and power. The habit to copy: check that your sim's torque and power numbers match the real robot, not just that the motion looks right.

### [Progress Probes: is the VLA falling behind?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31613)
Bhardwaj, Duan, Seo, Dan, Ma, Bajcsy, Culbertson. Instead of asking a big external VLM to watch the robot and judge whether things are going well, they train *linear probes* on the internal activations of π0.5 to predict task progress, using only successful demonstrations. If progress falls behind what is expected for this point in time, it flags a failure. It matches or beats VLM-based monitors and beats methods that need failure data. The seed: a big model's activations usually already encode what you want to know, and a linear layer is the cheapest possible way to read it out. That works for walking policies too; a probe on your locomotion policy's hidden state that predicts "about to fall" is a weekend project.

### [Retargeting that knows which moments matter](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30540)
Shi, Du, Yu and colleagues. Retargeting usually spends the same solver effort on every frame. For object interaction that is wasteful: the moment the hand closes on the box matters far more than the walk towards it. Their STR uses a vision-language model to choose which body parts and signals matter, finds keyframes where those signals change, and gives those frames more solver iterations and those body parts more weight. A mechanics-aware step then refines contact points on the object surface. Tested on a G1 in sim and a Unitree R1 in the real world. Pairs well with the lesson above: HuMiT throws retargeting away for speed, STR spends it where it counts for offline data. Your team will probably need both.

## Founder radar

### [RoboParty ships the files for its open humanoid](https://techtimes.com/articles/329432/20261023/roboparty-rp1-humanoid-releases-full-pcb-files-chinese-data-laws-cannot-waived.htm)
On 6 October this blog mentioned RP1, the 1.35 m, 39 kg open-source biped from Shanghai startup RoboParty. Now the files are out: SolidWorks CAD, wiring, bills of materials, and Gerber files for 14 custom PCBs (CERN-OHL-W-2.0), a Linux board support package, USB-to-CAN-FD bridge firmware, a ROS 2 deployment framework, URDF and MJCF models, and an Isaac Lab training workspace built on RSL-RL. The [GitHub overview](https://github.com/Roboparty/RP1) also lists demo projects including AMP, BeyondMimic, a parkour stack and a behavior foundation model, several marked "RP1 support coming soon". The company says it raised a Pre-A of about 500 million yuan led by CATL, which has not been independently confirmed.

Read the fine print, because that is where the business model is:

- *The actuator firmware is not open.* The control loops inside the ROMOMO joint modules stay closed. Everything else is a reference design; the motors are the product.
- *The motion-learning framework UFO is CC BY-NC*, so no commercial use.
- *The data question.* The article argues that under Chinese law the company could not refuse a government request for data, and that the US FCC voted in July to ban commercial imports of Chinese humanoids. Treat that as one outlet's reading, but if anyone ever proposes buying one for the club, it is a conversation to have up front.

Seeds:

- *Open everything except the part that is hard to copy.* This is the 3D-printer and drone playbook. For your own ideas, ask which single component you would keep closed, and whether that is where the value actually is.
- *A free reference design.* Even if you never touch an RP1, its PCB layouts, CAN-FD bridge and training configs are a real, shipped answer to questions your club's custom robot is answering from scratch. Reading another team's URDF and actuator settings is a cheap way to catch your own mistakes.

## Outside your bubble

### [How would you even notice mirror life?](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261023023726.htm)
Every molecule of life on Earth has a handedness. Our proteins are built from left-handed amino acids, our DNA from right-handed sugars, and the whole machinery depends on it, the way a right hand only fits a right glove. "Mirror life" would be an organism built entirely from the mirror images: same atoms, same bonds, opposite handedness. It does not exist. But synthetic biology has been building mirror-image proteins and mirror DNA pieces for years, and in 2024 a large group of scientists, including some who had worked towards it, published a technical report arguing that mirror bacteria should never be made.

The reason is a very clean piece of logic. Immune systems, viruses that kill bacteria, and the microbes that compete with them all recognize their targets by shape. A mirror bacterium might be invisible to all of them. It would not need to be aggressive to be dangerous; it would only need to be unrecognizable. Nobody can say how likely that is, and the point of the report was that nobody wants to find out.

This week, Lewis MacKenzie (Strathclyde) and Robert Pal (Durham) proposed in Royal Society Open Science a contingency plan: a way to *detect* mirror life if it ever appeared. Their tool is circularly polarized luminescence, light that twists left or right, the same principle as 3D cinema glasses. Fluorescent probes that bind to particular cell structures would give off light with a twist that depends on the handedness of what they bind to. Several probes together would act as a fingerprint for "ordinary" versus "mirror". The method has not been demonstrated end to end yet, but it can be developed using ordinary cells only, so nobody has to build the thing it is meant to detect.

Two reasons this is worth your evening. First, it is a live example of a field deciding *in advance* not to build something, before a single organism exists. That is rare, and AI and robotics people talk about it constantly without many real cases to point at. Second, the ETH talk on Wednesday (above) is exactly on this, and a room of biosecurity people is a very different crowd from the robotics meetups. Good for the bubble.
