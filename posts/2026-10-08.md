# When the robot falls: learning to get up

*2026-10-08*

The walking pipeline from the last week assumes the robot stays upright. It will not. The first hardware sessions will end on the floor many times, and every fall costs a person lifting the robot back onto the gantry. Today's lesson is about the policy almost every humanoid team adds right after walking: getting up. Plus a Berkeley paper that teaches a G1 loco-manipulation from human recordings alone, an NVIDIA learned simulator that trains an ANYmal gait in 34 seconds, a humanoid startup founded by ex-Optimus and ex-Boston Dynamics engineers, and a chemistry Nobel that continues yesterday's theme of mirror symmetry.

## Robot learning lesson: falling and getting up

**Why it is harder than walking.** Walking touches the ground with two feet in a repeating pattern. Getting up uses the whole body: knees, elbows, hands and back all make and break contact, the robot passes through poses no gait ever visits, and the hips and knees need close to peak torque for a short burst. Three consequences for training: self-collision and full-body collision meshes must be on (and reasonably accurate, not just foot spheres), the actuator torque and velocity limits from the 6 October post matter much more, and there is no single "right" motion to reward, because the best sequence depends on whether the robot lies on its back, its front, or its side.

**Approach 1: learn it from scratch with a curriculum (HoST).** [Huang et al., RSS 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08378) train a standing-up policy for the Unitree G1 with PPO and no motion data. The tricks that make it work:

- **A helping hand that fades.** Early in training a vertical force pulls the robot's base upward, so the policy discovers what standing even looks like. The force decreases as the policy improves, a curriculum on the task itself rather than on terrain.
- **Multiple critics.** Reward terms are split into groups (task, style, regularization), each with its own value head, instead of one critic for a weighted sum. This keeps a large standing-up bonus from drowning out the small terms that make the motion safe.
- **Bounded speed and smoothness.** Actions are scaled down so joints cannot move violently, and a smoothness penalty removes oscillation. Without these, the policy learns to fling itself up, which works in simulation and breaks gearboxes in reality.
- **Diverse starting poses and terrains.** Training starts from lying, sitting, and leaning against walls or slopes, so the policy handles whatever pose a real fall leaves it in.

Code is open at [OpenRobotLab/HoST](https://github.com/OpenRobotLab/HoST).

**Approach 2: discover, then refine (HumanUP).** [He et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12152) split the problem in two stages. Stage one finds any getting-up motion with relaxed constraints: generous torque limits, no smoothness penalties, flat ground. Stage two trains a deployable policy that tracks the stage-one motion as a reference, but now with realistic torque limits, smoothness terms, randomized terrain and varied initial poses. The insight transfers well beyond getting up: when a task is hard to explore and hard to make safe, do not ask one RL run to do both.

**The parts nobody writes papers about.**

- **Fall detection and switching.** A simple state machine: if projected gravity says the torso is tilted past a threshold, or base height drops below a limit, stop the walking policy, go limp or into a protective pose, wait until motion settles, then hand over to the get-up policy. When it reports standing, hand back to walking. The handover poses must be in each policy's training distribution, so train walking with episodes that start from the get-up policy's final stance.
- **Where the initial poses come from.** A cheap trick: run the walking policy in simulation with large pushes, record the joint configuration whenever it falls and settles, and use these as the reset states for get-up training. They match the falls your robot will actually have.
- **Falling well.** Before getting up comes landing. A protective posture (knees bent, arms forward, head away from the ground) reduces impact on expensive parts. It can be a fixed pose triggered by the fall detector at first; learning it is a later refinement.
- **Hardware first.** Check that your actuators can produce the torque the simulated get-up uses, with margin. Log simulated joint torques during get-up and compare them against measured peak torques from the bench. Add crash guards to the robot's protruding parts.

**Practical recommendation.** Do not train a get-up policy before walking works. But start two cheap things now: save fall states from walking training runs, and make sure your simulation has whole-body collision geometry that matches the real robot. When the gait is stable, start with HoST's open code and swap in your robot's model.

## New papers worth reading

### [Workhorse: Learning Robust Whole-Body Humanoid Loco-Manipulation from Human Data](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09117)
Hu, Liao, Chi, Zakka, Shao, Abbeel and Sreenath (Berkeley), submitted 6 October. Two policies, both trained only on recordings of humans, with no retargeting step: a visual planner that looks at egocentric RGB and predicts targets for five body points (torso, both wrists, both feet), and an RL tracker that drives the robot to those targets. The clever part is that each policy is trained on the mistakes the other makes during deployment, so the pair stays stable when chained. On a real G1 it sorts boxes with hands and a kick, catches a thrown box, topples and climbs onto a suitcase, and recovers when someone pushes it or takes the box away. Worth reading for the task phase: the planner and tracker split is a clean architecture for going from "walks" to "does things", and five body points is a much smaller interface than full joint trajectories. [Project page](https://hsb0508.github.io/workhorse/).

### [FlashNeRD: Performance-First Contact-Rich Neural Robot Dynamics](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09130)
Ataei, Heiden, Macklin, Narang, State and others (NVIDIA), submitted 6 October. A learned dynamics model that replaces the physics solver for the robot itself, keeping analytical collision detection and analytical simulation of the objects it touches. Contact can happen anywhere on the body, not only at predefined points. The headline number: PPO trains an ANYmal locomotion policy in 34 seconds, 3.8 times faster than with the analytical simulator. Why it matters for you: a learned simulator can be fine-tuned on real robot logs, which is a different route to closing the sim-to-real gap than the actuator networks from 6 October. Still early, but this is the direction simulators are heading.

## Founder radar

### [Minerva Humanoids comes out of stealth with 10 million dollars](https://thenextweb.com/news/minerva-humanoids-10m-pre-seed-general-catalyst)
Announced 6 October. General Catalyst led the pre-seed, with Long Journey Ventures and Credo Ventures co-leading and Hugging Face among the backers. CEO Sandor Felber worked on Tesla Optimus and helped start MIT CSAIL's humanoid research; CTO Maurice Rahme spent six years at Boston Dynamics as a technical lead on Stretch; the team also includes a British special forces veteran running hazardous operations. Their robot, Roger, is semi-autonomous: an operator in a VR headset does the fine manipulation, while onboard learned control handles balance, navigation and, notably, fall recovery. Targets: offshore rigs, high-voltage sites, bomb disposal, hazmat. Paid pilots start this autumn.

Two things to notice. First, the market choice: in jobs where sending a human costs a life, a teleoperated humanoid is already worth paying for, so they do not need full autonomy to sell. Second, the stack split: humans for manipulation, learning for the body. That is exactly the low-level skill set you are building in the club, and it is the part they chose to own.

**Idea seed.** Fall data is valuable and nobody shares it. Every humanoid lab logs falls on hardware (IMU, joint states, torques, the pose it ended in) and throws the logs away after debugging. An open "fall bank", a standard format plus a small dataset of real falls across robots, would let teams build fall detectors, validate simulated contact against real impacts, and seed get-up training with realistic poses. The club's first walking sessions will generate exactly this data; logging it carefully from day one costs almost nothing.

## Outside your bubble

### [Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2026: Henri Kagan and Kenso Soai](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/2026/press-release/)
Announced yesterday, for non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis. Yesterday's post was about making a robot's left and right halves behave as mirror images. Chemistry has the opposite problem. Many molecules come in two mirror-image forms, like left and right hands, and life uses only one: your proteins are built from left-handed amino acids, your sugars are right-handed. Physics treats both forms almost identically, so where did the bias come from?

Kagan showed that a catalyst that is only partly one-handed can produce a product that is more one-handed than the catalyst itself, a non-linear effect. Soai found a reaction whose product catalyzes its own formation, with the same handedness. Start it with a tiny excess of one form and the excess feeds on itself until the result is almost pure. Together they give a plausible mechanism for how a random small asymmetry on the early Earth could have been amplified into the one-handed chemistry of all life, and they matter in drug manufacturing, where the wrong mirror image can be useless or harmful. The idea worth keeping: small biases plus self-reinforcing feedback produce extreme outcomes, which is also how a policy settles into a lopsided gait, and how one standard wins a market. Read [C&EN's explainer](https://cen.acs.org/people/nobel-prize/chemistry-nobel-chirality-kagan-soai/104/web/2026/10) or [NPR's coverage](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/07/g-s1-146849/nobel-prize-chemistry).
