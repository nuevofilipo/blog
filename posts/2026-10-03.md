## Robot learning

### [DITTO-X: teleoperation where the robot can also move your fingers](https://tml.stanford.edu/ditto-x/)
Zhanpeng He, Matei Ciocarlie, C. Karen Liu, Jiajun Wu and others; [arXiv 2610.00781](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00781), posted 30 September.

A hand teleoperation interface with force feedback and touch sensing that works with several commercial dexterous hands, with no changes to the hands. The new part is reverse teleoperation: before handing control to the human, the robot drives the operator's fingers into the robot's current pose, so the takeover starts from a matching state. This matters when a human steps in during a policy rollout to correct it (a "human intervention" in DAgger-style data collection), because a mismatch between the human's hand and the robot's hand otherwise produces a jump. They report better demonstration quality and throughput than commercial hand-tracking gloves. Why it matters for the club: after walking comes manipulation, and the quality of your teleop setup will set the ceiling on your task data. Even if you never build force feedback, the idea of syncing operator and robot state before handover is worth copying.

### [ECoMEM: give a VLA an explicit memory instead of hoping it learns one](https://ecomem.github.io/)
Yize Liu, Mac Schwager, Jiajun Wu and others; [arXiv 2610.00801](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00801), posted 30 September.

Many real tasks need memory: which drawer did I already open, where did the object go when it left the camera view. Most VLAs see only the current frame or a short window. ECoMEM splits memory out of the policy: a "writer" keeps a small library of named, grounded concepts (for example "cup is in the left drawer") and updates it from evidence, and a learned "reader" turns those records into tokens the VLA conditions on. It wins 15 of 16 tasks on the RoboMME memory benchmark, and on a real robot gets 86.1% success against 8.6% for the same VLA without memory. The library transferred to new tasks with at most one new concept. Takeaway: when a learned policy fails because of missing state, adding a small explicit state can beat a bigger model.

## Learn

**Observation design: what a walking policy should see**

The earlier lessons covered actions, rewards and domain randomization. The observation is the fourth piece. The rule of thumb: give the actor only what the real robot can measure reliably at control rate, and nothing it can only know in simulation.

A typical humanoid walking observation (legged_gym, Humanoid-Gym, Isaac Lab), at a policy rate of about 50 Hz:

- **Base angular velocity** from the IMU gyroscope (3 values).
- **Projected gravity**: the gravity vector rotated into the robot's base frame (3 values). It tells the policy which way is down without the yaw ambiguity of raw Euler angles. Compute it from the IMU orientation estimate.
- **Velocity command**: desired forward, sideways and turning speed (3 values).
- **Joint positions** minus the default standing pose, and **joint velocities** (2 x number of joints).
- **Previous action** (number of joints). This lets the policy smooth its own output and partly "know" what it just commanded.
- **Gait clock** (optional): sin and cos of a phase variable that cycles once per step. This makes a regular, symmetric gait much easier to learn and pairs with the foot-contact reward terms from two days ago. Drop it later if you want the policy to choose its own rhythm.

What to leave out of the actor: **base linear velocity** (the real robot does not measure it well; IMU integration drifts), **base height**, **foot contact forces** unless you have real foot sensors, and **terrain heights** unless you have a working perception stack. These quantities are still useful during training, so give them to the critic only. This is called asymmetric actor-critic: the critic, which is thrown away at deployment, sees privileged simulation state, which makes value estimates better and training faster, while the actor stays deployable. Alternatively, train a small estimator network to predict base velocity from the proprioceptive history, as in ETH RSL's ANYmal work.

**Why history helps.** A single frame does not tell the policy the floor's friction, its own mass offset or its motor lag. A short sequence of past observations and actions does, because the robot can see how its commands played out. This is why history acts as implicit system identification, and why policies trained with domain randomization usually need it. Common options: stack the last 5-15 frames (Humanoid-Gym does this), use a GRU or LSTM, or use a teacher-student setup where a student with history learns to imitate a teacher that saw the true physics parameters.

**Practical checks.**

- Scale each input to roughly unit range (joint velocities can be in the tens of rad/s; scale them down) or use running mean/std normalization, and save the exact normalization with the checkpoint.
- Add the same noise in sim that the real sensors have (see yesterday's DR list).
- Make the observation vector built on the robot bit-for-bit identical in order, units and frames to the one in sim. Most first-deploy failures are a sign flip, a wrong joint order or a quaternion convention mismatch, not an RL problem. Log one second of real observations and replay it through the sim builder to compare.

Further reading: "Learning Sim-to-Real Humanoid Locomotion in 15 Minutes" (Seo, Sferrazza, Abbeel and others, December 2025) trains G1 and T1 walking policies on one RTX 4090 with off-policy RL (FastSAC, FastTD3) and minimal rewards; its open code is a clean reference for all four lessons. [arXiv 2512.01996](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.01996), [project page and code](https://younggyo.me/fastsac-humanoid).

## Founder radar

No robotics round from the past week was worth a slot, so here are two idea seeds from today's papers instead.

- **Teleop as a product.** DITTO-X works on several commercial hands without modifying them. Every humanoid and dexterous hand company needs demonstration data, and most build their own interface. A hand-agnostic teleop and intervention rig sold or rented to those teams (hardware plus data pipeline) is a "picks and shovels" business, similar to Tangent's dexterity layer from yesterday.
- **Memory as a module.** ECoMEM shows a small, explicit memory bolted onto an existing VLA gives a large jump. A model-agnostic "state and memory layer" for deployed robot policies, which logs what the robot saw and keeps task state across hours, is the kind of infrastructure that gets more valuable as more policies ship.

## Outside your bubble

### [A 10-minute X-ray flash marks the birth of a magnetar](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/09/260930020252.htm)
On 4 July 2025 a gamma-ray burst lasted only half a second, but the Einstein Probe satellite saw X-rays from the same spot glow for nearly 10 minutes. Eleonora Troja's group at the University of Rome Tor Vergata, with Chinese and European collaborators and follow-up from ESO's Very Large Telescope, conclude that two neutron stars collided and briefly formed a magnetar, a neutron star with an extremely strong magnetic field, whose spin-down powered the long X-ray glow. Published in Science Bulletin. Why it matters: it gives astronomers a new signature for neutron star mergers and a way to count how often they produce magnetars, especially when combined with gravitational-wave detectors. The general lesson fits robotics too: a short, sharp signal can hide a long tail of information if you keep the sensor watching after the event.
