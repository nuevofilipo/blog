## Robot learning lesson: force as a command

**The blind spot of motion tracking.** The big trend in humanoid control is general motion tracking: retarget lots of human mocap onto the robot, then train one policy to follow any reference clip. It works beautifully for dancing and walking. It falls apart the moment the robot has to do something to the world. A reference clip of "lift a box" says where the hands go, but not whether the box weighs 1 kg or 20. The authors put it nicely: "The motion is reproduced, but the intended interaction is not." In their tests, a force-blind tracker couldn't lift boxes between 1.5 and 6 kg.

**What [Ctrl-F](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05507) does.** Mengjie Zhao, Koushil Sreenath, Masayoshi Tomizuka and colleagues at UC Berkeley add one thing to the tracker's input: a 3D force vector for each body that's supposed to touch something (hands, or a foot). Then they split the problem in two.

- *Low level: the "inverse-force" trick.* The tracker sees the reference motion, the force command and proprioception. No objects. During training, whenever a body is free to interact, they sample a force command and apply the *opposite* force to that body in simulation. If the policy wants to stay on its reference, it has to push back with exactly the commanded force. So it learns "produce this force" without ever seeing a box. A load mask decides which bodies may be loaded (a standing foot, for example, is excluded), and a curriculum ramps the force range up from zero.
- *High level: a planner that picks the force.* A second policy looks at the object's state and the desired contact point, and outputs force commands to the frozen tracker. It's trained with PPO plus imitation of a simple analytical teacher: a PD controller on the object's pose, plus gravity and friction compensation, split over the contacts. The teacher is used as rough guidance, and its weight decays during training. No force sensors or known masses at deployment.
- *Setup.* Simulation in mjlab, Unitree G1, object pose from AprilTags.

**Numbers.** In simulation, with the force command, task completion stays above 90% across the whole mass range. Without it, the force-blind policies collapse right past the nominal mass and hit zero at 1.5 times nominal. A neat check: feed the command with the wrong sign and performance is worst of all, so the policy really listens to direction, not just magnitude. Behavior cloning of the teacher alone mostly fails (6% vs 100% on the raised-box task), which says the RL part is doing real work.

On the real G1: dragged a cart loaded with 14 kg, pushed a 20 kg box, raised a 22 kg bin in 9 of 10 trials, kicked a 20 kg box. The public SONIC tracker managed none of these. When someone dropped weight onto the cart mid-drag, the robot kept its pace. Honest detail: commanded 10, 20 and 30 N came out as 8.8, 16.7 and 24.5 N on a scale, 12 to 18% short.

**Caveats.** Needs object pose from markers. Trained on a limited motion set, so new clips mean retraining. Very large loads still exceed what the policy can do.

**Why this matters for your walker.** The inverse-force trick doesn't need objects, so you can borrow it before your robot ever touches anything.

1. *Random pushes are a weak version of this.* Most walking setups apply random shoves as domain randomization. The policy has to survive them, but it never knows they're coming. Ctrl-F makes the external force part of the command, which turns "survive whatever happens" into "hold this force on purpose". For walking, the same idea gives you a policy that can lean into a known load, like a backpack, a harness during early tests, or someone holding its hand.
2. *Design your command interface early.* Today your walker probably takes velocity and maybe base height. Every input you add later means retraining. Write down now which quantities the task layer will eventually need to set (velocity, height, maybe hand force) and leave room for them.
3. *Measure what you command.* The 12 to 18% gap between commanded and delivered force is the kind of thing you only learn with a cheap luggage scale and an afternoon. Do the same for your velocity commands: command 0.5 m/s, measure what you get.

## New papers worth reading

### [Soccerbot: humanoid soccer with a human motion prior](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06564)
Haoyu Shao, Siyuan Huang and colleagues distill a latent motion prior from large-scale human soccer mocap, then train a hierarchical RL policy that stays inside that "human-like" space. One skill-conditioned policy handles moving, dribbling, trapping and kicking, with smooth switches between skills, and transfers zero-shot to a G1. The abstract has no numbers, so judge it by the videos. Why read it: if your team's walking gait looks like a nervous penguin, a learned motion prior is the standard fix, and this is a clear recent example of the full pipeline.

### [GRASP: retargeting without a differentiable simulator](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05754)
Yang Liu, Heng Yang and colleagues at Harvard tackle the painful part between mocap and policy: turning a human demonstration into controls that actually work under the robot's contact physics. They use sampling, but reuse old rollouts to estimate gradients that steer the next samples. 92.8% success on 1,352 bimanual demos from OakInk2, and the cleaned-up references make PPO tracking better for humanoid-object interaction. [Project page](https://computationalrobotics.seas.harvard.edu/GRASP/).

### Also new
- [Where is VLA inference time spent?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06710) Profiles seven VLA models across GPUs from an H100 down to a Jetson Thor. Main finding: optimizations interact, so stacking two can give much less than the sum. Useful if your humanoid ever runs a VLA on board.
- [RACE, arXiv 2611.05838](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05838): picks the best policy checkpoint from held-out trajectories instead of running rollouts. Choosing checkpoints for a real robot is expensive, so this is worth a look.
- [Contact-implicit trajectory optimization on ErgoCub, arXiv 2611.05585](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05585): plans humanoid motion over uneven terrain without fixing the contact sequence in advance.

## Founder radar

### Seed: force is the missing data channel
Two news items line up today. Cognex is buying RealSense for 500 million dollars because robots need eyes. Ctrl-F shows that the next thing they need is a sense of force, and that it can be commanded without a force sensor. But notice what the paper had to fake: an analytical teacher with privileged object mass, and a luggage scale to check the result.

Questions worth an evening:
- Human mocap tells you how people move, not how hard they push. Who records demonstrations with force in them? A glove or handle with a cheap load cell, synced to video, could turn every human demo into a force-labeled one.
- A "force calibration kit" for humanoid teams: a few known loads, a scale, a script that sweeps commands and fits the gap. Boring, and every lab doing loco-manipulation would want it.
- RealSense going to an industrial vision company may change pricing, support and the open-source SDK that many research robots depend on. If that happens, there's room for a hardware-neutral perception layer.

## Outside your bubble

### [A third kind of magnet is real](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261104012256.htm)
School physics gives you two kinds of magnetic order. In a ferromagnet, like a fridge magnet, the electron spins all point the same way and add up to a field you can feel. In an antiferromagnet, neighboring spins point in opposite directions and cancel, so from the outside nothing seems to happen. In 2022 theorists proposed a third class, altermagnets: spins alternate and cancel like an antiferromagnet, but the crystal around each spin is rotated relative to its neighbor, which breaks a symmetry and lets the material behave, electrically, a bit like a ferromagnet.

This week a SLAC-led team, publishing in Science, reported strong evidence that manganese telluride, long filed under "ordinary antiferromagnet", is actually an altermagnet. They shone left- and right-circularly polarized X-rays at it at the ESRF synchrotron in Grenoble and found it scatters the two differently. That "handedness" is something a normal antiferromagnet can't have. They ran it in 2024, repeated it in 2025, and got the same answer.

Why care: computer memory that stores bits in spins has a dilemma. Ferromagnets are easy to read but stray magnetic fields can flip them. Antiferromagnets are robust but almost impossible to read. Altermagnets might give both. "Altermagnets combine the best of both," says lead author Daniel Jost. The bigger lesson is about categories: a material sat in the wrong box for decades because nobody had thought of the third box. Worth asking which of your own "obvious" two-way splits (model-based vs learned, sim vs real) is hiding a third option.
