# Reading the tea leaves: what PPO's training curves are telling you

*2026-10-15*

Every robot learning project has a phase nobody puts in the demo video: three people staring at TensorBoard at 1 a.m., arguing whether the reward curve going flat means "converged" or "dead". Your club is about to enter that phase, so today's lesson is a field guide to the PPO knobs and curves that matter for a walking policy. Also today: an MIT model that closes the actuator gap with six and a half minutes of walking data, a paper arguing discrete actions are underrated, someone running diffusion on the weights of humanoid policies (yes, the weights), 500 hours of human video for a humanoid foundation model, a curated frontier-AI day in Dübendorf with a physical-world track, and some bad news about your body after 35.

## Robot learning lesson: reading PPO like a dashboard

Almost every locomotion stack you will touch (legged_gym, Isaac Lab, Humanoid-Gym, the BeyondMimic tracker from yesterday) trains with PPO through [rsl_rl](https://github.com/leggedrobotics/rsl_rl), the library from ETH's own Robotic Systems Lab. Its defaults are good, which is exactly why people never learn what they do. Then something breaks, and nobody knows which knob to touch.

**The knobs, and what they actually mean.**
- *Rollout size.* Typically 4096 parallel robots times 24 steps each, so about 100k transitions per update. At a 50 Hz policy that is half a second of experience per robot per iteration. Short, but there are a lot of robots.
- *Epochs and minibatches.* Usually 5 passes over the batch in 4 minibatches. More epochs squeezes more out of the data but drifts further from the policy that collected it.
- *Clip range (0.2) and the adaptive learning rate.* rsl_rl's default schedule watches the KL divergence between the old and new policy and adjusts the learning rate to keep it near a target of about 0.01. That is why you rarely tune the learning rate by hand. It is also why a learning rate that collapses to its floor is a symptom, not a setting.
- *Discount and GAE (gamma 0.99, lambda 0.95).* Gamma 0.99 at 50 Hz means the policy cares about roughly the next two seconds. That is about four steps of walking, which is the horizon that matters for not falling.
- *Initial action noise (std around 1.0) and the entropy bonus (around 0.01).* Exploration lives here, and so do most of the silent failures.

**The six curves to watch, in this order.**
1. *Episode length.* The first sign of life. If episodes do not get longer within the first few hundred iterations, the robot is not learning to stay up, and nothing else matters yet. Check your termination conditions and reset states before touching PPO.
2. *Individual reward terms, not the total.* The total reward can rise while the robot learns to stand still and collect "upright" and "alive" bonuses. Plot velocity tracking on its own. If it stays flat while the penalties shrink, the robot has learned that the safest gait is no gait.
3. *Action noise std.* It should shrink slowly. If it collapses to near zero early, the policy has stopped exploring and will polish whatever bad habit it has. If it grows, the gradient is rewarding randomness, which often means a reward term pays for jitter.
4. *KL and the learning rate.* If the adaptive learning rate keeps falling to its minimum, every update is too large. Common causes are unnormalized observations, a giant reward term or an exploding value function.
5. *Value loss.* A value loss that spikes whenever the curriculum steps up is normal. One that climbs steadily usually means rewards that are too large in scale, or a termination bonus or penalty that makes returns jump.
6. *Terrain or command curriculum level.* The truest progress bar. Reward can go down while the policy gets better, because the curriculum is handing it harder ground.

**Failure modes you will meet, with first suspects.**
- *Standing still forever:* penalties too strong compared with velocity tracking. Scale penalties to zero for the first stretch of training, or ramp them in.
- *Vibrating at high frequency:* the action rate penalty is missing or too weak, or the PD gains in simulation are higher than on the robot.
- *Hopping on one leg, or a lopsided gait:* add the symmetry augmentation from the 7 October lesson, or check that the left and right joints in your URDF really mirror each other.
- *Good in training, falls in evaluation:* you are probably evaluating with the mean action while training with noise. That is correct, but it means a policy that relies on noise to look stable is not stable. Always watch deterministic rollouts.

**Rule of thumb:** change one thing per run, log every reward term separately, and keep a one-line note per run in a shared file. Most club time is not lost to bad ideas but to not knowing which of three changes made the robot walk.

## New papers worth reading

### [Contact-UAN: Unsupervised Actuator Networks as a Residual World Model for Humanoid Control](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18991)
From Pulkit Agrawal's lab at MIT (Fey, Nguyen, Prabhu, Margolis, Agrawal). The usual sim-to-real story is "the policy walks on hardware, ship it". This paper asks a sharper question: does the robot produce the forces it learned in simulation? Often it does not, mainly because the actuator model is wrong. They keep the simulator and learn a residual correction of the actuator dynamics so that replayed simulation matches recorded hardware logs. The clever detail: before replaying a walking log, you need the simulated stance foot to start with the same contact forces as the real one, or the model learns to fix reset artifacts instead of actuator errors. They infer the stance-foot pose from proprioception alone. Training on 6.5 minutes of real walking gives 20% lower running torque, 43% lower force-tracking error, and jumps that work. It is the natural sequel to the actuator modeling lesson: once your robot walks, log everything, because those logs are the training data for the next, better simulator. [Project page](https://contact-uan.csail.mit.edu/).

### [When Do Discrete Actions Make Good Continuous Controllers?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20429)
Yi, Sreenath, Simchowitz, Guanya Shi and colleagues compare discrete and continuous action heads head to head, single-step and flow-based, from scratch and in multi-task VLA training, in sim and on hardware. The finding goes against intuition: even naive uniform binning matches or beats continuous actions on success rate. The real weakness of discrete policies is not precision but factorization. Predicting each action dimension and timestep independently loses the correlations between them. Iterative discrete flows or classifier-free guidance fix most of that. Worth reading for the 12-page main text alone, and for the question it plants: the walking policy outputs continuous joint targets mostly out of habit.

### [Modeling and Understanding Neural Weights of Humanoid Policies with Diffusion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20606)
The weird one of the week, from Sreenath's group at Berkeley. They train lots of policies for the Unitree G1, compress the actor weights with a linear autoencoder and fit a diffusion transformer over the result. Sampling from it gives new networks that walk, dance and spin-kick on the real robot. The finding that should make you curious: the first-layer weights show a consistent structure that mirrors the robot's kinematic tree, and it appears under both RL and imitation learning. The body leaves a fingerprint in the network, whatever optimizer wrote it. Next time someone asks what a policy "knows", here is a strange but concrete answer.

### [λ0: Scaling Open-World Egocentric Human Data for Humanoid Loco-Manipulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18950)
A tech report from the Lambda-humanoid team. They collected HumanVerse, 500 hours of open-world human video paired with body and hand motion, aligned camera viewpoint and arm appearance to the robot, and mid-trained a humanoid foundation model on it. Result: zero-shot transfer to a Unitree G1 with no robot demonstrations for the target tasks, 62.5% success on real loco-manipulation tasks, and a curve where more human data keeps helping. It continues yesterday's thread: the cheapest robot data is still a human doing the thing. [Project page](https://lambdahumanoid.github.io/).

## Founder radar

### [ICON raises 200 million dollars for robotic construction](https://techstartups.com/2026/10/14/startup-funding-news-today-october-14-2026)
Austin's ICON closed a Series D for its Titan system, which 3D-prints the walls of houses. Investors include Baillie Gifford and BlackRock-managed funds. It is a useful counterpoint to the humanoid hype: ICON did not build a general robot that can do construction, it built one narrow machine that does one part of construction very well, and it sells houses, not robots. In the same list, Guangzhou's Yundie raised several hundred million yuan for hotel service robots, sold as robots-as-a-service. Both bet on a narrow job and a business model before generality.

**Idea seed.** Contact-UAN needs about six minutes of good hardware logs, and almost nobody collects them well. A small open tool for humanoid teams, "the black box recorder", would log joint targets, measured positions, currents, IMU and contact estimates at full rate on the robot, tag each run with the policy checkpoint and gains, and export straight into the format a residual actuator model or a system identification script expects. Every walking club needs it the week after the first steps, and most build a fragile version in a hurry. Build it for your robot first, open-source it, and see who asks for it.

## Outside your bubble

### [Your fitness peaks around 35, and then the long slope begins](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261014000912.htm)
Karolinska Institutet researchers (lead author Maria Westerståhl) followed 427 Swedes born in 1958 and tested them from age 16 to 63, one of the longest fitness studies of its kind. Aerobic fitness and muscular endurance peaked between 26 and 36, explosive power even earlier, and then everything declined. Slowly at first, then faster, until performance at 63 was 30 to 48% below peak. Men and women declined at the same rate. People who started training later still gained 5 to 10%, so it is never too late, but exercise slowed the slope rather than stopping it. The usual caveat applies: these are averages from an observational study, not a timer in your body.

Why it is here: you are in the decade where your physical capacity is at its peak, and also the decade where founders and PhD students trade sleep and sport for output. The study's quiet message is that what you build now, in muscle as in skills, sets the starting height of a curve you will ride for forty years. Go for a run before the TensorBoard session.
