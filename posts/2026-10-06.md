# Make the simulated motor match the real one

*2026-10-06*

Yesterday ended with a promise: actuator modeling. A walking policy that works in simulation but stumbles on hardware is most often failing because the simulated motor does not respond the way the real one does. Today covers how to measure your motors, the three ways to close the gap, and a July paper that suggests a fourth: make the real motor behave like the simulated one. Two papers from this weekend's arXiv batch round it out, plus an open-source humanoid unveiled at IROS yesterday.

## Robot learning lesson: actuator modeling

**Why it matters.** In Isaac Lab or MuJoCo, the default joint is an ideal PD controller: you set a target position, and the simulator instantly produces torque = Kp * (q_target - q) - Kd * qdot, limited to a maximum. A real joint is different. It has a motor with a torque-speed curve (less torque available at high speed), a gearbox with friction, backlash and sometimes elasticity, a driver running its own current loop, and communication delay between your policy computer and the driver. The policy learns to exploit the ideal version: fast, precise corrections that the real joint delivers late, weaker, or with oscillation.

**Step 1: measure.** Before any modeling, get data from each joint type (hip, knee, ankle actuators often differ). Fix the actuator on a test bench or hold the robot in the air, then send position targets and log at the driver rate (ideally 1 kHz or more): commanded target, measured position, measured velocity, motor current (a proxy for torque) and timestamps. Use varied signals: steps of different sizes, sine sweeps (chirps) from slow to fast, and random targets similar to what a policy produces. Do it with the PD gains you actually plan to use. If possible, also log under load, for example a pendulum arm on the joint, or the robot standing on the gantry.

**Step 2: pick a model.** Three options, in rough order of effort:

1. **Better analytic model.** Add the effects that matter: a torque-speed limit (Isaac Lab's DCMotor actuator does this), a fixed latency of a few control steps, joint friction and armature (rotor inertia reflected through the gearbox, which matters a lot for high-ratio gearboxes). Fit the parameters to your logs by minimizing the error between simulated and measured response to the same commands. This is system identification, and for quasi-direct-drive actuators it is often enough.

2. **Actuator network.** The approach from ETH's ANYmal work ([Hwangbo et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08652)): train a small MLP that takes a short history of position errors and velocities (for example the current step plus two past steps at 2.5 ms spacing) and predicts the torque the joint actually produces. In simulation, this network replaces the ideal PD controller. It captures nonlinear effects you did not model explicitly, but it needs good torque data (current is a proxy; a torque sensor or careful calibration is better) and data that covers what the policy will do. Isaac Lab ships `ActuatorNetMLP` and `ActuatorNetLSTM` classes that load such a network.

3. **Randomize around it.** Whichever model you use, randomize its parameters during training: gains, friction, latency, torque limits, within ranges that bracket your measurements. This makes the policy robust to the error that remains. Randomization without measurement is guesswork; measurement without randomization is fragile.

**Step 3: the fourth option, shape the hardware.** [Actuator Reality Shaping for Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Robot Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02205) (Yamamori et al., ATR and Kyoto, July 2026) turns the problem around. Instead of making the simulator more realistic, they put a two-degree-of-freedom controller (feedforward plus feedback) on each physical joint so that the closed-loop response matches a simple, idealized second-order system. The simulator then uses the same idealized model. The policy sees a standard actuator interface in both worlds, and they report zero-shot transfer on a servo, a 7-DOF arm, a wheeled-legged robot on a slope and a walking humanoid. For a custom-built robot like XALUN this is attractive, because you control the motor driver firmware: you can design the actuator to be easy to simulate rather than only trying to simulate a hard actuator. The cost is that shaping uses up some of the actuator's bandwidth and torque headroom.

**Practical recommendation for a first gait.** Start with option 1: measure latency and friction, add torque-speed limits and armature, fit to logs, randomize around the fitted values. Compare simulated and real step responses side by side for each joint type. Only if a clear mismatch remains (for example in the ankles, where small timing errors matter most) move to an actuator network or reality shaping for those joints.

## New papers worth reading

### [ROOT: Discovering Rewards for User-Specified Embodied Behaviors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04250)
Sadikoglu et al., submitted 3 October. Many gait properties are easy to see in a video but hard to write as a reward term: "walk more naturally", "don't shuffle", "keep the torso calm". ROOT keeps a tree of experiments, each with a reward program, a trained policy and rollout videos. A video-language model looks at the videos, describes what is wrong, and proposes a revised reward. Tested on MuJoCo benchmarks and a Unitree Go2 in simulation and on hardware, with behavior alignment rising from 3.56 to 4.14 out of 5. Relevant because tuning reward weights by watching videos is exactly what you will be doing for the first gait; this automates part of that loop. Treat the generated rewards as suggestions to read, not as code to trust blindly.

### [Continual Humanoid Motion Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04231)
He et al., submitted 3 October. Once the robot walks, the next step is tasks, and a question you will face is how to add skills without breaking walking. They use a progressive network: earlier skills are frozen, and each new skill gets small LoRA adapters that reuse the most similar previous skill (similarity measured with dynamic time warping and optimal transport on motions). Six skill categories learned in sequence, about 41% less training time, deployed on a Unitree G1. Worth bookmarking for the "after walking" phase.

## Founder radar

### [RoboParty unveils RP1, an open-source bipedal humanoid, at IROS](https://roboticsbusinessnews.com/news/14/3667/roboparty-introduces-rp1-open-source-humanoid-robot-at-iros-2026.html)
Announced 5 October. RP1 builds on RoboParty's earlier open hardware design (the RPO, whose repo passed 2,500 GitHub stars), with in-house actuator modules up to 160 Nm peak torque and a dexterous hand. They promise staged releases of the motion control stack, simulation environments, SDKs and training tools. Worth watching for two reasons: their released actuator models and simulation assets are a free reference point for your own actuator work, and an open-source business model for humanoid hardware is a real test of whether robots can follow the path of 3D printers and drones, where open designs created the ecosystem.

### [Safeworld raises 12 million dollars for robot safety simulation](https://chatai.com/posts/safeworld-raises-12-2m-to-build-safety-simulation-for-ai-powered-robots)
Out of stealth on 5 October, co-founded by Ding Zhao from CMU's Safe AI Lab, with a seed led by Shine Capital and a16z Speedrun. They build simulated test environments that check how learned robot policies react to unpredictable human behavior, starting with industrial sites like solar farms. The pattern: as learned policies replace hand-written controllers, buyers need evidence that a black-box policy is safe, and that evidence comes from large-scale testing in simulation, the same tool used to train the policy.

**Idea seed.** Every humanoid team spends weeks on the bench work described above, and most do it with ad hoc scripts. An "actuator characterization kit" (a standard test rig plus software that runs the step, chirp and load tests, fits the analytic model, trains an actuator network and exports a ready-to-use Isaac Lab or MuJoCo actuator config) would serve every lab and startup building custom robots. Start by building it properly for the club's actuators; if other teams ask for it, that is the signal.

## Outside your bubble

### [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 2026: optogenetics](https://www.statnews.com/2026/10/05/nobel-prize-medicine-2026-winner-deisseroth-hegemann-nagel/)
Announced yesterday: Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel, "for their discoveries concerning light-gated ion channels and optogenetics." Hegemann and Nagel studied how single-celled green algae sense light and found channelrhodopsin, a protein that opens an ion channel when hit by blue light. Deisseroth put the gene for it into neurons of mice, so that specific nerve cells could be switched on and off with pulses of light through an optical fiber. This turned neuroscience from correlation (this region is active during that behavior) into causation (activate these cells and the behavior happens). Two things worth noticing: a tool for brain research came out of basic research on pond algae that nobody funded with brains in mind, and the breakthrough was an actuator problem, just like today's lesson: finding a way to make a precise, fast command produce a precise, fast effect in a messy physical system.
