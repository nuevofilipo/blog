# The last centimetre: what a walking policy actually outputs

*2026-10-17*

Every robot learning diagram has an arrow from "policy" to "robot", and that arrow hides a surprising amount of engineering. A neural network outputs twelve-ish floating point numbers fifty times a second. A motor needs a current. In between sit a scale factor, a default pose, two gains per joint, a clipping rule and a timing contract, and every one of them can quietly make or break sim-to-real. Yesterday was about the reward, the number the policy chases. Today is about the other end, what the policy is allowed to say. Also today: a G1 that hugs boxes, human egocentric video that survives a change of controller, a lesson on what a VLA should predict, a 50-billion-parameter hand model out of Beijing, a new internship tracker, and an enzyme that eats both plastic and penicillin.

## Robot learning lesson: the action space is part of the policy

**The standard recipe.** Almost every RL humanoid you have seen walking uses the same interface:

- The policy outputs an action `a`, one number per joint.
- That becomes a joint position target: `q_target = q_default + action_scale * a`.
- A PD controller turns the target into torque: `tau = Kp * (q_target - q) - Kd * q_dot`.
- The policy runs slowly (typically 50 Hz). The PD loop runs fast (200 Hz in simulation through "decimation" of 4 physics steps per policy step, often 1 kHz or more in motor firmware).

It looks like position control, but it is better thought of as the policy commanding a set of virtual springs. The policy does not really care where the joint ends up. It chooses where the spring is anchored, and the physics does the rest. Once that clicks, most of the knobs make sense.

**Knob 1: the default pose.** `q_default` is the policy's zero, the pose it gets "for free" when it outputs nothing. For bipeds this is a slight crouch: hips flexed a little, knees bent, ankles compensating. A straight-legged default puts the knee at a singularity and makes the policy fight to get out of it. An asymmetric default (one hip offset by a URDF mistake) gives you an asymmetric gait that no reward term will fully fix. Check it by spawning the robot in simulation with zero actions and looking at it. It should stand, or at least sag gracefully.

**Knob 2: the action scale.** Usually 0.25 to 0.5 rad. This sets how far one unit of policy output moves the spring anchor. Too small, and the policy cannot reach the poses it needs, so you see stiff, tiny steps and saturated outputs. Too large, and early random exploration slams joints around, the robot falls on every episode, and nothing gets learned. Log the distribution of raw actions: if they constantly sit at your clip limits, the scale is too small. Some teams use a different scale per joint group, which is reasonable, but it is one more thing that must match on the real robot.

**Knob 3: the gains.** This is where taste comes in.
- *Stiff* (high Kp): the joint tracks the target closely, the policy behaves almost like a kinematic controller, and the robot feels precise. It is also less forgiving of model errors and impacts, and it amplifies any jitter in the policy output.
- *Soft* (low Kp): the joint behaves like a compliant spring, which absorbs foot impacts and hides model mismatch. The catch is that, to produce torque against gravity, the policy must command targets *beyond* where it wants the joint to be. The policy learns this offset happily in simulation, which means it is now baked into the weights.
- That last point is the big one. **The gains are part of the policy.** A policy trained with knee Kp of 100 and deployed with 80 is a different controller. The robot will squat lower, step differently and possibly fall. The gains, the action scale and the default pose belong in the same config file as the exported network, versioned together. Never let firmware defaults and training defaults drift apart.
- Ankles usually get much lower gains than hips and knees. They need to comply with the ground, and they are often driven through linkages that make high gains unstable.

**Knob 4: clipping and filtering.**
- Clip the raw actions (legged_gym clips at a generous bound), but more importantly clip `q_target` to the joint limits *with a margin*. A target past the hard stop turns the PD controller into a hammer.
- A low-pass filter on actions smooths output, but it adds latency. If you filter on the robot, filter in simulation too, or the policy will be surprised by its own sluggish legs. Most teams prefer the action-rate penalty from yesterday plus modest gains over heavy filtering.

**Why not output torques directly?** You can, and some groups have done it successfully. But Peng and van de Panne showed back in 2017 that, for learned locomotion, PD targets train faster and give more robust policies than raw torques. The PD loop acts as a cheap, high-rate stabilizer that the slow policy does not have to learn. For a first gait on a custom robot, do not fight this.

**A pre-flight checklist for your team:**
- Zero-action test in simulation: does the robot stand in the default pose?
- Same test on the real robot, hanging from the gantry: does it hold the same pose with the same gains?
- Step response: command a small target change on one joint, in simulation and on hardware. Compare rise time and overshoot. This is a 20-minute experiment that catches half of all gain mismatches.
- Export a single deploy config containing action scale, default pose, Kp, Kd, decimation, clip limits and observation scales, and load the network only through it.

Further reading: [Peng and van de Panne, Learning Locomotion Skills Using DeepRL: Does the Choice of Action Space Matter?](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.01055), and the `control` section of any config in [legged_gym](https://github.com/leggedrobotics/legged_gym) or [Humanoid-Gym](https://github.com/roboterax/humanoid-gym), where all of the knobs above live in about fifteen lines.

## New papers worth reading

### [Whole-Body Dexterity](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22184)
Humans pick up big awkward things with their whole body: a box under the arm, a basketball pinned against the chest. Robots usually try with fingertips and fail. This paper (Mc Gartoll, Fazeli, Mukadam, Nambi and colleagues) treats arms and torso as contact surfaces on a Unitree G1. The pipeline is a pattern worth stealing. First, generate a dataset of feasible whole-body grasps. Second, train RL experts that move objects from any grasp to any other grasp. Third, distill all of them into one policy that only needs proprioception and object geometry. Real-world success is 90%, against 18% for direct retargeting of human motion and 4% for a generalist whole-body controller. The long-horizon demo, where the robot tucks a box under one arm, carries it from a shelf to a table and puts it down, is exactly the kind of "task after walking" your club is heading toward. Note the recipe once more: many specialist experts first, then distillation. It is how most capable humanoid controllers are built today.

### [KODA: Kinematics-Observation Data Alignment for Humanoid Learning from Human Egocentric Demonstration](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21152)
Human video is cheap, but turning it into robot training data has an annoying failure mode: the action labels depend on the low-level controller. Swap controllers and your carefully converted dataset becomes stale. KODA stores each human demo as a controller-independent canonical record (motion, synthesized robot-view images, task instruction). It only generates action labels when you pick a specific whole-body controller, by re-optimizing that controller's commands to track the stored motion. On a G1 across four loco-manipulation tasks, adding human data improves success on conditions only seen in the human demos by about 45 points, for two different controllers (SONIC and HEFT), and the gains grow with more human data. The lesson in data engineering: store data in a form that outlives your current stack. Your team will change its walking controller many times this year.

### [What Should Robot Policies Learn to Predict? Rethinking Supervision Targets in VLA Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21915)
A clean, useful ablation paper. With a fixed training budget, is it better to spend all samples on predicting actions, or to spend some on auxiliary targets, such as latent actions (LAPA-style, DINO-based) or future visual features (DINOv3, V-JEPA 2, Wan2.2)? A router assigns each training sample to one objective. The answer: the right auxiliary supervision beats action-only training, even when actions get half the samples or fewer. Future-vision targets generally win, though the best latent-action target holds up on CALVIN. Their diagnosis is a nice mental model. Latent-action targets teach *relative motion*, while future-vision targets teach *where things end up and which task you are doing*. If you ever train a manipulation policy on a small dataset, predicting the future is a cheap regularizer.

### [Inertially Significant Humanoid Teleoperation via Whole-Body MPC and Online Parameter Estimation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22176)
From Robert Griffin's group at IHMC, a counterweight to all the RL above. When a humanoid picks up something heavy, its dynamics change, and a teleoperator in VR has no idea how to compensate. The paper runs an extended Kalman filter that estimates physically consistent inertial parameters online and feeds them into a whole-body MPC, which then produces the correct feedforward torques. The operator just moves their hands, and the controller worries about balance. It is worth knowing this world exists. The best learned controllers increasingly borrow from it: estimating payload is a natural extra input for a learned policy, too.

## Founder radar

### [Nature Will announces Natura-1, a 50B-parameter model for dexterous hands](https://runtimewire.com/article/nature-will-natura-1-robot-hand-foundation-model)
Beijing-based Nature Will (some outlets write "Natural Will") announced Natura-1 on 13 October. By the company's account, it is a 50-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model pretrained on more than a million hours of first-person human video. It takes language and vision as input and drives 20-degree-of-freedom hands within a 100 ms control loop, after fine-tuning on a modest amount of robot data. The demos include folding thin materials and calligraphy. Big caveats: the benchmark score (53.4) is run by the vendor, nothing has been independently replicated, and there are no weights, no API and no pricing. Treat it as a press release with a parameter count.

Still, put it next to KODA above and a pattern stands out. The bet across the industry is that **human egocentric video is the pretraining corpus for robots**, the way web text was for language models. If that is right, the valuable startups may not be the ones training the big model. They may be the ones who own the pipes: capture hardware people actually wear, retargeting that survives controller changes, labelling and quality filtering for hand video at scale. Those are infrastructure problems that a small team can own. More detail: [humanoid.guide](https://humanoid.guide/?p=20402).

## Outside your bubble

### [A "Pac-Man" enzyme that eats plastic, and penicillin](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261014015509.htm)
Biologists Harry Lerner and David Schleheck at the University of Konstanz buried films of a new biodegradable polyester in forest soil and watched what came for them. The answer was soil bacteria carrying an esterase on their cell surface, nicknamed "Pac-Man" for its unusually wide active site. It chews polymer chains into their building blocks, and the buried films ended up full of bacteria-sized holes. The surprise: the enzyme is structurally related to beta-lactamases, the enzymes bacteria use to destroy antibiotics. In the lab it cut penicillin as well as plastic. The paper is in The ISME Journal. Caveats: cutting penicillin in a test tube does not mean the bacteria are antibiotic resistant, the films took 250 to 330 days to degrade, and ordinary polyethylene barely degraded at all.

Why it is here: two ideas transfer well. First, evolution reuses machinery. A tool built for one job (fighting antibiotics) turns out to do another (eating a material that did not exist a few years ago). That is the same reason a motion tracker turns into a universal task interface. Second, the chemists' takeaway is to *design materials with breaking points* that biology can attack, rather than hoping nature adapts to whatever we make. It is a design-for-the-end-of-life mindset, and robotics has almost none of it. What would it mean to design a robot, or a policy, so that it fails in a planned and harmless way?
