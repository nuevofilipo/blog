# What the robot thinks it knows: state estimation for a walking policy

*2026-10-13*

Yesterday the policy drove real motors for the first time, on paper. Today's question is one that ambushes most teams somewhere around Gate 3: the policy was trained on numbers that the simulator handed it for free, and some of those numbers do not exist on the real robot. Also today: a humanoid that plays tennis rallies after learning from LAFAN1 and YouTube, Xue Bin Peng's group making retargeting obey a strict pecking order, a world model that plans 32 times faster, DeepSeek placing its first bet on robots, and a clock that ticks with an atomic nucleus.

## Robot learning lesson: state estimation, or the lies in your observation vector

**The simulator is an oracle. Hardware is not.** In Isaac Lab or MuJoCo, base linear velocity, base orientation and foot contact are just fields you read from the physics state. On the robot, every one of them has to be estimated from sensors, and every estimate is wrong in its own way. A policy that learned to rely on a perfect velocity signal will treat the estimator's errors as real events and react to them. That shows up as drift, stepping in place, or a gait that is fine for ten seconds and then wanders off.

**Sort your observations into three buckets.**
- *Measured directly and trustworthy:* joint positions and velocities from the encoders, base angular velocity from the IMU gyro. Add realistic noise in training and you are done.
- *Estimated but reliable:* the gravity vector in the base frame (the "projected gravity" most locomotion code uses). The IMU's onboard filter gets roll and pitch well because gravity anchors them. Yaw drifts, which is one reason good policies use gravity direction and not a full orientation quaternion.
- *Estimated and treacherous:* base linear velocity, and base height if you use it. Integrate an accelerometer and the velocity error grows without bound within seconds. This is the observation that silently breaks sim-to-real.

**You have three ways to deal with linear velocity.**

1. *Drop it.* Many humanoid policies simply leave base linear velocity out of the actor's observations and give it a short history of proprioception instead (the last few steps of joint states, actions and gyro). The policy learns to infer its own motion implicitly. Keep the true velocity in the critic, since the critic never runs on hardware: this asymmetric actor-critic setup is cheap and strong. For a first walking policy on a custom robot, this is the safe default.

2. *Classic filter.* Fuse IMU with leg kinematics: when a foot is in stance, it is assumed not to move, so the body velocity follows from the joint velocities through the forward kinematics. This idea has deep ETH roots, Michael Bloesch's RSS 2012 work on fusing leg kinematics and IMU came out of the Autonomous Systems Lab, and a well-known modern version is the [contact-aided invariant EKF](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.09251) from Hartley, Grizzle and colleagues. The weak point is contact detection: if the filter thinks a slipping foot is planted, the velocity estimate lies exactly when the robot is in trouble.

3. *Learned estimator.* Train a small network that maps proprioceptive history to base velocity (and optionally foot contact and height), supervised by simulator ground truth, and feed its output to the policy. The KAIST paper [Concurrent Training of a Control Policy and a State Estimator](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.05481) (Ji, Mun, Kim, Hwangbo) trains both at the same time, so the policy learns to live with the estimator's real error, not a clean signal. That last part is the key: whatever estimator you deploy, the policy should see that estimator's output during training, not the true state plus Gaussian noise.

**How to check it on hardware.** Before you trust any estimate, compare it against something independent. A motion capture room is the gold standard; a treadmill with a known belt speed or a tape measure and a stopwatch is the student version. Walk the robot on the gantry at a commanded 0.3 m/s and plot estimated forward velocity against the truth. Look for bias (it always says 0.25), lag (it catches up a few hundred milliseconds late) and spikes at foot touchdown. Each of those is a specific thing to add to your training noise model.

**One more trap: frame and sign conventions.** Velocity in the world frame versus the base frame, the IMU mounted rotated by 90 degrees, a yaw rate with the wrong sign. The 10 October audit and Gate 1 from yesterday catch most of these, but run the check again after anyone touches the IMU mount or firmware.

**Rule of thumb:** if a quantity is not measured on the real robot, the actor should either not see it, or see exactly what the deployed estimator would produce. Everything else goes in the critic.

## New papers worth reading

### [ATHLETE: Learning Reactive Humanoid Tennis via Trajectory-Guided Motion Matching](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13572)
Submitted 9 October, from Junwei Liang's group. Recording motion capture for every way a robot might approach and hit a tennis ball is hopeless, so the authors stitch together two cheap sources: generic locomotion clips from LAFAN1 and swing motions pulled from ordinary tennis videos. Their Trajectory-Guided Motion Matching combines them into varied whole-body reference motions for a given ball. Then the familiar recipe: a teacher-student pair in simulation with online DAgger, followed by PPO fine-tuning on the actual task. At deployment the policy reads only robot and ball state, no reference motion. On a real humanoid it hits 90 percent of forehands and 86.7 percent of backhands, and plays multi-shot rallies with people. The lesson for a club project: general locomotion data plus a little task-specific video can go a long way, and the reference motions are scaffolding you can remove at the end.

### [PriMoRetarget: Humanoid Motion Transfer Through Prioritized Motion Retargeting](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12642)
From Xue Bin Peng's group (the DeepMimic and AMP author). Retargeting human motion to a robot is usually one big weighted cost: match the pose, keep the feet planted, keep the hand on the box, avoid self-collision, with weights you tune until it looks fine. PriMoRetarget replaces the weights with a strict priority order: foot placement and interaction contacts first, and lower-priority pose matching may only act in ways that do not disturb the higher ones. Tested on four datasets including human-object and human-human interaction, and the reference motions turn out easier for a robot to track. No headline numbers in the abstract. It pairs nicely with yesterday's VICAR: two groups converging on "contacts first, pose second" in the same week is a signal.

### [LeWAM: End-to-End World and Action Modeling with JEPAs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12818)
From Randall Balestriero and colleagues. One model learns a JEPA-style latent world model and an action policy (via flow matching) together, so the same network can act directly or plan by imagining futures in latent space. Reported success rises from 28.6 to 89.7 percent on contact-rich tasks and from 10.9 to 46.2 percent on long-horizon tasks, with planning 32.3 times faster than the comparison. It is manipulation, not walking, but worth knowing as the "what comes after walking" phase approaches: world models are becoming practical planners, not just pretraining tricks.

## Founder radar

### [DeepSeek leads a seed round for Winverse AI](https://investing.com/news/stock-market-news/deepseek-backs-extencent-scientists-robotics-startup-winverse-ai--bloomberg-93CH-4944424)
Reported by Bloomberg on 12 October. Winverse AI, a few months old, is founded by Hu Han, who led Tencent's vision-language and embodied foundation model teams. DeepSeek has agreed to lead a seed round of "tens of millions of dollars", with Meituan's Long-Z Investments among the others, at a post-money valuation of about 400 million dollars. The deal is not final and terms may change. It is DeepSeek's first visible step from chatbots into the physical world.

Why it matters: a seed round at 400 million is a pure bet on a person and a model team, not a product. The pattern is the same as Physical Intelligence or Skild in the US: whoever trains the robot brain wants many bodies, and whoever has the best LLM lab wants a robotics team. For a student founder, the opposite corner is open: you will not out-fund a DeepSeek-backed foundation model, but every one of these brains needs reliable bodies, data pipelines, evaluation and deployment tooling.

**Idea seed.** State estimation is an unglamorous, universal problem: every legged robot needs a velocity estimate it can trust, every team rebuilds it, and most do it badly. A drop-in estimator package (IMU plus kinematics plus a learned correction, with a calibration routine and a validation report against a cheap ground truth) for the hundreds of custom humanoids now being built in labs and clubs would be a small, real product. Your club's walking project is the first customer and the test bench.

## Outside your bubble

### [A clock that ticks with an atomic nucleus](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261008102701.htm)
Published in Nature, reported 10 October. A TU Wien team led by Thorsten Schumm built the first self-stabilizing nuclear clock. Ordinary atomic clocks count the oscillations of electrons jumping between energy levels. This one uses a transition inside the nucleus of thorium-229, held in a special crystal and driven by a laser, and a feedback loop locks the laser to the nuclei's own absorption, so no atomic clock is needed as a reference. It ran for more than 24 hours unattended, with a relative precision around 10^-15. It is a prototype and not yet better than the best optical atomic clocks; the point is that the nucleus is about 10,000 times smaller than the atom and far less disturbed by stray fields, so the ceiling is higher.

Two thoughts to take away. First, this is a decades-long bet paying off: the thorium transition was predicted in the 1970s and only pinned down by laser in 2024. Some of the best ideas are slow. Second, look at the move: the clock is good because it measures something the environment can barely touch. That is the same instinct as today's lesson, trust the signals that are hard to corrupt (gravity, gyro, encoders) and be suspicious of the ones that drift.
