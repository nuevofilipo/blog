## Robot learning

### [HumanVerse-500 and lambda-0: pretraining humanoid loco-manipulation on human whole-body data](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00438)
Chongyang Xu, Zhao Wu, Jin Chen and others, with Steven C.H. Hoi; posted 30 September.

The authors built a lightweight wearable rig that records egocentric video together with body and hand tracking, and used it to collect HumanVerse-500, 500 hours of people doing loco-manipulation (walking while handling things) in open-world settings. Their model, lambda-0, trains in three stages: general pretraining on existing egocentric human datasets, then whole-body coordination on HumanVerse-500, then task-specific adaptation on the robot. They report state-of-the-art results in simulation and on real tasks, and promise to release code, models and data. Why it matters for the club: once your robot walks, the "teach it tasks" phase runs straight into the data problem, and the bet here is that human body data, captured cheaply, is the scalable source rather than robot teleoperation alone. Watch for the dataset release; it may be usable for motion priors even before you do manipulation.

### [DexPolicy: an exploration schedule turns weak RL runs into strong ones](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00360)
Haoyu Wang, Siyuan Qian, Yanjun Li and others, with Hao Tang; posted 30 September. [Project page](https://aigeeksgroup.github.io/DexPolicy), [code](https://github.com/AIGeeksGroup/DexPolicy).

Dexterous manipulation needs broad exploration early (to discover finger-object contacts at all) and precise, low-noise actions late (to control the object). DexPolicy makes the exploration scale an explicit function of training step, annealing from broad to narrow, instead of letting the policy learn its own action noise. Simulation success jumps from 49.4% to 68.1% with flow-based PPO (FPO), from 14.1% to 45.4% with critic-free GRPO, and from 32.0% to 35.7% with plain PPO; on a real RealMan arm, FPO goes from 25% to 85%. Takeaway for walking: the action noise level is a first-class hyperparameter, not a detail. See the lesson below.

## Learn

**PPO vs off-policy RL for a walking policy, and the knobs that matter**

The earlier lessons covered actions, rewards, domain randomization and observations. The last piece before your first training run is the algorithm.

**PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization)** is the default for legged locomotion: legged_gym, Isaac Lab (via rsl_rl) and Humanoid-Gym all use it. It is on-policy: it collects a batch of experience with the current policy across thousands of parallel simulated robots, takes a few gradient steps on that batch, then throws it away. It is stable, forgiving and well understood, and with massive GPU parallelism its sample inefficiency stops mattering because samples are cheap.

**Off-policy methods (SAC, TD3, and the recent FastSAC and FastTD3)** keep a replay buffer and reuse old experience many times, learning a Q-function. Historically they were awkward with thousands of parallel environments, but the "FastTD3/FastSAC" line (Seo, Abbeel and others) showed that with large batches, many environments and a distributional critic they can train humanoid walking in minutes of wall-clock time on one GPU. Rule of thumb: start with PPO because every reference config and every debugging forum post assumes it; try FastTD3 or FastSAC once your environment works, when wall-clock time per experiment becomes the bottleneck.

**PPO knobs, and what each does** (typical rsl_rl humanoid values in brackets):

- **Number of environments and steps per environment** [4096 envs x 24 steps]: together they set the batch size per update. Bigger batches give lower-variance gradients. If training is noisy, raise the env count before touching anything else.
- **Learning rate** [1e-3 with adaptive schedule]: rsl_rl adapts it to keep the KL divergence between old and new policy near a target [0.01]. If the KL keeps blowing past the target, the policy is changing too fast.
- **Clip range** [0.2]: limits how far each update can move the action probabilities. It is PPO's main safety rail; rarely worth changing.
- **Discount gamma** [0.99] and **GAE lambda** [0.95]: gamma sets how far ahead the policy cares (0.99 at 50 Hz is about a 2 second horizon). Lambda trades bias against variance in the advantage estimate.
- **Learning epochs and mini-batches** [5 epochs, 4 mini-batches]: how many times each batch is reused. More reuse learns faster per sample but risks instability.
- **Entropy coefficient** [0.01 or lower] and **initial action noise std** [about 1.0, in normalized action units]: these control exploration. This is exactly what DexPolicy schedules. Watch the logged action std: if it collapses to near zero early, the policy has stopped exploring and progress stalls; if it stays high, the deployed gait will be jittery because the policy never learned precise actions.
- **Network size** [actor and critic MLPs around 512-256-128, ELU]: rarely the bottleneck for walking. Do not tune it first.

**What to watch in the logs.** Mean episode length (are robots falling?), each reward term separately (which one is the policy exploiting?), value loss (exploding means reward scale is off), action std (exploration) and KL (update size). Change one thing at a time and keep a seed-fixed baseline run to compare against.

**A practical order of operations.** Get a stock config walking on a known robot (Unitree H1 or G1 in Isaac Lab) first, so you know your pipeline is correct. Then swap in your own robot's URDF and actuator model with the same hyperparameters. Only then start tuning rewards, and only after rewards start tuning algorithm knobs.

Further reading: [rsl_rl](https://github.com/leggedrobotics/rsl_rl), the PPO implementation from ETH RSL behind most of this; [FastTD3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22642) for the off-policy alternative; and "The 37 Implementation Details of PPO" (Huang and others, ICLR blog track 2022) for what actually makes PPO work in practice: [blog post](https://iclr-blog-track.github.io/2022/03/25/ppo-implementation-details/).

## Founder radar

### [Quartermaster: $140M Series B, of which $40M is debt](https://traded.co/vc/deal/quartermaster-secures-140-million-series-b-investment-led-by-insight-partners-in-arlington/)
The Arlington, Virginia maritime intelligence startup raised a Series B led by Insight Partners in late September, split into $100M of equity and a $40M debt facility from Stifel, only months after a $43M Series A in May. The structure is the interesting part: hardware-heavy companies increasingly use debt to finance things that produce revenue (fleets, units, inventory) so that they give away less ownership. If you ever build a robot company, learn early which costs can be financed with debt and which only with equity.

**Idea seed.** HumanVerse-500 relied on a cheap wearable capture rig. A university-scale version, a club or lab that builds a low-cost whole-body capture suit and collects task data in everyday Swiss environments, is both a research asset and a possible startup: everyone training humanoids wants diverse human data, and few teams want to build the collection operation themselves.

## Outside your bubble

### [ESA's Juice used Earth as a slingshot to Jupiter](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261001214102.htm)
On 28 September the Juice spacecraft passed 8,640 km above the Indian Ocean, using Earth's gravity to bend its path by 20 degrees and gain 3.5 km/s, with no fuel spent on that change. The team needed only one of six planned course corrections, so it saved propellant for later. Another Earth flyby comes in January 2029, and it arrives at Jupiter in 2031 for 35 flybys of Ganymede, Callisto and Europa, looking at their buried oceans. The general idea is worth taking with you: instead of paying for a change yourself, borrow energy from a system that already has plenty of it. In robotics, passive dynamics and good mechanical design do the same thing for a walking gait.
