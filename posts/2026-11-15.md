# The e-stop that makes it fall

*2026-11-15*

Sunday morning, so let's talk about the least glamorous part of making a humanoid walk: the big red button. A team from the University of Colorado has written an unusually honest paper about what happens when a full-size humanoid gets pushed, loses its balance and someone hits the emergency stop. Short version: the stop is what makes it fall. If your club is about to put a custom robot on the floor for its first steps, this is the paper to read before you plug in the battery. Also today: Runway open-sources a 27-billion-parameter world action model, a humanoid controller that feels force without force sensors, Nvidia losing its CEO two days before earnings, and why your neck may be an accident.

## Robot learning lesson: design the stop before you design the walk

**The paper.** [Runtime Safety and Emergency Stops on a Full-Size Humanoid](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15928) (Conway, Zhang, Devaraj, Xie, Roncone, Correll, posted 11 November). The robot is a Unitree H1-2 running an open-source autonomy stack, aimed at nasty industrial jobs like taking apart electric-car batteries.

**The setup.** Every joint command passes through a separate safety layer that runs outside the control software. It enforces limits on joint position, velocity and torque, and it stops the robot if the state estimate goes stale or if the hard-wired e-stop is pressed or unplugged. Textbook stuff, and roughly what most student teams build.

**What went wrong.**
- **The push test.** They pushed the standing robot until it lost balance. With their model-predictive lower-body controller, it never recovered on its own. The operator had to hit the e-stop in every single trial. And because the e-stop cuts balance control, the robot then fell.
- **Limits that fire during normal walking.** The joint limits had been chosen for manipulation. Normal walking exceeded them, which triggered the same stop, which meant the same fall. The safety layer itself was causing accidents.
- **The fall can be worse than the fault.** Next to an operator or a battery pack, a 1.7-metre robot collapsing is more dangerous than whatever made the system want to stop.
- **Limits measure force, not judgement.** Joint limits cap how hard the robot can push. They say nothing about whether pushing is a good idea right now.

**Their proposal.** "Graduated force authority": give the robot permission to use force in stages, like a teenager getting a learner's permit before a full licence, with a named person or organisation explicitly responsible at each stage. It's a policy idea more than an algorithm, but the underlying engineering point is sharp: a stop is not one thing.

**What to take for your walking project.**
- **Separate "stop" from "cut power".** Plan at least two levels: a soft stop that tells the policy to come to a controlled stand or a controlled crouch and sit, and a hard stop that kills torque. Only the second should be the red button, and it should be the last resort.
- **Train the fall, not just the walk.** If the robot is going to fall anyway, make it a good fall. Learned fall-and-recover or protective-crouch behaviours are a normal RL task in Isaac Lab or MuJoCo: random pushes, a reward for low impact velocity on the torso and head, and a reward for ending in a safe pose. Consider it a second policy you can switch to.
- **Fit your limits to walking data.** Before setting position, velocity and torque limits, log them during simulated walking with domain randomisation and pushes, then add margin. Limits copied from a datasheet or a manipulation setup will fire on the first stumble.
- **Make stale state a soft fault.** A dropped IMU packet shouldn't cut torque mid-stride. Hold the last command for a few milliseconds, then go to a soft stop if it persists.
- **Gantry first, always.** For the first walking trials, a harness or gantry turns "the e-stop makes it fall" from a disaster into a non-event. Ask whoever is mechanical lead whether one exists, and whether it catches before the knees hit the floor.

The big idea: on a legged robot, "safe" and "stopped" are different states. Design the path from one to the other before you need it.

## New papers and releases worth reading

- **[Praxis-1 by Runway](https://www.therobotreport.com/runways-praxis-1-world-action-model-for-robotics-is-now-available/)** ([code](https://github.com/runwayml/praxis-1-oss), [weights](https://huggingface.co/collections/runwayml/praxis-1)). The video-generation company released a 27-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts diffusion transformer that predicts future video and robot actions together, with open weights under Apache 2.0, fine-tuning code including LoRA, and native LeRobot support. It runs on anything from a B200 to an RTX 5090 to a Jetson Thor. On the RoboLab-120 benchmark it scores 46.2% against 28.0% for pi0.5, and fine-tuned on RT-1 data it gets 80.0% on SimplerEnv. Launch partners include humanoid maker Noble Machines. The interesting bit is the bet: learn how the world moves from video first, and learn to act second. For your later task phase, this is one of the few open models of this class you could actually fine-tune on a student budget.
- **[FORGE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17142)** (Xing, Fan, Li and others, [project page](https://bb0928.github.io/forge/)). A humanoid loco-manipulation controller with commands that go from coarse to fine: walk at this velocity, put your feet here, put your wrists there, and optionally push this hard. Two things stand out. It needs no full-body human motion data for training. And it estimates the force at the wrists from joint readings and the arm PD torques alone, with no force-torque sensor, then adjusts the wrist target to apply the force you asked for. It keeps a frozen locomotion policy underneath and adds residual corrections on top, which is the same "don't break the walking" pattern as PERA yesterday. Useful when your robot graduates to carrying things.

## Founder radar

### The tools layer is filling up

Three launches from this week point the same way:

- **[Viam Launchpad](https://www.therobotreport.com/viam-launchpad-aims-to-accelerate-robotics-deployments/)** sells software and engineering infrastructure to help robotics startups get from a working demo to commercial deployment.
- **[Memories.ai](https://www.therobotreport.com/memories-ai-builds-standardized-egocentric-video-pipeline/)** launched a standardised pipeline for collecting and annotating first-person video as robot training data.
- **[Serve Robotics](https://www.therobotreport.com/serve-robotics-opens-robot-delivery-network-for-others-to-build-on/)** opened its fleet of more than 2,000 delivery robots to third parties through an on-demand API. That turns a robot company into a platform others can build on.

The seed: in every gold rush, the money is split between people digging and people selling tools. In robotics, a lot of the digging (building full humanoids) now needs hundreds of millions. The tools layer, meaning data pipelines, deployment software, evaluation, safety layers, fleet APIs, still has room for small teams. Today's safety paper is a good example. A clean, tested "graduated stop" layer that any humanoid team could drop between their policy and their motors doesn't really exist as a product. Neither does good fall-recovery training as a service. Things you build for your own club because you need them are often the best startup ideas, because you already know the customer.

The thesis: whoever wins in humanoids will need motors, drives and factory integration, which Yaskawa already sells. The risks: it's tied to the factory-investment cycle, it competes with Fanuc and ABB on margins, and its humanoid work is still early.

## Outside your bubble

### [Your neck might be an accident](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261108233101.htm)

The textbook story goes like this. Fish don't have necks, because their shoulders are attached straight to the skull, so to look sideways they turn their whole body. When our ancestors crawled out of the water about 380 million years ago, they evolved necks so they could turn their heads to spot prey and danger without moving everything else. Neat, logical, and possibly wrong.

A team led by Penn State (first author Dana Korneisel, now at Uppsala; published in *Nature*) asked a simple question: could those early animals actually move their heads? Early four-limbed animals like *Tiktaalik* didn't have a stack of jointed vertebrae. They had a notochord, a thick flexible rod of soft tissue, and soft tissue almost never fossilises. So the team used the sturgeon, a living fish with a similar notochord, measured how stiff it is, and plugged those numbers into models of the fossils. The result: even with the head separated from the shoulders, the backbone barely let them move it. Lifting the head was no better than in fish, sometimes worse, and twisting it like shaking your head wasn't possible in any of the models. The authors suggest the neck appeared as a side effect of changes that let the front limbs hold up and move the body. Mobility came later. The caveats are real: a sturgeon is a distant cousin, and muscles and skin would make the necks even stiffer than modelled.

Why it belongs here: evolution kept something that appeared for one reason and later turned out to be great for another. Engineers and founders do this all the time without noticing. A feature built to support the body ends up being the thing that lets you look around. And notice the method: when the data you need is missing (soft tissue that never fossilises), find a living system with the same mechanics and measure that. It's the same move as using a simulator when you can't afford to break the real robot.
