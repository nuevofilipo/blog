# How do you know the gait is good?

*2026-10-11*

Training curves go up, the robot walks in the viewer, and someone asks: is it ready for hardware? The mean episode reward cannot answer that. Today's lesson is how to evaluate a walking policy with a fixed test suite and a small set of numbers that mean something, so that "it looks fine" becomes a decision you can defend. Plus a humanoid world-action model that predicts what its body will actually do, a study of why chained skills fail at the seams, a humanoid that finds its way by touch, Agility's unit economics laid bare at its investor day, and a nuclear clock that keeps itself on time.

## Robot learning lesson: evaluating a walking policy

**Why reward is not the metric.** Reward is a training signal that you tuned to make PPO learn. It mixes a dozen terms with weights you changed last week, so a run with a higher return may simply have a softer penalty on joint torque. It is also averaged over the training distribution, including the easy terrain and the slow commands. You need numbers that stay fixed across runs and that relate to what you care about on the real robot: does it stay up, does it go where it is told, how hard does it push the motors, and how does it degrade when things go wrong.

**A small metric set.** Run every checkpoint you might deploy through the same evaluation, with randomization fixed by seed and the policy acting deterministically (the mean action, no sampling noise). Report:

- **Survival rate.** Fraction of episodes of fixed length (say 20 s) without a fall, where a fall is a termination condition you define once (base height below a threshold, or torso tilt above a limit). This is the headline number.
- **Command tracking error.** Mean absolute error between commanded and achieved base velocity (forward, lateral, yaw), measured after a settling window of about 1 s. Break it down per command range: many policies track 0.5 m/s well and quietly stop trying at 1.2 m/s.
- **Torque and power.** Peak and RMS joint torque as a fraction of the motor limit, plus mean mechanical power. A policy that sits at 95 percent of the knee's torque limit will overheat or saturate on hardware even if the simulation is happy.
- **Cost of transport.** Mechanical power divided by weight times speed. It is dimensionless, so you can compare runs and even compare with published robots. A falling cost of transport across training usually means the gait is getting smoother, not just faster.
- **Action smoothness.** Mean squared difference between consecutive actions. High-frequency chatter is the most common thing that looks fine in the viewer and destroys gearboxes.
- **Foot behavior.** Contact pattern (is it a clean alternating gait with a consistent period), foot slip velocity while in contact, and foot clearance during swing. Slipping feet in simulation usually become stumbles on a real floor.

**A fixed test suite, not just random rollouts.** Random evaluation hides specific failures. Add scripted scenarios that every candidate must pass:

1. Stand still for 30 s with zero command. Many walking policies march on the spot or drift; check base drift and joint jitter.
2. Step changes in command: 0 to 1 m/s, then a stop, then a 90 degree turn. Look at overshoot and how long it takes to settle.
3. Pushes: a horizontal velocity impulse to the torso from four directions at increasing magnitude. Plot survival against push size. This curve is far more informative than one push setting.
4. Out-of-distribution physics: friction, mass and motor strength set just outside the training randomization range, plus 20 ms extra action latency. A good policy degrades gradually; a brittle one falls off a cliff.
5. Sensor faults: IMU bias and dropped joint readings for a few steps.

**Cross-simulator check.** If you trained in Isaac Lab, replay the exported policy in MuJoCo (or the other way around) with the same robot description and the same control frequency. Contact models differ between simulators, so a policy that only survives in its training simulator has probably learned to exploit it. This "sim-to-sim" step is cheap and catches many problems before the robot hangs from the gantry. The deployment post on 5 October covered exporting the policy; this is the first place to use that export.

**Make it a script and a table.** One command should take a checkpoint, run the suite, and write a single row: run name, git hash, survival, tracking error per range, peak torque fraction, cost of transport, smoothness, and the push-survival curve. Keep every row. When the team argues about whether the new reward term helped, the table settles it, and when a hardware test goes badly you can check whether the warning was already in the numbers.

**Then the hardware version.** On the real robot you cannot measure everything, but you can log joint positions, velocities, motor currents and IMU at the control rate and compute the same smoothness, torque and contact metrics offline. Start with the stand-still and step-command tests on the gantry, then on the floor with a spotter. Comparing the hardware row to the simulation row for the same checkpoint is the most direct measure of your sim-to-real gap.

## New papers worth reading

### [Humanoid World Action Model With Joint State-Action Generation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12026)
Submitted 8 October. In a hierarchical humanoid, the high-level policy outputs reference motions and a whole-body controller tries to follow them; balance and contacts mean the executed motion differs. The authors call this the action-execution gap and make the robot's post-execution proprioceptive state an explicit prediction target: the model generates reference actions and the resulting body states together, trained with a policy path, a forward-dynamics path that predicts future images, and an inverse-dynamics path. On a LimX OLI humanoid it reaches 70.6 percent success on a candy-picking task against 43.3 percent for the Fast-WAM baseline. Relevant for the club's second phase: once the walking controller exists, any task policy on top of it has to reason about what the legs will actually do, not what it asked for.

### [Diagnosing and Recovering from Observation-Space Shift at Long-Horizon Skill Seams](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10810)
Submitted 7 October. When independently trained skills are chained, each one starts from whatever state the previous one left behind. Using privileged simulator resets, the authors find the main cause of failure is not the robot's joint configuration but displaced scene state, such as a drawer left open. Their fix is a progress monitor that detects stalls, a learned policy that restores the scene, and fine-tuning that makes the skill robust to resuming. On the BOSS-44 benchmark full-chain success rises from 7.6 to 26.5 percent; on a real Franka with a fine-tuned pi0.5 policy, the monitor was limited by what the external camera could see. A good reminder that task evaluation, like gait evaluation, should test the transitions, not just each skill alone.

### [TAPNAV: Humanoid Navigation through Tactile Active Perception](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10748)
Submitted 7 October. A Unitree G1 navigates to a goal without vision by deliberately touching walls and obstacles. Pose is estimated by fusing odometry, IMU and contacts; a global planner picks routes that pass places where touch can correct drift, a local planner picks probing actions that maximize information gain, and a whole-body controller combines walking with reaching. The abstract reports lower estimation error and higher completion than baselines but no numbers. The idea worth taking away: when a sensor fails, a legged robot can turn its own body into the sensor.

## Founder radar

### [Agility's investor day: Digit 5 at a 150,000 dollar bill of materials](https://www.humanoidsdaily.com/news/agility-investor-day-digit-5-launch-costs-and-a-300m-order-that-unlocks-skill-by-skill)
Held 6 October, ahead of Agility's SPAC listing. The rare thing here is that a humanoid company published its unit economics. Digit 5 will cost about 150,000 dollars in materials at launch (Digit 4 is about 125,000), with a target of 30,000 dollars per unit at 10,000 units a year; the in-house cycloidal actuators are about a quarter of the cost. Early-access units ship in the first half of 2027, general availability around the end of 2027. The business model is robots-as-a-service: about 500,000 dollars of revenue per robot over five years, against roughly 240,000 dollars of cost, so about 50 percent margin at launch. 2025 revenue was 1.78 million dollars. The headline 300 million dollar order covers 1,000 robots from an unnamed related-party customer, released in tranches: each new use case, with throughput targets agreed with the customer, unlocks the next batch. At Schaeffler, Digit has moved bearing parts for about 18 months.

The founder lesson is in that contract structure. Nobody buys "a general-purpose humanoid"; they buy one proven task at a time, and revenue follows skills, not units. That also means the bottleneck is how fast a team can go from a new task to a reliable, measured deployment, which is exactly the evaluation problem in today's lesson at a larger scale.

**Idea seed.** If customers pay per proven skill, there is room for a tool that turns "the robot can do X" into a signed-off acceptance test: a standard protocol, logged trials, throughput and intervention rate, and a report a plant manager trusts. Think of it as a certification and evaluation layer for robot skills, sold to integrators and to the customers checking them.

Also this week: Microagi, which sells human-activity data for robot training through a platform called Shift, claimed a 100 million dollar annualized revenue run rate six months after Shift launched ([report](https://www.humanoidsdaily.com/news/microagi-100-million-revenue-run-rate-six-months)). The figure is unverified and the company has not explained it, but it is another sign that selling training data is currently one of the fastest ways to make money in robotics.

## Outside your bubble

### [A nuclear clock that keeps itself on time](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261008102701.htm)
Reported 10 October, published in Nature. Atomic clocks count the oscillations of light that makes electrons in an atom jump between energy levels. Thorium-229 is unusual: its nucleus has two states so close in energy that a laser can drive the transition, which was first shown in 2024. Nuclei are about ten thousand times smaller than atoms and much better shielded from stray fields, so a clock based on them could in principle beat the best atomic clocks. A team at TU Wien led by Thorsten Schumm has now built the first nuclear clock that stabilizes itself: thorium atoms sit in a crystal, a laser shines on them, and because the nuclei absorb only at exactly the right frequency, a feedback loop pulls the laser back whenever absorption drops. It ran for over 24 hours without intervention, at a relative precision of about one part in 10^15. It is not yet as good as the best optical atomic clocks; better lasers and crystals are the next step.

Two things to take away. The core mechanism is a plain feedback controller: the physical system defines the reference, and the loop holds the actuator (the laser) on it, the same structure as a PD loop holding a joint on target. And more precise time is not only about clocks: if a fundamental constant of nature drifted slightly, a nuclear clock and an atomic clock would drift apart, so comparing them is a way to test physics itself.
