# Falling down is part of the gait

*2026-11-19*

Good morning. Yesterday was about what makes a walking robot stop. Today is about what happens after it stops the hard way: on its face. Every humanoid team learns sooner or later that the robot will fall, and the real question is whether the next ten seconds bring a recovery or a sad walk across the lab with a hoist. A paper from yesterday trains one policy that walks, runs, falls, gets up and carries on, with no switching between controllers. That's the main thread. After that: DeepMind's new robot brain on a Mercedes line, a VLA fine-tuning result that goes against intuition, robot video labelling getting very cheap, and a billion years of missing rock.

## The big one: one policy for walking and getting back up

**[ReLoco: Unified Humanoid Locomotion and Fall Recovery via Behavior-Conditioned Motion Priors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23916)** (Fu, Hu, Zhong and others), submitted 18 November.

The usual setup on a real humanoid is two or three policies held together with a state machine: a walking controller, a get-up controller, and some logic that decides "we are on the floor now" and hands over control. Every handover is a seam, and seams are where robots break, both literally and in your debugging schedule. ReLoco gets rid of the seams. A single proprioceptive policy handles locomotion, standing up from any fallen posture, and going back to whatever velocity command it was following.

How it works, from the abstract:

1. **Motion priors chosen by posture.** Training uses adversarial motion priors, where a discriminator rewards the policy for moving like reference motions. The key point is that the robot's current posture decides which pool of expert motions the discriminator compares against: walking clips when upright, get-up clips when on the ground.
2. **One discriminator, two heads.** Rather than a single output that has to judge both "nice walking" and "nice getting up", the shared discriminator has a separate output per behaviour. Their ablation shows this matters: velocity tracking error is 14.7% lower than with a single-output version.
3. **Training wheels for exploration.** Getting up from a random pile of limbs is a nasty exploration problem, because a random policy almost never stumbles into standing. They add temporary assistance during training and remove it later. Think of it as a hand under the robot's back that gradually goes away.
4. **References the robot can actually perform.** The reference motions are refined in simulation, so the discriminator compares against motions that this robot executed, not human mocap it physically can't reproduce.

The numbers: 96.8% independent get-up success and 97% push-recovery success, both including going back to the commanded motion. On the real Tiangong 3.0, a 64.5 kg full-size humanoid, the one actor walks, runs, gets up and resumes walking without controller switching or fine-tuning on the robot. There's no code link yet.

**Why this matters for your walking phase.** You're not going to build ReLoco next month, and you shouldn't. But three habits from it are worth starting now:

- **Treat falling as a state, not a failure.** In your training environments, don't reset as soon as the torso drops below some height. Even before you train get-up skills, starting some episodes from fallen or nearly fallen states makes the policy much better at catching itself. Later it gives you the data a get-up skill needs.
- **Collect get-up references on your own hardware early.** Hand-pose the robot through a get-up in sim and keep only what the simulated robot can actually do. Item 4 above is the step most likely to be skipped, and it's the one that makes adversarial priors work on a custom body.
- **Keep the observation space the same for every behaviour.** One policy across behaviours only works if it sees the same inputs in every state. If your walking policy relies on a gait phase clock, ask yourself what that clock means when the robot is lying on its back.

The bigger idea: a state machine is a list of things you've decided the policy can't learn. Every year that list gets shorter.

## DeepMind's robot brain gets a factory job

On 10 November, Google DeepMind released **Gemini Robotics 2.5**, its new vision-language-action model ([Robotics 24/7](https://www.robotics247.com/article/google-deepmind-introduces-gemini-robotics-2.5-ai-model-to-improve-robot-dexterity-and-reliability), [Let's Data Science](https://letsdatascience.com/news/deepmind-releases-gemini-robotics-25-and-open-asimov-jailbre-ca67d149)). Here's what DeepMind says:

- Across more than 200 dexterous tasks, success rates are 30% to 100% higher than Gemini Robotics 2, and tasks finish about 20% to 30% faster.
- Apptronik's Apollo 2 humanoid reached 96% success in a kitting pilot on Mercedes-Benz lines in Germany and Hungary. Kitting means collecting the right parts into a tray for the assembly line: boring, repetitive and exactly where humanoids have to prove themselves first.
- For safety, it uses reasoning at inference time to steer around physical hazards, and DeepMind is open-sourcing a benchmark called **ASIMOV-Jailbreak** for trying to talk robots into doing dangerous things.
- An internal Gemini model was used as a simulation engine to speed up training.

The catch: there's no public API. It's being rolled out only to partners. So for you this is a signal, not a tool. What it signals is that "world model as simulator" is no longer just an idea in a paper. A frontier lab now credits it with making the training of its main product faster. Put that next to the learned-simulator audit from yesterday's post, which showed how easily such models fool you, and you can see the tension the field is working through.

The safety benchmark is worth a look for a separate reason. Prompt injection against a chatbot gets you a rude paragraph. Prompt injection against a 70 kg robot is a different problem.

## New papers worth reading

- **[Finetuning VLAs for Dexterous Manipulation: The Right Checkpoint Is All You Need](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23676)** (Kuramshin, Cloutier, Przystupa, Ellis, Akhter, Berseth). This is relevant for the "teach it tasks" phase after walking. They adapt π0.5 to a multi-finger LEAP hand with a few demonstrations, then refine with diffusion-steering RL. Three findings that go against intuition: the checkpoint with the best success after supervised fine-tuning is rarely the one RL can improve most; freezing the vision-language backbone and training only the action expert gives the most steerable policy; and LoRA is the least steerable option. Their advice is to stop picking checkpoints by success after supervised fine-tuning if you plan to do RL afterwards. That's a cheap lesson to learn from someone else's compute budget.
- **[WAM-Policy: World Action Models with On-Policy Distillation for Quadrupedal Loco-Manipulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24011)** (Long and others). A quadruped walks on three legs and uses the fourth as an arm. A large world-action model is distilled into a student that runs about 14x faster and reaches 85% after task-specific RL, deployed on a Unitree Go2 Air. The pattern to note: a big generative model as the teacher, a small fast policy on the robot. Your humanoid's onboard compute will almost certainly force the same split.
- **[BiCAT: Bimanual Coordination Action Tokenization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23499)** (Liu and others). An action tokenizer for autoregressive VLAs that encodes how two arms coordinate, rather than tokenizing each arm on its own. It improves mean simulated success by 17.6 points over the best of eight baselines. Two arms that each do sensible things separately but fight each other is the bimanual equivalent of a bad gait, so it's good to see a fix at the representation level.

## Founder radar

### Labelling robot video is getting very cheap, very fast

Two price points side by side:

- **Perceptron** published a self-serve API price list for labelling egocentric and robot video: $9 per video-hour for subtask timelines, with add-ons for 2D hand tracking ($12), 3D hand tracking ($14) and per-hand activity labels ($6). The full set comes to about $29 an hour ([FourWeekMBA](https://fourweekmba.com/ai-perceptron-puts-robot-video-labels-at-9-an-hour-astra-76/), [ExplainX](https://explainx.ai/blog/perceptron-egocentric-1-0-api-9-dollars-video-hour-egohome-gpt-6-astra-one-eighth-cost-2026)). Its comparison with GPT-6 Astra (about $76 an hour) is Perceptron's own estimate.
- **Nvidia Flora**, open-sourced in late October, is a data engine built on three fine-tuned Qwen3.5 models that you can run on your own hardware. The reported figure is **72 cents per video-hour** ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/nvidia-open-sources-flora-to-make-robot-video-labeling-125x-cheaper-than-gpt-6-astra/)).

When the open-source option costs a fortieth of the cheapest commercial API, labelling stops being a business. The seed: the value moves to what comes just before and just after labelling. Before: **capture**, meaning cheap, consistent, well-calibrated egocentric rigs and the people wearing them. After: **curation**, meaning deciding which of the ten thousand labelled hours actually improve your policy. Data selection for robot learning, scoring clips by how much they would change a model, is still mostly done by intuition. A tool that took your Flora-labelled pile and your current policy and told you "train on these 300 hours, skip the rest, and here's what you're missing" would be useful to every team, including yours.

### Path Robotics: the boring robot that pays

On 10 November, Path Robotics raised a **$300M Series E at a $2.45B valuation**, led by Georgian ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/11/10/industrial-automation-startup-path-robotics-raises-300m-in-funding/), [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/path-robotics-300m-series-e-2-45bn-valuation)). It makes AI welding cells: no legs, no faces, just a gun arm that watches the seam and adapts. One tracker reports that revenue grew 17-fold in 18 months ([ARR Club](https://www.arr.club/signal/path-robotics-grew-arr-17-fold-in-18-months-raising-300m-series-e-at-2-45b-valuation)). The plan is to expand beyond welding to new tasks and robot types. That's a reminder for founders that the way into general-purpose robotics often starts with one narrow, annoying skill that a factory will pay for this quarter.

## Outside the bubble: a billion years that went missing

Stand at the bottom of the Grand Canyon and put your hand on the line where 540-million-year-old Cambrian sandstone sits directly on granite more than a billion years older. Everything that should be in between is gone. This is the **Great Unconformity**, and it shows up in Europe, Australia, Africa and Asia too. Depending on where you look, it's a gap of 200 million to over a billion years.

A [new Nature paper](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261116023403.htm) from the University of Wollongong (Dhungana, Flament) proposes a single deep-Earth cause. Between roughly a billion and 540 million years ago, convection in the mantle pushed the old continental cores upward, and erosion scraped them bare. Then the continents slowly sank, the seas came in, and new sediment buried the scraped surface, preserving the seam. Their simulations of the mantle over hundreds of millions of years reproduce this uplift-then-sinking pattern in exactly the places where the gap is clearest: North America, northern Europe and North China. The authors say plainly that it doesn't rule out other contributors.

Why it's worth a minute: it's the same move as the ReLoco paper. Instead of a separate explanation for each site, find one underlying process that produces all of them. It's also a good lesson in reading records: the most important part of a dataset can be the part that isn't there.
