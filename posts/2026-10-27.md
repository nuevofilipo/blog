# Know when you are about to fall

*2026-10-27*

Tuesday. Every walking robot falls. The question is whether it finds out half a second before the floor does, or at the same moment. A Stanford and Amazon paper from last week trains exactly that early-warning signal for a Unitree G1: a small network that watches the robot's state and says "you are not getting out of this one, hand over to the balance controller now". It is today's lesson, because when your team's robot takes its first wobbly steps, a fall detector is the cheapest piece of insurance you can build. Also: one tracking policy for 464 different humanoid bodies, a dexterous hand tracker that edits its own reference, Teradyne reporting tonight, a robot-teaching founder talk in Zurich, and a 2,700-year-old eclipse that measured the length of a day.

## Robot learning lesson: a fall monitor is a value function

**The setup.** You have a task policy (say, your walking policy) and a fallback policy (a conservative "just stand still and balance" controller). At every step you want to know: if I switched to the fallback right now, would it save me? If yes, keep walking. If the answer is about to become no, switch while you still can. The set of states from which the fallback succeeds is the *recoverability envelope*. Your monitor's job is to know where its edge is.

**The trick.** "Will the fallback succeed from here?" is a reachability question, and reachability questions can be learned like value functions in RL. [Feng, Singh, Kaingade, Bansal and Trinh](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33317) define the value as the probability that the fallback fails to reach a stationary upright state within 150 steps (3 seconds at 50 Hz). The monitor reads a 69-number state: torso height, roll and pitch, base velocities, joint positions and velocities. On the real robot, base velocity and height come from a small LSTM estimator, since you cannot measure them directly.

**The actual problem is data.** A monitor trained on states your walking policy normally visits will be confidently wrong about the strange states just before a fall, which are exactly the ones you care about. So they alternate two steps for 30 rounds:

- *Learn the value* with a mix of temporal-difference and Monte Carlo targets. TD alone generalizes, MC alone is accurate where you have rollouts; together they beat TD-only (F1 0.949 vs 0.912 on a toy system).
- *Explore on purpose.* An explorer policy is rewarded for reaching states where the monitor is uncertain (measured with random network distillation), while avoiding states the monitor is already sure are lost. It is curiosity, but aimed at the edge of the envelope.

And one conservative rule: anywhere the monitor is still uncertain counts as unrecoverable. The trigger fires if the value says "likely to fail" or the uncertainty is high.

**The numbers.** On the G1 in simulation, about 6 hours of training. On out-of-distribution pushes, recall for detecting unrecoverable states climbed from 0.67 to 0.89 over the rounds. In closed-loop walking with the hardest pushes:

- *Healthy walking policy:* falls drop from 12.2% to 8.9%, with no false alarms during normal walking or gentle pushes.
- *Degraded walking policy:* falls drop from 47.8% to 20.8%. The monitor matters most when the policy underneath is bad, which is exactly your situation for the first weeks.
- *Warning time:* on average 0.58 seconds before the torso hits the ground. Long enough for a controller, not for a human.

A simpler baseline without the time horizon fired on over 86% of perfectly fine states, which is what a naive fall detector tends to do: cry wolf until everyone ignores it.

**Caveats.** The hardware result is qualitative (a video, no numbers). The values are not calibrated probabilities. The monitor reacts to weird states rather than predicting them, and it only knows as much as the explorer could reach. No code yet.

**What to steal for your robot.**

- *Build the fallback first.* A monitor is useless without something to hand over to. A boring "stand and balance" policy, trained with heavy pushes, is the right second policy for your team, before any fancy gait.
- *Start with a cheap version.* Roll out the fallback from many random perturbed states in Isaac Lab, label each one recovered or not, and fit a small classifier. That alone gives you a first envelope. Add the uncertainty-seeking explorer when the classifier starts being confidently wrong.
- *Log what it says on hardware.* Even if you never let it trigger anything at first, a recoverability score in your logs tells you which test runs were close calls and which were never in danger. That is better data than "it fell at second 12".

It pairs well with yesterday's delay lesson: a 0.58 second warning is generous, but only if your control loop isn't eating 40 ms of it.

## New papers worth reading

### [MimicAll: one tracker, 464 bodies](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33508)
Li, Xu, Li, Zhao. Train a single whole-body motion tracker across 4,096 humanoid variants from 16 families, conditioned on a structured description of each body (global properties plus per-joint information). The headline: with the same number of training transitions, the shared policy reaches 99.4% 30-second success on randomized G1 bodies in the 100STYLE motions, against 42.9% for a specialist trained on one body. Feeding it the wrong body description drops success from 61.7% to 47.0%, so the policy really uses it. Simulation only. Why you care: your robot is custom built, so no off-the-shelf tracker was trained on it. Body-conditioned policies are the path where "describe your robot, get a controller" might one day work. It is also a strong argument that randomizing your own robot's masses and link lengths broadly, rather than trusting the CAD, does more than just robustness.

### [AdaDexTrack: let the tracker edit its own target](https://janebek.github.io/AdaDexTrack/)
Adalibieke, Han, Liu, Qin, Yi ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33852)). Dexterous hand tracking usually follows a fixed reference trajectory, often retargeted from human hands, and small errors pile up. Here a distilled generalist tracker holds the skills, and an RL-trained modulator makes three online corrections: it adjusts the reference itself, the object representation, and the position targets. Zero-shot sim-to-real, with clear gains over the tracker alone. The pattern is the same as yesterday's HoloBoxing: keep a strong low-level skill frozen, learn a small thing on top. It keeps showing up because it works.

### For the reading list
[Learning Dexterous Manipulation: Hands, Simulators, Training, and Evaluation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33486) is a fresh single-author survey covering hands, simulators, RL, sim-to-real and VLAs. Good for the "task" stage of your project, when someone asks which hand and which simulator.

## Founder radar

### Selling the parachute, not the plane
Today's lesson points at a gap. Everyone is building humanoid policies. Almost nobody sells the safety layer around them, and every company deploying humanoids near people will need one, if only for the insurer.

Seeds:

- *Runtime monitors as a product.* Give us your fallback policy and a simulator model, we return a trained recoverability monitor and a report of where your envelope is thin. The paper's 6-hour training run is a service, not a research project.
- *The black box recorder for robots.* Planes have flight recorders, and every fall investigation in aviation starts with one. A standard log of state, monitor score and handover events for humanoids, plus tooling to replay near misses, would be useful for engineering and for certification.
- *Teach by demonstration for small factories.* Dominique Paul's Dream Machines (talk on 3 November, below) bets that small and medium manufacturers will automate if a worker can just show the robot the task. The interesting question to bring to that talk: what does the robot do when the demonstration doesn't cover the situation? That is today's problem again, in a different shape.

## Outside your bubble

### [An eclipse from 709 BCE that measures the length of a day](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261025073055.htm)
On 17 July 709 BCE, court astronomers in Qufu, capital of the Chinese state of Lu, wrote down that the Sun "was totally eclipsed". It is in the Spring and Autumn Annals and is the earliest explicit, securely dated record of a total solar eclipse. There was one awkward problem: when modern astronomers computed the eclipse path, it seemed the eclipse should not have been total in Qufu at all.

A team led by Hisashi Hayakawa at Nagoya University, with colleagues from Reading, Lockheed Martin and the National Astronomical Observatory of Japan, found the bug. It was not in the astronomy. Earlier calculations had put ancient Qufu about 8 kilometres from where archaeology says it actually was. Fix the location, and the record fits.

Why this matters beyond history: the Earth's spin is slowly slowing down, mostly because of tidal friction from the Moon. You cannot compute where an ancient eclipse was visible without knowing how far the Earth's rotation has drifted since, a quantity called Delta T. Turn that around, and a well-placed eclipse record becomes a measurement. For 709 BCE they get Delta T of 20,264 to 21,204 seconds, roughly five and a half to six hours of accumulated drift. A one-line note from a court official is now a data point for geophysics. A later text describes the Sun as "completely yellow above and below", which might be the corona and would suggest the Sun was coming out of a long quiet period, though the authors are careful to call that uncertain. Published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters.

The lesson travels well: like yesterday's delay paper, the "physics problem" was a misplaced coordinate. Check the harness before you doubt the theory.
