# Domain randomization and human data for humanoids

*2026-10-02*

A third edition for today. It continues the walking series with domain randomization, which the last edition promised, and adds a fresh paper on using human data for the task phase that comes after walking.

## Robot learning

### [lambda-0: a humanoid loco-manipulation VLA pretrained on human data](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00438)
Chongyang Xu, Steven C.H. Hoi and others; arXiv 2610.00438 (posted this week).

The authors release HumanVerse-500, 500 hours of people doing loco-manipulation in open-world settings, recorded with egocentric video plus wearable body and hand motion capture. They train lambda-0 (written λ₀ in the paper), a vision-language-action policy, in three stages: generic pretraining on large egocentric video datasets, whole-body coordination pretraining on HumanVerse-500, then fine-tuning for specific tasks and robot bodies. It does well on the SIMPLER simulation benchmark and four real-world loco-manipulation tasks, and the paper discusses how performance scales with the amount of human data. Code, models and data are promised. Why it matters for the club: once your robot walks, the next problem is task data, and teleoperating a custom humanoid is slow. Human capture (a mocap suit plus a head camera) is something a student team can afford, and this paper is a recipe for turning it into a policy prior. Caveat: the gap between human and robot bodies is still filled in by fine-tuning on the robot itself, so you will still need some robot data.

### [HumanoidTTT: reuse checked motions instead of generating new ones](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00198)
Jingtai Yang, Hao Tang and others; arXiv 2610.00198, [code](https://github.com/AIGeeksGroup/HumanoidTTT).

A smaller, practical idea. When a humanoid generates whole-body motions on the fly, each one has to be checked for safety. HumanoidTTT keeps a library of motions that have already been validated, reuses a whole motion when the robot is in a state it knows is safe to start from, and grows or prunes that library based on feedback while deployed. They report zero unsafe motions accepted and a 16.4x end-to-end speedup over generating each motion from scratch. Idea to take away: caching plus a "known safe starting state" check is cheap engineering that makes learned motion generators usable on hardware.

## Learn

**Domain randomization: making a sim gait survive real hardware**

The previous two lessons covered actions (PD targets) and rewards. A policy trained against one exact simulated robot will exploit that model's exact friction, mass and timing, and then fail on the real robot. Domain randomization (DR) means randomizing those quantities during training, so the policy learns behavior that works across the whole range, and the real robot is hopefully one more sample inside it. What legged_gym, Isaac Lab and Humanoid-Gym typically randomize:

- **Contact.** Ground friction (about 0.3-1.25) and restitution. This is the most important one; slipping feet are the most common way sim gaits fail on hardware.
- **Body.** Base mass offset (plus or minus a few kg, or about 10-20% for small robots), center-of-mass shift of a few cm, and link mass scaling. Custom robots almost never match their CAD, because cables, batteries and fasteners are missing from the model.
- **Actuators.** PD gains (Kp, Kd scaled by about 0.8-1.2), motor strength or torque limits, joint friction and damping, and armature (rotor inertia). For custom actuators, consider an actuator network instead: a small model trained on logged real motor data, as in ANYmal's work from ETH's RSL.
- **Latency.** An action delay of 0-20 ms and an observation delay. Real controllers have communication and compute lag, and a policy that never saw it often oscillates.
- **Observation noise.** Gaussian noise on joint positions, velocities, IMU angular velocity and projected gravity, at sensor-realistic levels.
- **External pushes.** Random velocity kicks to the base every few seconds. This gives push recovery for free and makes the gait more conservative.
- **Initial state.** Random starting joint positions and base orientation, so the policy cannot rely on always starting from a perfect stance.

Practical advice: randomize too little and the policy breaks on hardware; randomize too much and it learns a stiff, cautious gait or fails to learn at all. Start with friction, mass, pushes, noise and latency; if it fails on hardware, measure what is different (log real joint tracking against sim), then widen that one range rather than all of them. A curriculum helps here too: narrow ranges early, wider ones once the robot walks. Finally, note what DR does not fix: a wrong URDF (joint axes, limits, inertias) or a badly modelled actuator. System identification first, randomization around it second.

## Founder radar

- [Tangent Robotics raises a $4.5M pre-seed](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260930237631/en/Tangent-Robotics-Raises-4.5M-in-Pre-seed-Funding-to-Advance-Fine-Motor-Skills-for-Robot-Dexterity), led by Fly Ventures and Toyota Ventures. Idea worth stealing: rather than a whole robot, sell the dexterity layer that every humanoid and arm company is missing. Note that a European fund (Fly Ventures) led it, so European investors are backing deep robotics IP at the pre-seed stage.

It is a reminder that a boring, narrow product that floor workers can operate without programming can reach hundreds of deployments before the humanoid companies ship any.

## Outside your bubble

### [Silent artery plaque in 1 in 13 adults under 30](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/09/260929053541.htm)
The REACT study (published in NEJM) imaged the neck, leg and heart arteries of 16,808 Danish and Spanish adults aged 18-70 with no known heart disease. 57% had atherosclerotic plaque. That included about 1 in 13 of those aged 18-29, rising to 9 in 10 at 60-70. In women, plaque grew quickly after menopause. The broader point: a disease usually seen as a problem of old age starts decades earlier and gives no symptoms, so prevention data from 20-year-olds is now possible to collect. It is the same lesson as sim-to-real: failures that show up late usually started small and early, where nobody was measuring.
