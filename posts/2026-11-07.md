# Predict the step before you take it

*2026-11-07*

The idea behind the cleaner of the two is one you can use on a blind walker today: don't just encode what the robot sees, make it predict what happens next. ## Robot learning lesson: auxiliary prediction for locomotion

**The problem.** Perceptive locomotion means feeding a policy two very different streams: a depth image (or height map) of the terrain ahead, and proprioception (joint angles, velocities, IMU). The usual approach is to encode each stream, concatenate, and let PPO figure out the rest. The only training signal for that fused representation is the reward, which is a weak and noisy signal for "understand how this stair relates to my left foot". The policy often learns to lean on proprioception and treat vision as optional, until the terrain makes vision essential.

**What [STEP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08097) does.** Youhao Lyu, Chenghao Sun, Wenhao Cui and colleagues give the fused representation two extra jobs, trained together with the RL loss in a single stage (no teacher-student split):

- *Fusion with learned queries.* A small set of learned query tokens attends over depth and proprioceptive features and produces a compact policy state. You can think of it as a tiny Perceiver that decides what to keep.
- *Bidirectional masked prediction.* Hide one modality and predict it from the other. Proprioception has to predict what the terrain looks like, and depth has to predict body state. This forces the representation to encode how the two relate, which is exactly what "my foot is about to hit the edge of that step" needs.
- *Action-conditioned forward prediction.* Given the current fused state and the action, predict the next fused state. This is a world-model loss used purely as a regularizer: the policy never plans with it, but its representation now has to know what its own actions do.

**Numbers.** In simulation, STEP beats the fusion alternatives and ablations it was tested against on terrain traversal, and degrades less when depth frames are dropped or delayed. On hardware: 98 of 100 trials across stairs, a 40 cm platform, a 70 cm gap, stepping stones, and walking backward up stairs. No project page or code yet.

**Its sibling paper.** The same group's [parkour paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08056) goes further: human parkour clips are converted into robot references, then terrain-indexed adversarial motion priors teach the skills, and finally the prior's weight is turned down so the policy can find its own hand and knee contacts. 96.34% hybrid success in simulation, then 29 of 30 obstacle trials and 4 of 5 full courses on the real robot with no real-world fine-tuning. The three-stage "imitate, then loosen the leash" schedule is the interesting part.

**Why this matters for your walker.** You're starting blind, on flat ground. Three things to take from this anyway.

1. *Forward prediction is cheap and it works blind.* Add a small head that predicts the next proprioceptive latent (or simply the next joint state) from the current latent and the action, and add its loss to PPO with a small weight. It costs a few lines and a little GPU time, and it gives the encoder a dense signal that doesn't depend on reward shaping. Ablate it: run with and without on the same seeds and compare.
2. *Train for missing data now.* STEP's robustness to dropped and delayed depth frames came from caring about it during training. Your equivalent is IMU dropouts, a stale joint reading, or a late CAN message. Randomly zero or repeat sensor readings in sim before your hardware does it for you.
3. *Plan the interface for vision later.* If the team will add a depth camera after the first walk, design the observation pipeline so a terrain encoder can be bolted on without retraining from scratch. A query-token fusion like STEP's is a clean place to start.

## New papers worth reading

### [HITCH: a VLM that drives a humanoid through a harness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08039)
Qingzhou Lu, Jianyu Chen and colleagues let a vision-language model control a humanoid by picking points in the image, which a "whole-body harness" turns into commands for a low-level controller. Without any task-specific trajectories it reaches 51% on SIMPLE and 42.5% on HumanoidArena; with five trajectories per task, 95% on SIMPLE. Why read it: this is the architecture your project will need in phase two. The walking policy is the low level, and the question is what interface the high level speaks. Points in an image are a surprisingly good answer.

### [Robot evaluation for the price of a coffee](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07441)
Rohun Kshirsagar and Diana Rypkema built a closed-loop physics evaluation harness that runs 200-rollout studies on serverless L4 GPUs for about 3.50 dollars per replication. The surprise result: three action-chunked policies scored zero successes across 780 rollouts, while single-step checkpoints got 2.6 to 11.8%. Not a general verdict on chunking, but a good reminder that offline metrics lie and cheap rollouts are the cure.

### Also new
- [PhySA, arXiv 2611.07679](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07679): uses a VLM as an "aesthetic" reward for humanoid gait, plus a separate physics smoothness term. Cut joint acceleration 26 to 32% on MuJoCo Humanoid, but, to the authors' credit, they report the gain did not carry over to IsaacLab or the G1. Negative results like that are rare and useful.
- [AffectiveVLA, arXiv 2611.07712](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07712): a small intent model on a G1 that blocks VLA actions when it isn't confident the human wants them. GR00T macro-F1 goes from 0.75 to 0.90, at 6.27 ms p95.
- [Spatial-UMI-Dream, arXiv 2611.07229](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07229): a 5B world-action model pretrained on 605 hours of RGB-D handheld-gripper data, predicting future RGB, metric depth and actions. 85% average real-world success.
- [TactileLab, arXiv 2611.07881](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07881): shear-sensitive tactile simulation in IsaacLab for sim-to-real dexterous manipulation, from Nathan Lepora's group.

## Founder radar

### Seed: the fleet is the product, the stack is the business
Serve Robotics reported this week (numbers below) and quietly launched "Serve Physical AI Services", which offers the technology behind its delivery fleet to other robot makers. That's a familiar move: when you've paid to solve autonomy, mapping, teleop and fleet ops for your own robots, the stack can be worth more than the robots. Waymo-style vertical integration on one side, Android-style licensing on the other.

Questions worth an evening:
- What does a humanoid team need that it doesn't want to build? Teleoperation fallback, remote monitoring, over-the-air policy updates, incident logs. Every humanoid startup is rebuilding the same ops layer.
- The 3.50 dollar evaluation paper points at a second gap: evaluation as a service. Upload a checkpoint, get back 200 rollouts across randomized scenes and a report. Labs pay a lot of engineer time for this today.
- And STEP's auxiliary losses are a reminder that small training tricks transfer across robots. A curated, benchmarked library of "things that make locomotion PPO work" would get a lot of stars.

## Outside your bubble

### [Swift, the two-year telescope that lasted twenty-two](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261105224545.htm)
On Wednesday morning, NASA's Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory re-entered the atmosphere over North Sumatra, Indonesia, at 7:39 EST, as confirmed by the US Space Force. NASA expected most of it to burn up, though some parts may have survived.

Swift launched in 2004 with a two-year plan. Its job was gamma-ray bursts, the most violent explosions in the universe, which happen when a massive star collapses or two neutron stars merge, and which fade in seconds to minutes. Swift's trick was speed: spot a burst, swing its telescopes to the spot on its own, and alert observatories around the world within moments so they could look too. Over two decades it was used for far more than that: black holes in active galaxies, flaring stars, supernovae, comets.

Two things worth noticing. First, a "rapid response" design that is simple and autonomous turned out to be useful for problems nobody had planned for. Second, low orbits always decay; satellites are slowly pulled down by the thin upper atmosphere, and an orbit that's fine for twenty years still ends. Robots work the same way. The system you build for your first walk will probably end up doing jobs you haven't imagined yet, so make it simple and well-documented, and let it react on its own.
