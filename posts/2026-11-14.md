# Three and a half minutes to learn to dance

*2026-11-14*

Saturday, which means you might actually have a free GPU this weekend. Good timing, because today's lesson is a paper that trains a humanoid to track 40 motion-capture clips in 3.4 minutes on one RTX 4090. The headline number is fun, but the useful part is how the author *got* there: by treating training time as an equation with three terms and attacking each one separately. That way of thinking will help your walking project more than any single trick. Also today: a plug-in expert that teaches a humanoid new skills without forgetting old ones, a multi-material simulator built for coding agents, the first pure-play humanoid stock in the US, Europe's most valuable robotics startup, an RL infrastructure internship in Zurich, and what 5,000-year-old skulls say about re-reading old data.

## Robot learning lesson: make training fast by splitting the clock

**Where this comes from.** Two weeks ago we covered SuperMimic: instead of PPO, learn a neural network that imitates the simulator and backpropagate the tracking error through it to get direct policy gradients. [BlinkMimic](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16422) (Fuyi Chen, posted 12 November, [code on GitHub](https://github.com/fuyichen0725/blinkmimic)) builds on that and on a follow-up called InstantTrack. In simulation, on a Unitree G1, it trains one policy for all 40 LAFAN1 clips in about 3.4 minutes, roughly 2.2 times faster than InstantTrack re-run on the same GPU, with far fewer clips still failing at the end.

**The equation.** The paper writes total training time as

T = c × (N_fit + N_tail)

- **c** is the wall-clock cost of one iteration (about 120 to 135 ms here).
- **N_fit** is how many iterations it takes until average performance is good enough (normalised return 0.82).
- **N_tail** is how many more iterations it takes until the last few stubborn clips stop failing.

In the final recipe, about 9 seconds go to startup, about 167 seconds to fitting, and about 26 seconds to the tail. Every trick in the paper targets exactly one of these terms, and the paper tells you which.

**Attacking c (cost per iteration).**
- *Capture the whole iteration as a CUDA graph*, including the MuJoCo-Warp physics step and the resets. That means the GPU replays a recorded sequence of kernels instead of Python launching each one. It cut the steady-state step from 127.5 to 118.5 ms, about 7%.
- *Find the batch knee.* Halving the batch from 4096 to 2048 environments needed about 20% more iterations, but each iteration got so much cheaper that the total time dropped. There's a point where smaller batches stop costing iterations and start costing a lot. You want to sit just above it.

**Attacking N_fit (iterations to get good).**
- *Use the Muon optimiser.* Muon orthogonalises each weight matrix's update, which roughly means it takes an equally sized step in every direction instead of letting a few directions dominate. This was by far the biggest single factor: with Adam on the policy instead, training never reached the target in 6,000 iterations.
- *Use a residual policy.* The network outputs a correction to the reference joint targets instead of the full action. Removing this cost 23% more iterations. The reason is that tracking rewards give almost no gradient when you're far from the target, so starting close keeps the gradient useful.
- *Decay the step size based on progress.* The policy step starts at 4x the base rate and falls linearly to 1x once return passes 0.75. Removing it cost 14%.

**Attacking N_tail (the last stubborn failures).** This is the part most people ignore. Every 125 iterations, a separate simulator runs the deployed policy (an exponential moving average of the weights) over the whole motion library *under deployment conditions*, records where in each clip it first fails, and sends 15% of training resets to those moments. In one test, a hard section that had been getting about 1% of resets went from a 19% failure rate to zero once it got 10%. Interestingly, removing it made the average target arrive about 5% *sooner*, but doubled the number of tail failures (18 against 9 across three seeds). It's insurance for the worst cases, not a speed-up.

**The honest caveats.** Everything is in simulation, with one robot, one 40-clip library and three seeds per setting, which the author says can't detect differences below about 15%. Root position drifts about 93 cm per clip because the policy has no global position feedback. And a checkpoint that passes can still fail later; the author calls these "late flips" the main open problem.

**What to take for your humanoid.**
- **Log the three terms for your walking run.** Time per iteration, iterations until the robot walks on average, and iterations until the last command ranges and terrains stop failing. When training feels slow, you'll know *which* kind of slow it is, and you won't waste a day tuning the optimiser when the real issue is a slow reset function.
- **Send resets to where the deployed policy fails.** You don't need SuperMimic for this. In a PPO walking setup, the equivalent is a periodic evaluation of the exported policy (with deployment noise, latency and the same observation filtering as on the robot), then biasing the curriculum toward the commands, terrains or pushes where it falls. This aims your compute at the tail instead of at things the policy already does well.
- **Evaluate the policy you will deploy, not the one you train.** If you use weight averaging, observation noise or action filtering on the robot, the evaluation should too. A pattern across this paper and most sim-to-real failures: the training metric and the hardware behaviour quietly measure different things.
- **Try Muon, carefully.** Its gains here are for a gradient-through-a-model setup. For PPO it's less proven, so try it as an ablation on a short run, not as a default.

The big idea: "training is slow" is not a diagnosis. Split the clock, and each part has its own cure.

## New papers worth reading

- **[PERA: Plug-in Experts for Robotic Actions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16188)** (Hu, Sun, Yu, Huang). Fine-tuning a humanoid policy on a new skill tends to break old skills, and training a separate policy per skill is a maintenance headache. PERA freezes a pretrained mixture-of-experts motion policy and bolts on a small new expert, about 1% of the actor's parameters, with its own routing score. It learned 10 new tasks on a simulated G1, and switching the new expert off restores the original model exactly, so all 126 old motions still run. This matters for your "chapter two": once the robot walks, you'll want to add tasks without risking the walking.
- **[MotionSkill](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16346)** (Niu, Han, Pan, Wang). Take one four-second monocular walking video, extract a timed reference, retarget it to each of ten different bipeds by keeping limb directions but using each robot's own segment lengths, then train a small residual policy per robot in MuJoCo. It reached 98% success on held-out starting states, but only 26% when the upper body was hidden for one second of the video. That second number is the useful warning: the policy is only as good as the reference.
- **[Alloy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16312)** (Gupta, Rohilla, [code](https://github.com/argonrobotics-ai/alloy)). An open-source physics engine built on Newton, MuJoCo Warp and Warp that puts rigid robots, cloth, cables, liquids, sand and soft bodies in one scene and couples the solvers every control step. Two unusual design choices: each simulated world gets a "health grade" so you can tell physics failures from policy failures, and the engine is exposed to coding agents through the Model Context Protocol. In their test, agents that had never seen Alloy installed it and trained walking, cable and pouring policies on one GPU. Keep an eye on it for later manipulation tasks.
- **[Remember What Changes the Odds](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16386)** (Liu, Li, Yao, Ren). Sometimes a robot sees the exact same scene but should act differently, because it's on its second attempt or halfway through a task. This memory uses a critic to keep only events that noticeably change the predicted chance of success: positive jumps become progress notes, negative ones become failure notes that get cleared when the subtask is done. In a ten-subtask diagnostic environment it reached 88% success, against 0% for a 36-step observation window and 1.7% without the failure notes. It's the natural follow-up to yesterday's "is your robot's memory just a clock?" paper: index memory by events, not time.

## Founder radar

### Agile Robots: 8.5 billion dollars, but count the robots

On 12 November, Munich-based [Agile Robots closed a 500 million dollar Series F](https://tech.eu/2026/11/12/softbank-leads-500m-funding-round-in-agile-robots) led by SoftBank, with Nvidia, Hitachi and T.Capital joining, at an 8.5 billion dollar valuation. That makes it Europe's most valuable robotics startup. The money is for US expansion, more production capacity including in Germany, and new markets.

The interesting story is in [Tech Times' read of the numbers](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/330608/20261113/agile-robots-raises-500m-series-f-humanoid-factory-capacity-public-shipment-count-not.htm). Agile's revenue comes from more than 20,000 deployed industrial robot systems: arms, assembly cells and automation for car, electronics and logistics customers. It then added humanoids on top: the full-size Agile ONE (174 cm, 69 kg) and a compact ONE SN running Nvidia's GR00T on a Jetson Thor, with 1,000 to 2,000 units planned for universities and labs in 2027. Its plant near Munich can, it says, build up to 10,000 humanoids a year. But it hasn't published humanoid shipments or orders, Forbes reportedly saw only wheeled units shipping during a visit, and the round came in below the roughly 800 million dollars reported in June.

The seed for you: notice the order of operations. Agile didn't start with a humanoid. It built a boring, profitable automation business, got customers and factories, and then sold the humanoid story to investors who could see real revenue underneath. That's the opposite of most humanoid startups, and it's why it can raise at this size. If you ever start something in robotics, ask: what is the boring product that pays the bills while the exciting one matures? Also notice the research-lab humanoid segment. 1,000 to 2,000 robots for universities is a market, and those buyers need software, training pipelines and teaching material, which is exactly what a team like yours ends up building anyway.

## Outside your bubble

### [5,000-year-old skulls, read again](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261112025540.htm)

In the 1920s, the archaeologist Flinders Petrie dug up the royal cemetery at Abydos, where Egypt's First Dynasty kings were buried about 5,000 years ago. Around the kings' tombs were rows of graves for attendants. Petrie suspected they had been killed to follow their ruler into the afterlife, but he couldn't prove it. He reburied most of the skeletons and kept a selection of skulls, which ended up in the Duckworth Collection at Cambridge.

A century later, Roselyn Campbell at Purdue examined 46 of those skulls with modern forensic methods, published in *Frontiers in Environmental Archaeology*. Eighteen of them, about 39%, have fractures that happened around the time of death, which Petrie had probably taken for ordinary damage. Some also show sharp-force injuries, including a chopping wound near one person's eye. The pattern doesn't look like battle injuries, and these weren't enemy soldiers, which supports the old theory of retainer sacrifice. Campbell is careful about the limits: a fall can't be ruled out for every case, people who were strangled or suffocated leave no marks on bone, and most of the skeletons are still reburied in the sand.

Why it belongs here: the data was sitting in a museum drawer for a hundred years, and the answer was in it the whole time. What changed was the method of looking. Your training logs, failed rollouts and old club recordings are the same kind of drawer. A lot of good research, and a lot of good startups, come from re-reading existing data with a better tool rather than collecting new data. And notice the selection bias too: Petrie kept only the skulls he found interesting, so every conclusion is limited by what someone decided to save. Decide carefully what your robot logs.
