# Teaching a five-tonne loader to do wheelies

*2026-11-17*

Good Tuesday. The ETH Robotic Systems Lab has put out a paper in which a 5-tonne John Deere skid-steer loader tips onto its two front wheels and balances there, drives around, spins in place, crosses 75 cm bumps and then sets itself down gently. A laptop with no GPU runs the controller, and nobody gave it a single demonstration. It sounds like a stunt, and it is one, but under the stunt is the most useful sim-to-real lesson for a custom robot: if you can't model your actuators, learn them. Your humanoid's motors are not hydraulic, but they are also not the ideal torque sources your simulator thinks they are. Also today: a humanoid that learns to wait for its partner, a navigation stack that walks a biped 100 metres from a lobby into the street, two December coding competitions near Zurich, Nvidia's earnings tonight and 245 animals living inside a single rock.

## Robot learning lesson: when the motors lie, learn the motors

**The paper.** [Heavy Metal Acrobatics: Learning Dynamic Control for Skid-Steer Loaders](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18365) (Lorentz, Egli, Jelavic, Hutter and others; ETH RSL, Gravis Robotics, Blue River and Intel, posted 13 November).

**The problem.** A hydraulic machine is close to impossible to simulate from first principles. Oil compresses, hoses flex, valves have delays that change with load, and the joints have play. Model-predictive control can balance the machine once it's up, but it can't handle the parts where contacts change, like the swing-up and the landing. That's the same wall you hit with a home-built humanoid. The rigid-body physics is easy to get right. The actuators are where the simulator lies.

**The recipe.**
- **Measure the rigid body roughly.** They measured link lengths with a tape measure, then found mass and centre of mass by driving the loader onto truck scales. Crude but sufficient. The arm's mass was simply lumped into the body.
- **Learn the actuator, not the physics.** They recorded 1.5 hours of commands (steps, sine waves, ramps, zeros, up to full amplitude) plus 20 minutes of balancing, logged the wheel speeds, and trained a small network (3 layers of 32 units) that maps 1.8 seconds of command history to the wheel velocity 20 ms ahead. A PD controller turns that velocity into torque inside RaiSim. The long history is what captures the delay and hysteresis. This is the same trick as the famous ANYmal actuator network, now applied to a construction machine.
- **Randomise what you measured badly.** Arm pose, centre of mass, inertia, random pushes and Gaussian sensor noise were randomised each episode. One finding stands out: **policies trained without sensor noise did not transfer reliably.**
- **Keep the observation tiny.** IMU pitch, pitch rate, average left and right wheel speed, the previous action and a short history. That's all.
- **Split the job into skills.** Four PPO policies (swing up, balance, balance while tracking a velocity, land softly), switched by hand-written rules. The reward curricula slowly increased penalties, for example for scraping the rear underside, so the agent first learned to get up and only later learned to get up cleanly.
- **Don't let the agent cheat on safety gear.** The real machine had a wheelie bar and bucket wheels. Both were deliberately left out of the collision model, so the policy could never learn to lean on them.

**The numbers.**
- **Swing-up:** 11 of 11 and 12 of 12 successes at the two arm angles inside the training range. At 34 degrees, just outside the range, 11 of 14, with the bucket hitting the ground in the failures.
- **Balancing:** oscillations of plus or minus 1.6 degrees, as good as the MPC baseline. On hardware, the robot balanced at a pitch about 2 degrees different from simulation, which is the kind of offset you only find by testing.
- **Where RL beat MPC:** it tracked wheel speeds up to 4 rad/s while MPC failed beyond 3.5, it crossed 75 cm bumps in both directions when MPC couldn't clear the first, and it landed without bouncing. MPC couldn't swing up or land at all.

**What to take for your walking project.**
- **Spend a day on actuator identification before you spend a week on rewards.** Put one leg on a stand, send step, chirp and random commands to each joint, and log commanded position, measured position, velocity and current. If your simulated joint responds faster or more crisply than the real one, your policy will learn to rely on reflexes the robot doesn't have. A small history-based actuator model, or at least randomised delay, PD gains and friction fitted to that data, is often the difference between a robot that walks and one that shakes.
- **Add sensor noise from day one.** That's cheap and this paper says it's mandatory. Put noise and bias on IMU orientation and angular velocity and on joint velocities. The joint velocities are probably finite-differenced encoder readings on your robot, and they are much noisier than in simulation.
- **Expect a constant offset on hardware.** Their loader balanced 2 degrees off. Your humanoid might stand slightly leaning. Log the mean torso pitch while it stands still and compare it with simulation before you start tuning anything else.
- **Look at the edges of your randomisation ranges.** Swing-up worked perfectly inside the training range and failed 3 times in 14 just outside it. If your measured link masses could be 10% off, randomise by more than 10%.

The big idea: the simulator doesn't have to be right, only right enough in the places the policy depends on. The actuator is almost always one of those places.

## New papers worth reading

- **[When Will You Hand It Over? A Hidden Start Delay Makes Self-Play Handover Robust to Unfamiliar Partners](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18491)** (Khoshnazar, Degany, Qu, Yang, Szanto). A G1 learns to receive a parcel through PPO self-play with a learned giver. It reaches 90 to 95% success with its training partner but only 31% with unfamiliar scripted partners. The reason is silly but instructive. The learned giver always started immediately, so the receiver memorised a single timing, and delaying the partner by one second dropped success from 95% to 6%. The fix is to randomise when the partner starts without telling the receiver. After 300 more updates, success with unseen partners reached 80%, compared with 35 to 41% for an equal-compute control, and high-force contacts fell three to five times. A good lesson for the "teach it tasks" phase: in self-play, your partner is part of the training distribution, and a predictable partner is a narrow distribution.
- **[X-Nav: Real-World Long-Horizon Indoor-to-Outdoor Navigation on a Humanoid Robot](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17598)** (Huang, Lin, Luo and others). A technical report on walking a Lingxi X2 biped about 100 metres from an office lobby, through a door and onto the road, to a shop in the building next door, steered by language and the robot's built-in RGB camera. The pieces are a data engine (open scenes, digital twins of the deployment site, real robot data), a navigation model in two versions (one outputs actions, one outputs goals) and an agent layer that splits instructions into subtasks. The abstract gives no headline success rate, so read it as a systems blueprint rather than a benchmark win. It's a useful map of what sits on top of a walking controller once you have one.

## Founder radar

### Actuator identification as a product

Every team building its own legged robot runs into today's lesson, usually after weeks spent blaming the reward function. ETH learned it on ANYmal in 2019, and the same idea is still being rediscovered on 5-tonne loaders in 2026. Yet there's still no off-the-shelf way to do it. Each lab writes its own test-bench scripts, its own logging and its own little actuator network, then throws it away when the motor changes.

The seed: a small hardware-and-software kit. You clamp the joint into a bench (or hang the leg), and the software sends a standard set of excitation signals over CAN or EtherCAT, logs everything and fits both a parametric model (delay, friction, gain and saturation) and a history-based actuator network. It then exports both straight into Isaac Lab, MuJoCo and Genesis, along with suggested randomisation ranges based on how much your data varied. Add a public library of characterised motors from common suppliers, and you've built the dataset nobody else has. Your club is about to need this on a deadline. Write the scripts once properly, open-source them, and see whether other humanoid teams show up asking for them.

## Outside the bubble: 245 animals in one rock

At 4,000 to 6,000 metres down in the Pacific's Clarion-Clipperton Zone, the seafloor is covered in polymetallic nodules: fist-sized lumps of iron and manganese that grow a few millimetres every million years. A team from MARUM at the University of Bremen cracked open 244 of them and counted the animals living inside their cracks and pores. They found 4,139, mostly nematodes and copepods smaller than a millimetre, or about 17 per nodule. One nodule held 245. The communities inside the nodules differ from those in the surrounding mud, but are similar across sites up to 1,000 km apart. In other words, these are a habitat of their own, not just a hiding place ([ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261114202133.htm), [paper in Scientific Reports](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-76737-w)).

Why this connects back to you: these nodules are the target of deep-sea mining because they are rich in nickel, cobalt and copper, which go into batteries, motor windings and much of the hardware a humanoid is built from. A nodule takes millions of years to form, and a mining collector removes it in seconds. It's worth knowing that the "physical" in physical AI has a supply chain that ends on the ocean floor, and that the debate over whether to mine it is only just getting the biology it needs.
