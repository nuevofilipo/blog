# Steer the walker, don't retrain it

*2026-11-24*

Good Tuesday morning. Your club's roadmap has two big steps: get the robot walking, then get it to do things. Most people assume step two means going back into the training loop with a new reward and hoping the walking survives. Today's main paper suggests a different move: keep the controller you already trained frozen, and learn to *steer* it into behaviours it was never trained on, like hurdling or shouldering a door open.

After that: a paper on what a damaged robot should tell its planner (useful if your custom actuators turn out weaker than the spec sheet), a way to ask a flow-matching policy "are you sure?", Nvidia's CEO coming back after eleven days, John Deere buying another camera company, and leukemia that can start in your teens.

## The big one: steering a frozen humanoid into new tricks

**[Latent Jacobian Steer: Eliciting Contact-Rich Out-of-Distribution Behaviors from Pretrained Humanoid Controllers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27338)** (S K Swaminathan, Shivam Sood, Shreyansh Kansal, Chengyang He, Guillaume Sartoretti, NUS), submitted 20 November.

Setup first. A *behavioural foundation model* (BFM) is a whole-body controller trained on lots of motion that takes a latent command z and turns it into joint actions. Feed it the right z and the robot walks, crouches or waves. The problem is that the z space only covers what was in the training data. Ask for "jump over a hurdle" or "push a door open with your hip" and there's no z for that.

You have two obvious ways to explore, and both are bad on their own:

- **Perturb joint actions directly.** You can push a knee exactly where the task needs it, but you trample the coordination the controller learned. The robot falls over.
- **Perturb the latent z.** The robot stays coordinated, but you have no idea which direction in z produces "foot higher" or "shoulder into door". You're steering a car whose steering wheel isn't labelled.

LJS combines them. For a task described by the physical effect you want (foot clears the bar, door angle increases):

1. Sample joint-action perturbations that would help the task.
2. Map them back into latent space through a **damped local inverse of the policy's Jacobian** (how actions change when z changes). This answers: "which change in z would most nearly produce this action change?"
3. Run those candidate z commands through the **frozen** policy in simulation, keep what achieves the effect, and chain the winners into a sequence of z commands.

So you explore in action space, where the task makes sense, and execute in latent space, where the controller stays upright. The ablations show neither space alone gets there. Across ten seeds, LJS completes more of each task than searching in either space alone, on hurdling, manipulating articulated objects and opening doors through non-prehensile contact. As a bonus, it also steers a goal-conditioned dexterous hand policy through four assembly tasks better than searching in its goal space directly.

Limits to keep in mind: it's a search done in simulation, per task, and the abstract doesn't report real-robot runs. It's not a policy that generalises; it finds a command sequence for one task.

**Why this matters for your club.** Three takeaways for when walking works:

- **Design your walking policy to be steerable.** If your locomotion policy takes a command vector (velocity, heading, base height, maybe a gait phase), you've built a small latent space yourself. A richer command interface now means more behaviours you can unlock later without retraining.
- **The Jacobian trick is cheap to try.** With PyTorch you can get d(action)/d(command) via autograd on your trained policy. Even on a simple velocity-conditioned walker, looking at that Jacobian tells you which command dimensions actually do something and which the policy ignores.
- **"Frozen base, learned driver" is a pattern worth knowing.** It shows up across robotics: keep a stable low-level skill and put the task-specific learning on top. It also means a bad experiment on a new task can't break the walking you spent weeks getting right.

## Robot learning lesson: tell the planner what will happen, not what to do

**[What Must a Model Tell a Planner? Outcome Interfaces for Risk-Aware Planning After Robot Damage](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27939)** (Hassan Iqbal, Krishna Kumar), submitted 20 November.

Here's a scenario that's very real for a custom-built humanoid: one actuator overheats and loses torque, or the feet are slipperier than in sim. Which routes can the robot still manage?

The authors compare four ways a learned model can talk to a planner, using simulated Unitree Go2 and H1 robots with weakened motors, extra payload or low foot friction:

1. A **decision policy** that just says which route to take.
2. The same policy trained with **fixed penalties** for infeasible routes.
3. An **outcome model** predicting whether each route is feasible and how long it takes.
4. A **capability contract**: an outcome model predicting success probability and travel time *per terrain type*, then composing those along a route.

All four get the same data, tuning, risk calibration and uncertainty guard. With the planner limited to picking an empirically infeasible route at most 5% of the time, the outcome models cut declined missions from about 32% to 7-8% on the Go2, and from 57% to 35-36% on the H1. Fixed penalties close some of the gap, but the best penalty changes every time you change the risk target. Bonus result: two low-risk probes let the capability contract rank hazardous terrains about as well as four fresh trials on every hazard.

**The lesson in one sentence:** a model that predicts outcomes is reusable when the requirements change; a model that outputs decisions has the requirements baked in. That's true well beyond damaged robots. For your project, once walking works, think of logging "per-terrain success rate and speed" as a capability table for each policy version. It costs almost nothing, and it's exactly the information you'll want when someone asks "can it do the demo on that carpet?"

## Two more papers worth a skim

- **[Coherent Moment Propagation: Epistemic Uncertainty for Pre-trained Robot Policies](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26855)** (Saavedra-Ruiz, Li, D'Arpino, Degirmenci, Hermans, Ramos), 19 November. Flow-matching policies are everywhere in manipulation now, but they never tell you when they're guessing. The existing fix (BayesDiff) has a nasty flaw the authors prove: its variance shrinks as 1/T with the number of solver steps, so the more carefully you integrate, the more confident it looks. CoMP carries the state-weight cross-covariance through the ODE, Kalman-filter style, and fixes it without retraining. Relevant for phase two, when your robot does tasks and you want it to stop when it's unsure.
- **[RoboColiseum: Co-evolution of Simulation, Evaluation, and Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27042)** (Yin, Huang, Yang et al.), 20 November. A sim benchmark built on replaying recorded robot commands for system identification. Across 22 paired real-sim robot-task configurations, sim scores correlate with real scores at a reported Pearson 0.9686. The idea to steal: replay logged real commands in your sim and compare trajectories. It's the cheapest sim-to-real gap check you'll ever do.

## Founder radar: Deere keeps buying eyes

John Deere has [acquired Orchard AI](https://www.therobotreport.com/how-does-orchard-ai-fit-into-john-deeres-technology-portfolio/), a San Francisco company that mounts AI cameras on vehicles and analyses hundreds of images per tree to estimate yield, count fruit and measure canopy. Price undisclosed. It continues a long run: Blue River (305 million dollars, 2017), Bear Flag Robotics (250 million), Light's IP, SparkAI and GUSS Automation.

The pattern is worth noticing. Deere doesn't need to invent the robot; it has the tractors, dealers and farmer relationships. What it buys is perception and data for one well-defined crop problem. If you're thinking about a robotics startup, "the perception layer that makes an incumbent's existing machines smarter" is a much shorter path to an exit than "a general-purpose robot". Specialty crops (orchards, vineyards) are also where labour is scarcest and the margins per plant are highest, which is why Deere is moving there.

## Outside the bubble: leukemia that starts in your teens

A [Nature study](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261121221122.htm) from Cambridge and UCL (Caroline Watson, Jamie Blundell) reconstructed how acute myeloid leukaemia develops in the years before diagnosis. The trick was a lucky dataset: an ovarian cancer screening trial that took annual blood samples from about 50,000 women for up to 11 years, some as much as 15 years before a diagnosis. The team compared 47 women who later developed AML (average diagnosis age 72) with 46 who didn't, sequenced their blood very deeply, and used mathematical models to date each mutation.

What they found:

- In about two-thirds of cases, **the first cancer-driving mutation appeared before age 20**, more than 50 years before diagnosis.
- Each additional driver mutation roughly doubled or tripled the clone's growth rate. With four drivers, populations could double in weeks rather than years.
- In at least a third of cases, one mutated clone took over the blood years before diagnosis. None of the cancer-free women showed this.
- In about one in five cases, detectable growth began only one to two years before diagnosis, so not every case gives a long warning.

Why it's interesting beyond medicine: it's a control problem. Growth is exponential with a rate you can measure from a few samples, so a small, early slowdown compounds over decades. That's the same reason a small bias in your robot's gait estimator matters a lot over a long walk. The authors are clear that trials are still needed to show that slowing clones actually prevents disease.
