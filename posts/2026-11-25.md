# Bolt on, don't start over

*2026-11-25*

Good Wednesday morning. Yesterday's main paper showed how to steer a frozen humanoid controller into new behaviours by searching over its commands. Today's arXiv batch has a sibling idea: keep the big pretrained controller and train a small adapter on top so it can deal with stairs, boxes and furniture. Same principle, different tool. Don't throw away a policy that already works.

After that: a fix for the jittery actions that real motors hate, a language model that repairs a robot policy by editing 0.0002% of its weights, a benchmark where the best VLA hits 8.3%, two robotics earnings reports with opposite moods, and why the US is building a tenth of the power lines it needs.

## The big one: an adapter for humanoid foundation models

**[Ada-BFM: Adapting Behavior Foundation Models for Humanoid Scene Interaction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31863)** (Furui Xu, Weishuai Zeng, Zirui Wang, ... Jingbo Wang, Weinan Zhang), submitted 24 November.

Quick recap. A *behaviour foundation model* (BFM) is a whole-body humanoid controller pretrained on lots of motion data. It's good at moving like a person in open space: walk, turn, crouch, wave. It's much worse at moving *with* the world: stepping onto a platform, climbing stairs, sitting on a chair, carrying a box. That needs motion that adapts to scene geometry and contact.

The usual fix is to train a separate controller for each interaction task. You end up with a pile of specialists and no shared skills. Ada-BFM keeps the pretrained BFM and adds a **lightweight adapter** that learns interaction-specific adjustments. You train one adapter per interaction *domain* (say, terrain, or objects), not one controller per task.

What the authors report, in simulation and on a real robot:

- A range of scene-interaction behaviours from one shared model per domain.
- Faster learning than training from scratch, because the adapter starts from a controller that already knows how to balance.
- The BFM's general whole-body skills are **largely kept**. That's the important claim. Fine-tuning a whole network on a new task usually erodes what it knew before, and keeping the backbone mostly intact is the point of using an adapter.

Caveats: the abstract doesn't say which humanoid they used or give real-robot numbers, and there's no code link yet. Read the PDF before you trust the details.

**Why this matters for your club.** Put yesterday and today together and you get a clear plan for the next year:

1. **Phase one: a good base walker.** Flat ground, then rough terrain, then pushes. This is the asset everything else is built on.
2. **Phase two: add behaviours without touching the base.** Use either a *driver* that picks commands for the frozen walker (yesterday's idea) or an *adapter* that adds small corrections to its outputs or hidden layers (today's idea). Both protect the walking you worked hard for.
3. **Practical consequence now:** write your policy code so it's easy to freeze the network and attach extra modules, with a clean split between observation encoder, backbone and action head. An afternoon of refactoring now saves a messy rewrite later.

If you want to understand adapters properly, the original idea comes from language models (LoRA and adapter layers). Reading the [LoRA paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09685) takes an evening and makes this whole family of methods easy to follow.

## Robot learning lesson: smooth actions by fixing the critic

**[POISE: Curvature-Aware Critic Regularization for Smooth Actor-Critic Control](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31835)** (Jeong Woon Lee, Namho Kim, Hyoseok Hwang), submitted 24 November.

Here's a problem you will hit on the real robot. The policy looks great in sim, then on hardware the joints buzz. Actions change a lot from one control step to the next, the motors heat up, gears wear, and sometimes the robot shakes itself over. RL policies are known for this high-frequency jitter.

The usual fixes act on the **actor** (the policy): add an action-rate penalty to the reward, filter the outputs, or limit the network's Lipschitz constant. POISE looks somewhere else. In actor-critic methods the actor follows the critic's preferred action. If the critic's landscape is flat and wobbly, its preferred action jumps around as the state changes a little, and the actor learns to jump with it.

So POISE regularises the **critic**. It defines a *greedy shift*, roughly "how far the critic's best action moves between neighbouring states", and penalises it, weighted by how much value you'd lose by keeping the old action. The actor isn't modified.

Results, on six standard control tasks with TD3:

- Smoothness score improved by **5.9 times** on average, against 2.2 times for the strongest baseline.
- Best smoothness on five of six tasks.
- Returns were mostly unchanged. On four tasks the difference wasn't significant, Ant improved 14% and Hopper dropped 11%.

Limits: these are MuJoCo-style benchmarks with TD3, not a humanoid on hardware, and most locomotion pipelines use PPO, which has a different kind of critic (a value function, not a Q-function). So you can't use it as is.

**The takeaway for your club** is still useful: when you see jitter, don't only add reward penalties. Ask where the jitter comes from. If you ever move to off-policy training (FastTD3 and FastSAC-style recipes are getting popular for fast humanoid training), smoothness on the critic side is worth trying. On PPO, log action rate per joint from day one so you can spot jittery joints before you deploy.

## Seed for your brain: a language model as a policy surgeon

**[Sparks of Weight-Space Programming for Robot Foundation Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31715)** (Kyle Coelho, Eugen Solowjow), submitted 24 November.

This one is strange and fun. The authors take pi0.5, a large vision-language-action policy, and fix its recurring failures **without new data and without training**. A language-model agent reads the network's internal activations, finds the pattern that shows up whenever the robot is stuck (for example, gripper hovering above an object and not going down), and then directly writes a few weights so that pattern triggers a corrective movement.

The trick: they reuse a few **near-silent neurons** in the action expert as a switch. Those neurons barely fire normally, so changing them leaves everything else alone. In total they edit about **0.0002% of the parameters**. In simulation this fixed recurring failures, even on a policy already fine-tuned for the task. Reversed or random edits made things worse, which suggests the effect is real and not luck.

It only works when the stuck state is recognisable and a simple correction fixes it. But the idea is worth thinking about:

- **A startup angle:** "policy hotfixes as a service". Companies deploying robot fleets see the same failures over and over. Retraining is slow and expensive. A tool that finds failure signatures in logs and patches a deployed policy without retraining could save a lot of money.
- **A club angle:** even without editing weights, finding the activation pattern for "about to fall" in your walker is a nice side project. If you can detect it reliably, you have an early-warning signal for a recovery controller.

## Quick hits from the arXiv batch

- **[Roboclock](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32233):** 60 manipulation tasks with timing constraints ("pour for three seconds", "wait, then act"). The best trained VLA gets **8.3%** temporal success even on the easy level. Robots still have no sense of time. That's an open research problem and maybe a product gap.
- **[FabricBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31577):** a bimanual cloth benchmark with a contact simulator. The best policy averages 77%, but none tops 36% on long-horizon T-shirt folding. Laundry is still undefeated.
- **[EgoLoAF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32260):** trains humanoid loco-manipulation from egocentric human videos, no motion-capture suits or teleoperation. Useful to know about for the "teach it tasks" phase, because collecting teleop data on a custom humanoid is a lot of work.

## Industry: one robot company gets paid, another gets cut

**Symbotic reported its first profitable year.** Results came out on 23 November ([24/7 Wall St.](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/11/24/walmarts-robot-warehouse-partner-symbotic-just-had-its-first-profitable-year/)). Fiscal Q4 revenue was 777.6 million dollars, just above consensus. For the full year: about 25% revenue growth, 133 million dollars of GAAP net income (after a loss the year before), 596 million of free cash flow, 2 billion in cash and no debt. Backlog is 22.3 billion. The weak spots: next quarter's guidance is a bit below consensus, Walmart is still the anchor customer and a shareholder, and the company still reports material weaknesses in its financial controls. The stock jumped about 12% before the report and barely moved after.

**Kraken Robotics missed and cut guidance.** Q3 revenue was CA$102M, more than triple last year, but below estimates. It cut 2026 revenue guidance to CA$250 to 260M and adjusted EBITDA guidance to CA$53 to 58M, blaming delayed defence awards and weak offshore wind activity. Shares fell about 23% in pre-market trading. First formal 2027 guidance: CA$490 to 540M revenue, which management says excludes large defence wins and is 70 to 75% covered by backlog and existing services ([Investing.com](https://za.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/earnings-call-transcript-kraken-robotics-q3-2026-misses-estimates-shares-sink-93CH-4594848)).

The lesson for a future founder: both companies have real products and growing revenue. What separated their days was **customer concentration and timing**. Symbotic depends on one giant customer that keeps signing. Kraken depends on defence budgets that arrive when they arrive. When you pitch your own company one day, investors will ask exactly this.

## Outside the bubble: the grid is the bottleneck

A Policy Forum piece in *Science* by Madeline Yozwiak, David Victor and Granger Morgan (Carnegie Mellon, UC San Diego) puts a number on a problem that sounds boring but decides a lot ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261123005339.htm)).

- Over the last five years the continental US has added about **500 miles a year** of high-voltage transmission lines.
- To triple interregional capacity by 2050, it needs about **5,000 miles a year**. So it's building at a tenth of the needed pace.
- The authors say the blocker is **not technical**: "The fundamental challenge isn't technical, it's jurisdictional." Approval is split between federal agencies, states, local governments and private companies, and each one can delay a project. Existing utilities can also resist lines that would bring in competing power.

Their proposed fixes: use existing federal powers more actively, upgrade existing lines with better conductors (reconductoring) and HVDC, bury HVDC cables along highways and railways, and set up a national transmission commission.

Why you should care: every AI data centre, every electric vehicle, and one day every fleet of charging humanoids needs power that can move from where it's made to where it's used. It's a good reminder that the hardest part of deploying technology is often permits and politics, not engineering. Europe has its own version of this (Switzerland's grid links with the EU are a long-running political question). If you ever want a startup idea with huge demand and few young founders, grid permitting and planning software is worth a look.
