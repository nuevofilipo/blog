# Plan with a brick, walk with a body

*2026-10-30*

Friday. Here is a trick that controls people have used for decades and that RL people keep rediscovering: before you ask a 29-joint robot to move, ask a brick. A single rigid body with feet attached is easy to reason about, easy to make physically consistent, and surprisingly good at deciding *where* the robot should go and *when* the feet should land. A Caltech paper accepted to Humanoids 2026 turns that idea into a clean two-stage learning recipe and gets a Unitree G1 running at 2.8 m/s with one policy. Also today: why "position control" is not one thing, a London seed round built on simulation only, AWS growing 39% while Amazon's stock drops, a BMW robot learning internship, and T cells that can feel how stiff your tissue is.

## Robot learning lesson: reduced-order references, full-order tracking

**The problem.** Teaching one policy to walk, turn, run backwards, side-step and hop is hard. The usual recipe gives it a velocity command and a pile of reward terms and hopes the right gait emerges for each speed. In practice you get one gait that stretches badly at the edges, or separate policies with a mode switch in between. Motion imitation (track a mocap clip) gives nicer motion, but only for the clips you have, and human clips are not physically consistent for your robot.

**The idea.** [FORGE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37974), from Ghansah, An, Kim and Chung at Caltech, splits the job in two.

- *Stage 1: train a planner on a brick.* The reduced-order model (ROM) treats the robot as a single rigid body (the torso and everything bolted to it) plus feet and hands that move kinematically. The policy's 28 actions are foot and hand accelerations, foot pitch and yaw, ground reaction forces and moments, and two gait-timing knobs: the step period and when in the cycle each foot lifts off. Leg torques are estimated from the contact forces. Joint angles come from inverse kinematics. It's trained with RL on velocity tracking, timing and shaping rewards, plus a torque penalty raised to the sixth power, which hits hard as soon as a plan asks the legs for too much.
- *Stage 2: freeze the planner, train a tracker.* The frozen ROM policy runs online and outputs a whole-body reference: base pose, joint positions, velocities and accelerations. A second policy, built on the BeyondMimic motion-tracking setup, learns to follow that reference on the full robot in simulation. One extra reward term asks it to match the commanded velocity directly, because the brick and the real body don't have identical dynamics.

Both run at 50 Hz. On the G1: forward from minus 2.0 to 2.8 m/s, lateral up to 1 m/s, yaw up to 3 rad/s, one-legged hopping, walking on sand and running on a grassy slope, with one planner and one tracker, no mode switching, and trained on flat ground only.

**Why it works, in plain terms.**

- *The brick enforces physics where it matters.* Momentum, contact forces and gravity are what make a gait feasible or not, and an SRB model captures exactly those. A reference that comes out of it is roughly feasible by construction, unlike a human mocap clip retargeted onto your robot's proportions.
- *The planner chooses timing, not just poses.* The step period and lift-off point are actions. At 0.3 m/s the planner can pick a slow, long-stance walk; at 2.8 m/s it can pick a short-stance run. The tracker never has to invent a new gait; it just has to follow one.
- *The tracker solves a narrower problem.* "Follow this reference" is a much denser learning signal than "walk at 1.2 m/s and figure out how". That's why motion tracking works so well, and FORGE gets that density without needing mocap.

**Caveats.** The ROM treats the limbs as massless, so fast swing legs and arm motion are not modeled; the tracker has to absorb the mismatch. The paper has no baselines, no ablations and no tracking-error numbers. 2.8 m/s was measured by timing a 15-foot run because odometry was too noisy, and 4 m/s running and sustained hopping are simulation only. No code is linked yet.

**What to steal for your humanoid.**

- *For the first walk, don't do this.* A single velocity-tracking PPO policy with good rewards is still the fastest route to a robot that stands and steps. Get that working first.
- *Do borrow the gait-timing idea now.* FORGE makes step period and lift-off phase things the planner chooses per command. Even in a single policy you can make the gait clock's period a function of commanded speed rather than a constant. That's one line of code and often fixes "walks fine at 0.5 m/s, stumbles at 1.2".
- *Build an SRB model of your robot anyway.* Total mass, center of mass and torso inertia from your CAD. You need them to sanity-check the simulation, and an SRB model is a ten-minute tool to ask "can our knee motors even support a 1 m/s gait?" before you burn GPU hours.
- *When tasks start, this is the architecture to remember.* A planner that outputs references and a tracker that follows them is how you add new behaviors without retraining the low level. The same tracker can follow references from a planner, from mocap, or from a VLA later. That's the pattern yesterday's motion-tokens paper and the 20 October post on decoupled control were also pointing at.

## New papers worth reading

### [Same label, different interface](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38104)
Sangwan and Zhang ask a deliberately annoying question: when a paper says "position actions" or "velocity actions", what did the robot actually execute? Between the policy output and the motor sit filtering, scaling, reference construction, feedback gains, timing and effort limits. On three contact-rich assembly tasks (peg insertion, gear meshing, nut threading), ten policies per condition, changing *only* the physical action scale shifted median success by 0.645 to 1.0 across tasks, and removing a moving-average filter helped some tasks and hurt others. Matching the velocity interface's gain to the position interface's helped on two tasks but didn't close the gap. The takeaway is the one from the 17 October lesson, now with numbers: the action scale, gains and filter are part of the policy. When your team compares two configs, write down the whole pathway, not just "PD position targets".

### [METATRON: a promptable whole-body layer for humanoids](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38027)
A frozen generative whole-body policy on a G1 that takes body-part targets and velocity commands as prompts, with skills written as code on top. Same shape as FORGE: one low-level controller, many ways to tell it what to do.

### Also new
- [Catch It All: dynamic catching with a dexterous hand](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39044): sim-to-real catching of unseen objects from a single egocentric camera.
- [What actually matters for pi-0.5 on BEHAVIOR-1K](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37917): a paired evaluation of VLA inference settings across 100 household tasks. Useful once you start caring about task policies.

## Founder radar

### Machenta: one demonstration, zero real data
On 27 October [Machenta](https://imperial.ac.uk/news/articles/2026/imperial-ai-spinout-machenta-raises-40-million-to-make-robots-instant-learners), an Imperial College London spin-out, raised a 40 million dollar seed round led by Creandum and Union Square Ventures, the largest ever seed for a UK robotics startup ([tech.eu](https://tech.eu/2026/10/27/london-robotics-startup-machenta-raises-40m-seed-investment/)). Founders are Ed Johns, professor of robot learning at Imperial, and his PhD student Vitalis Vosylius. The pitch: a foundation model pre-trained entirely on simulated data that learns a new task from a single demonstration, across different robots including humanoids. The team is around a dozen people, moving to a lab in King's Cross.

Meanwhile, Bloomberg reported this week that Physical Intelligence is in talks to raise about a billion dollars at roughly 20 billion, double its last valuation ([summary](https://startupfortune.com/physical-intelligence-is-in-talks-to-double-its-valuation-to-20-billion/)). Talks, not a closed round.

Seeds to think about:

- *Two bets on where robot data comes from.* PI collects huge amounts of real teleoperation data. Machenta bets that simulation alone is enough for pre-training and one human demo is enough for the task. If Machenta is right, the moat moves from data collection to simulation and the learning algorithm.
- *A professor plus their PhD student is a classic founding team.* A body of published work is the due diligence. Creandum's partner said as much: "very few teams in robotics can point to a body of work like this." If you ever want to found in robot learning, the papers you write with the club and your lab are your future pitch deck.
- *"Time to learn a new task" is a product metric.* Johns' line is "intelligence is not so much about what you can already do, it's about how quickly you can learn to do something new." For a customer, the cost of adding task number two is what decides whether a robot is a tool or a science project. That's the same point GXO's CEO made yesterday.

## Outside your bubble

### [Your immune cells can feel how stiff you are](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261026225617.htm)
After an infection, some T cells don't go home. They settle in the skin, gut or lungs as "tissue-resident memory" cells, a local guard that reacts fast if the same pathogen returns. The textbook story is that chemical signals decide which T cells become these residents. A McGill team led by Judith Mandl, publishing in Nature Immunology, adds a physical one: T cells sense the *stiffness* of the tissue around them.

They grew human and mouse T cells in collagen gels tuned to different stiffnesses. The stiffer the gel, the more the cells switched on the gene program of tissue-resident memory. The working theory is that adapting to mechanical force helps them survive long term in tissue.

Caveats: this is in gels, not in living animals, and the sensor itself is unknown. The press release names no protein and gives no effect sizes. The applications floated (autoimmunity, transplant rejection, getting immune cells into dense, stiff tumors) are hopes, not results.

The reason it's here: your cells are doing proprioception. They read the mechanical properties of their environment and change policy accordingly, much like a walking robot reads ground stiffness through its joint torques and adapts its gait. Biology keeps finding that force is information, not just a side effect. Robotics keeps finding the same thing.
