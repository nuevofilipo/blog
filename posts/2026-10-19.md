# One policy, any reward: behavior foundation models for humanoids

*2026-10-19*

Every controller we have discussed so far on this blog has the same shape: write a reward, train a policy, get one skill. Want a different skill? Write a different reward and train again. Behavior foundation models try to break that loop. They train once, without any task reward, and afterwards you can hand them a reward function, a goal pose or a motion clip and get a policy back instantly, with no gradient steps. It sounds like magic and is actually some rather neat linear algebra, and a paper out this morning pushes it further on a Unitree G1. So that is today's Monday lesson. Also: a failure-driven terrain curriculum from FieldAI, a faster way to RL-finetune flow policies, Physical Intelligence opening its models to everyone with an API, a VLA internship in Zurich, a Robotics VC Night in Schlieren this Friday, Tesla's earnings on Wednesday, and a third kind of magnet.

## Robot learning lesson: behavior foundation models, or how to train before you know the task

**The idea in one sentence.** Learn a representation of "where does the robot tend to end up, under which behavior", good enough that *any* reward can be turned into the right behavior by a simple lookup.

**The forward-backward trick.** The core method is called FB, from Touati and Ollivier (2021). Train two networks:

- a *backward* network B(s) that embeds a state into a vector of, say, 256 numbers, and
- a *forward* network F(s, a, z) that predicts, for a policy indexed by the latent z, a summary of the states the robot will visit in the future when starting from (s, a).

They are trained together so that F(s, a, z) dotted with B(s') approximates how often policy z visits s' in the (discounted) future, starting from s and a. In RL language, F and B factorize the *successor measure*. At the same time, a policy network π(s, z) is trained to pick actions that maximize F(s, a, z) · z. None of this needs a reward. It needs transitions, lots of them, and some idea of which behaviors are worth covering.

**Why that is useful.** The value of any policy under any reward r is "sum over future states of how often you visit them, times their reward". With the factorization, that becomes F(s, a, z) · E[r(s') B(s')]. So for a new reward you compute one vector, z_r = average of r(s) B(s) over a batch of states, and then π(s, z_r) is (approximately) the optimal policy for r. Zero-shot. Goal reaching is the special case where the reward is 1 at one state: z = B(goal). Motion tracking is a sequence of goals.

**Why it took until 2025 to work on humanoids.** Pure unsupervised RL on a humanoid finds a large space of behaviors, most of which are flailing on the floor. Meta's Meta Motivo (Tirinzoni et al., ICLR 2025) fixed this by regularizing the latent space towards unlabeled human motion capture: the policy is pushed to produce behaviors that a discriminator cannot tell apart from AMASS clips. That gave a simulated humanoid that could be prompted to track, reach poses or optimize rewards. BFM-Zero (Li, Luo, Zhang, Shi and colleagues, CMU and Meta, ICLR 2026) then brought it to a real Unitree G1, adding the usual sim-to-real toolkit you already know from this blog: domain randomization, reward shaping for safety and history-dependent asymmetric critics.

**Today's step: BeJEPA.** The new paper (link below) argues that root-frame tracking policies forget *where in the world* the robot is. Push one off course and it keeps dancing, two metres to the left. BeJEPA learns one embedding for both body pose and world position and heading, and adds an explicit dynamics predictor next to the "successor" predictor, which allows a bit of online action refinement. On AMASS it reports 61.7% lower global body-position error than BFM-Zero and 58.7% lower than SONIC, and it runs on a G1 that gets up from the ground and walks back to its goal after being shoved.

**What to take for your team.**
- *Not for the first walk.* A well-tuned PPO walking policy is simpler, more debuggable and better at walking. Behavior foundation models trade peak performance on one task for breadth.
- *Remember the pattern for later.* When the project moves to "teach it tasks", you will face the reward-per-task treadmill. A model that covers the space of human-like behaviors once and is then prompted is the alternative to training a new specialist each time, and it fits nicely with the distillation pipeline from yesterday.
- *The data question.* These models need a motion prior (retargeted mocap for your robot's body) and a lot of off-policy transitions. If your team retargets AMASS for a motion-tracking teacher anyway, you are already building half of the required dataset.
- *Prompting by reward is fragile.* z_r is only as good as the states you average over. Rewards defined on states the model rarely visited give strange behavior. In practice, tracking and goal prompts work much better than arbitrary rewards.

Further reading: [Touati and Ollivier, Learning One Representation to Optimize All Rewards](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.07945) (the FB paper, short and readable), [Meta Motivo](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11054), and [BFM-Zero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04131) with its [project page](https://lecar-lab.github.io/BFM-Zero/).

## New papers worth reading

### [BeJEPA: Goal-Conditioned Humanoid Behavior World Model via Unsupervised RL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23127)
Covered in the lesson above. The design choice worth noticing: policy improvement composes one step of physical prediction (a dynamics model) with one step of behavioral summary (the successor predictor), instead of imagining long rollouts. Long imagined rollouts are where world models usually go off the rails, so "predict one step, summarize the rest" is a pattern to remember for any model-based method you try.

### [FCTS: Failure-Guided Curriculum Tree Search Over Generated Terrain for Legged Parkour](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22619)
CMU and FieldAI. Hand-designed terrain curricula cover a few tidy families (stairs, gaps, slopes). Real industrial sites are messier. FCTS records exactly how and where the policy fails (location, termination mode, robot state, nearby geometry), generates new terrain targeted at those failures, and uses Monte Carlo Tree Search to choose between alternative curriculum paths under a fixed compute budget. Evaluated on terrain scanned from real construction sites, success rises from 28.4% to 85.8% on the medium course and from 14.9% to 44.7% on the hard one, and policies transfer to a real quadruped. The cheap version for your team: log *why* each episode terminated and *where*. Before any clever curriculum, that histogram tells you what to train on next.

### [AnchorQ: RL for Policy Improvement via Flow-Reversed Action Anchors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22852)
A recurring problem: you have a good flow-matching or diffusion policy from demonstrations and want RL to make it reliable. A popular approach is to let RL steer the noise that goes into the flow, but the noise space has no behavioral structure, so exploration is blind. AnchorQ runs the flow *backwards* from reference actions to find the noise that produces them, samples a few candidates around those "anchors", and lets a critic pick the best. They report 37% higher real-world success and 97% higher simulated throughput under the same interaction budget. Seed to plant: when exploration is the bottleneck, explore near what already works.

### [Hardware-as-Regularization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22977)
A small, practical one. Instead of cleaning noisy teleoperation data in software, the authors build a passively compliant leader arm, so the demonstrations come out smoother in the first place. Block stacking with the resulting imitation policy goes from 36.7% to 73.3% success. The lesson generalizes to humanoids: data quality is often a mechanical problem before it is a learning problem.

## Founder radar

### [Physical Intelligence opens the Pi API beta](https://theaiinsider.tech/2026/10/14/physical-intelligence-opens-pi-api-beta-for-robot-model-training-and-deployment/)
On 13 October, Physical Intelligence, maker of the π0 family of VLAs, launched an API: upload your robot data, fine-tune their latest model on their compute, deploy through a hosted inference gateway. Access is via a waitlist and pricing is not public. The early users are telling: Almond (dual-arm assembly) trained its robot to deburr spacers in about four days; others include Chef Robotics, Formic and Actor, which works on wheel loaders. The company also admits cloud inference has hit network connectivity problems in some environments, which is exactly the objection everyone has to running a robot's brain in a data centre.

The seed: this is the OpenAI API moment for manipulation, and it changes what a robotics startup is. If the brain is rented, the defensible parts become the body, the data from your specific deployment, and the integration into a customer's process. Almond did not train a foundation model; it found a boring, paid task (deburring) and a fast path to a working policy. Also worth asking: who builds the *on-premise* version, the edge inference box that keeps the arm running when the factory Wi-Fi drops? There is a business in every limitation a platform admits on launch day.

## Outside your bubble

### [The third kind of magnet wins the Europhysics Prize](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261017230035.htm)
For about a century, textbooks had two kinds of collinear magnetic order. In ferromagnets (your fridge magnet), the atomic magnetic moments line up and add up. In antiferromagnets, they alternate and cancel, so the material has no net magnetism and is almost useless as a magnet. In 2019, a group around Libor Šmejkal, Jairo Sinova (Mainz) and Tomas Jungwirth (Prague and Nottingham) proposed a third class, *altermagnets*: the moments still cancel, but because of how the crystal's symmetry rotates the neighbouring atoms, the electrons behave as if the material were a ferromagnet, carrying strongly spin-polarized currents. They classified hundreds of candidate materials by symmetry in 2022, experiments confirmed it in 2024, and the trio has now received the 2026 Europhysics Prize.

Why it is here: spintronics wants the best of both worlds. Ferromagnets are readable but slow and leak stray fields that disturb neighbours; antiferromagnets are fast and stray-field free but nearly impossible to read. Altermagnets might give both, which matters for denser and faster memory. The broader lesson is about where discoveries hide. This one did not come from a new instrument. It came from people re-reading the symmetry rules of materials that had been sitting in databases for decades and noticing that the old two-box classification had a gap. Many fields, robotics included, have a "two categories, everybody knows" assumption somewhere. Finding the third box is often a theory exercise, not a lab one.
