## Robot learning

### [GaitSpan: growing humanoid locomotion from walking to running](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12114)
Kwan-Yee Lin, Zilin Wang, Janelle J. Liu, Stella X. Yu; arXiv 2607.12114 (July 2026, older than a week but directly relevant to your next milestone).

GaitSpan's argument is that once a humanoid can walk, it should not relearn locomotion from scratch to jog or run. It takes a frozen, pretrained walking policy and wraps it in three pieces: rhythm generation (several internal clocks that modulate the frozen walker), stride shaping (a reward based on spring-loaded inverted pendulum dynamics that pushes towards longer, bouncier strides at higher commanded speeds), and a residual policy that learns whatever details the first two miss. One policy then shows a continuous progression from walking to jogging to running as commanded speed rises, transitions on sand and outdoor terrain, and generalization across embodiments. Why it matters for the club: it suggests a staged plan where the first deliverable is a boring, robust walker you freeze and build on, rather than one giant policy trained for every speed. Caveat: residual-on-frozen-policy approaches inherit every flaw of the base walker, so the walker still has to be good.

## Learn

**Reward terms for a first walking policy**

Last time covered the action space: the policy outputs joint position targets, a PD loop turns them into torque. On top of that sits the reward, which for walking is almost always a weighted sum of a few task terms and many small penalties. A typical starting set, as found in legged_gym, Isaac Lab's locomotion tasks and Humanoid-Gym:

- **Command tracking (the main positive terms).** Reward exp(-|v_cmd - v_xy|^2 / sigma) for linear velocity and the same for yaw rate. The exponential keeps the reward bounded and smooth; sigma of about 0.25 is common. These carry the largest weights.
- **Staying alive / not falling.** Either a small per-step alive bonus or a large termination penalty when the base height drops or the torso touches the ground. Too large an alive bonus teaches the robot to stand still.
- **Feet air time and gait shaping.** Reward each foot for staying in the air for a target duration (about 0.3-0.5 s) when it lands, only when a non-zero command is given. Without this, policies often shuffle or vibrate in place. Many humanoid setups add a gait clock: a periodic phase signal fed to the policy, with rewards for each foot being in contact when its phase says stance and in the air when it says swing.
- **Posture.** Penalize base roll and pitch (projected gravity away from straight down), base height deviation and, for humanoids, deviation of hip roll and yaw joints from their defaults so the legs do not splay.
- **Smoothness and effort.** Penalize joint torques, joint accelerations, and the action rate (difference between consecutive actions). The action-rate penalty is the single most useful term for removing the high-frequency jitter that breaks real motors.
- **Safety and limits.** Penalize approaching joint position limits, exceeding soft torque or velocity limits, and foot slip (foot velocity while in contact). Also penalize feet impacting too hard, which protects gearboxes.

Practical advice: start with only tracking, termination, air time, orientation and action rate; get something that walks in sim; then add penalties one at a time and watch each one's contribution in the logs (Isaac Lab and RSL-RL log every term separately). Weights on penalties are usually one to three orders of magnitude smaller than tracking weights. Ramp penalties up with a curriculum, because heavy penalties early stop the policy from discovering how to move at all. Finally, a gait that looks good in sim but uses tiny, fast steps usually means the air-time reward is too weak or the action-rate penalty too strong.

## Outside your bubble

### [A qubit made of superfluid helium](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/09/260930020309.htm)
A University of Surrey team proposes SHOQ, a qubit built from oscillations of superfluid helium-3. Because the fluid carries no electric charge, it should be naturally shielded from the electromagnetic noise that limits today's superconducting qubits, and their calculations predict error rates about 100 times lower. It is a theoretical design, not yet built. The broader lesson carries over to robotics: sometimes the fix for noise is not better filtering but choosing a medium that does not couple to it in the first place. ([phys.org write-up](https://phys.org/news/2026-09-superfluid-qubit-scale-quantum.html))
