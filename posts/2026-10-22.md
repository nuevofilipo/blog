# Look where your feet are going

*2026-10-22*

Once your robot walks on a flat floor, the next thing someone will do is put a step in front of it. A blind policy can handle a surprising amount by feel alone, but stairs, gaps and stepping stones need eyes. The standard way to give a legged robot eyes is an elevation map, and the standard way to feed it to the policy is "all of it". A paper from Marco Hutter's lab at ETH, out this morning, asks the obvious question nobody bothered to ask: why show the policy terrain it will never step on? Their answer trains about three times faster and runs on a real G1. That is today's lesson. Also: a robot that learns from one human video, an MIT professor and the co-creator of SAC raising $150 million to bet against imitation learning, Tesla starting Optimus production, Texas Instruments having a very good quarter, and blood cells from an 80-year-old that came back about 60 years younger.

## Robot learning lesson: perceptive locomotion, and where to look

**What an elevation map is.** A robot-centric 2.5D grid: each cell around the robot stores one number, the terrain height there. Depth cameras or LiDAR produce point clouds, the robot's state estimate places them in the world, and a mapping node fuses them into the grid as the robot moves. ETH's own [elevation_mapping_cupy](https://github.com/leggedrobotics/elevation_mapping_cupy) does this on a GPU and is what ANYmal uses. The map is not an image of the world; it is a guess about the ground, with holes where the cameras never looked and smearing where the state estimate drifted.

**How a policy usually reads it.** The common recipe, from [Miki et al., Learning robust perceptive locomotion for quadrupedal robots in the wild](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.08117), samples heights at a fixed pattern of points around each foot or around the body, a few hundred numbers, and passes them through an encoder into the policy. Two details matter as much as the architecture:

- *Teacher and student.* The teacher in simulation sees perfect heights. The student sees heights with realistic noise, drift and missing patches, plus its own history, and learns when to trust the map and when to ignore it and feel its way. This is the distillation story from the 18 October post, applied to vision.
- *Noise models are the whole game.* If simulated map noise is too kind, the real robot will trust a map that is lying, and step into a ghost stair. If it is too harsh, the policy learns to ignore the map and you are back to a blind robot.

**The problem with "all of it".** A dense map around a humanoid is thousands of cells. Only a handful decide the next step: the patches where the feet are about to land. The policy has to learn two things at once, how to walk and which cells to look at, and the second is hard to learn when the first is still terrible. Attention-based encoders can learn where to look, but they cost compute and time.

**The Watch Your Step trick.** Do the "where to look" part explicitly:

1. A small foothold predictor takes proprioception and the velocity command and guesses where the next footsteps will land.
2. The map is sampled only in small patches around those predicted footholds. The policy sees the terrain it is about to step on, nothing else.
3. The predictor trains alongside the policy and is periodically refit on fresh rollouts, because the gait, and therefore the foothold pattern, changes as training improves.

Results: the same terrain difficulty in about a third of the training iterations, better performance than full-map policies when footholds are sparse (narrow corridors, gaps) and on unseen terrain, and parity with an attention encoder that costs several times as much. It transfers zero-shot to a Unitree G1 on stairs, gaps and step-downs, indoors and out.

**What to take for your team.**
- *Walk blind first.* A good proprioceptive policy is the fallback for every moment the map is wrong. Do not add perception until flat-ground and small-rough-terrain walking is boringly reliable.
- *Build the map noise model before the perceptive policy.* Record your real cameras on your real robot, look at the holes and drift, and reproduce them in sim. That is the part that decides whether you can sim-to-real.
- *Steal the inductive bias.* Even if you never run a foothold predictor, sampling height points around the feet rather than a big grid around the body is a cheap way to tell the policy what matters. Fewer inputs, faster training, less to overfit.
- *Walk upstairs.* This is RSL's work; the authors are a few buildings away. If your team gets stuck on perceptive locomotion, an email asking for twenty minutes is not a strange request.

## New papers worth reading

### [Watch Your Step: Anticipated Perception for Humanoid Locomotion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29612)
Covered in the lesson. Heyrman, Li, Dadiotis and Hutter, ETH Robotic Systems Lab. [Project page](https://leggedrobotics.github.io/watch-your-step). The seed: "predict where it matters, then only look there" is a general pattern. Grasping (look at the contact points), driving (look where the car will be in two seconds), even VLAs (crop to where the gripper is heading). Every time a policy wastes capacity on irrelevant input, there is a small paper waiting.

### [Real2Sem2Real: 3D semantic augmentations from a single human RGB demonstration](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28880)
Film a human doing a task once, with an ordinary camera. The pipeline lifts the scene to 3D, then generates variations of it (different objects, layouts, appearances) and the matching robot trajectories, over 2,000 episodes in about two hours. Policies trained on this synthetic data match or beat those trained on teleoperation. For your stage two (tasks after walking), the bottleneck will be demonstrations, and teleoperating a custom humanoid is miserable. Keep an eye on methods that turn one human video into a dataset.

### [TIDE: Training in Deployment, human-in-the-loop online post-training](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29264)
The robot runs its policy, a human steps in when it is about to fail, and the policy updates online from those interventions, no critic needed. Each of four bimanual tasks finishes its training session in under an hour. It pairs nicely with [SimPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29161), out the same day, which automates the intervening in simulation and lifts a real cable-insertion task from 72% to 96% success. The trend is clear: pretraining gets you close, and a cheap correction loop on the real task gets you the rest.

### [SMR: streaming motion retargeting for humanoids](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29169)
Yesterday's FRDV made offline retargeting fast. SMR does it causally, frame by frame, with a Transformer trained on physics-validated retargeted trajectories, so it can drive a robot from a live human performer. That matters the day your team wants teleoperation by motion capture instead of a joystick.

### [One Policy, Many Styles: where to aggregate preferences for steerable humanoid control](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28925)
A practical question for anyone with many reward terms: should you combine them into one scalar reward early, or keep them separate and combine later in the policy? Early mixing gives a wider range of behaviors with more side effects; late mixing gives more precise control over style. Worth a skim before your reward file grows its twentieth term.

## Founder radar

### [Eka Robotics raises 150 million dollars to bet on simulation over demonstrations](https://techfundingnews.com/eka-robotics-raises-150m-at-1-45b-valuation-led-by-a16z-and-f-prime-for-dexterous-robots/)
Founded by Pulkit Agrawal (MIT) and Tuomas Haarnoja, who co-created Soft Actor-Critic and worked on RL for small humanoids at Google DeepMind. Andreessen Horowitz and F-Prime led a Series A at a 1.45 billion dollar valuation, announced around 20 October. Their model is a Vision-Force-Action model running on a custom gripper packed with force sensors, trained mostly with RL in simulation instead of imitating human demonstrations. The claim: about 1,500 unfamiliar objects per hour against roughly 600 for a human. That figure is the company's own, and there is no named production customer yet.

Why it is interesting: most of the money in robot learning this year went to imitation (teleoperation fleets, human video, egocentric data). Eka is the loud counter-bet: imitation tops out at human speed, RL in simulation does not. You are already on the RL side of that bet for locomotion. The open seeds:

- *Force is underrated.* Vision tells you where things are; force tells you whether the screw is threaded. Cheap, robust force and tactile sensing for hands is still a hardware problem with few good answers.
- *Sim assets for contact-rich tasks.* If RL in sim wins for manipulation, someone has to build the thousands of accurate objects, cables and fasteners to train on. That is a data business disguised as a graphics business.

## Outside your bubble

### [Blood cells from an 80-year-old, turned back about 60 years](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261020085013.htm)
A team led by Oliver Brüstle at the University Hospital of Bonn, with RWTH Aachen, took immature red blood cell precursors from an 80-year-old donor and converted them directly into neural stem cells using transcription factors, skipping the usual detour through pluripotent stem cells. Then they watched the cells' epigenetic clocks, which estimate age from chemical marks on DNA, for more than 100 days. The estimated age fell gradually, and after about 50 days the cells read as under 20. The study is in *Aging Cell*.

The caveats are big. An epigenetic clock is a model of age, not age itself, and "the cells behave like young cells" is not yet backed by much data. Nobody is getting younger from this. What it gives is a lab model: young-ish neurons made from an old person's blood, useful for studying diseases like Alzheimer's.

Why it is here: it is a reminder that "age" in biology turns out to be partly a state you can read out and, in a dish at least, reset. Plenty of things we treat as one-way arrows are really accumulated state. Your robot's drifting state estimate, a codebase's technical debt, a startup's habits. The interesting question in each case is the same as in Bonn: what exactly is the state, how do you measure it, and which parts can be reset without losing what you want to keep?
