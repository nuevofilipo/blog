# Don't trust the loudest gradient

*2026-11-23*

Good Monday morning. Yesterday's post was about success checkers that lie. Today's is about something that lies more quietly: the training signal itself. A new paper on humanoid motion tracking finds that when you train a policy through a learned world model, the rollouts that shout loudest are the ones the simulator least agrees with. The fix is almost embarrassingly simple, and it works.

After that: a quadruped that carries nearly its own weight because it estimates disturbances instead of hoping domain randomisation covered them, a humanoid that gets told only where to end up and when, an image generator repurposed as a robot policy, Atlas going to a chip fab, a European robot consolidation deal, and a chemistry rule that Einstein quietly broke.

## The big one: trust what repeats

**[Trust What Repeats: Capped World-Model Policy Gradients for Humanoid Motion Tracking](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27648)** (Xuzhe Dang, Stefan Edelkamp), submitted 20 November.

Some quick background. One fast way to train a motion tracker is to learn a differentiable world model and then backpropagate the imagined return straight through it into the policy. You get first-order gradients instead of the noisy score-function estimates of PPO, so you need far fewer samples. The catch is that the world model is only an approximation, and its gradients can be confidently wrong.

The authors measured exactly how wrong, on a Unitree G1 tracking human motion:

- **A tiny minority dominates.** The top 0.5% of imagined rollouts by gradient norm carry 22% of the total gradient norm.
- **Those rollouts don't replicate.** Neither a second imagined rollout from the same state nor the real simulator agrees with them.
- **The bigger the gradient, the bigger the lie.** When they nudged actions along the model's gradient and checked in the simulator, rollouts below the median gradient norm delivered 94% of the predicted improvement. At 3 to 10 times the median, 48%. Above 30 times the median, 1%.

So the policy spends a fifth of its learning effort chasing improvements that exist only inside the world model. The fix, on top of the SuperMimic baseline, has three parts:

1. **Cap each rollout's action-gradient norm at 10 times the batch median.** That's it. Loud gradients get turned down to a volume where they're mostly honest.
2. **Start imagined rollouts more often where gradients are large**, so the cap removes less useful signal overall.
3. **Reset episodes where the world model and critic predict the worst return**, so training time goes to the hard cases.

The results, on 223 hours of training motion and 353 test clips from performers never seen in training: normalised return goes from 0.953 to 0.967, the worst 1% of clips improve from 0.65 to 0.76, and clips that keep falling drop from 15 to 18 down to 7 to 10. It reaches the baseline's final score in 40% of the iterations. On LAFAN1 it scores 0.935 against 0.906. All of this is in simulation; the abstract reports no real-robot run.

**Why this matters for your walking phase.** You will probably start with plain PPO in Isaac or MuJoCo, not world-model gradients. The lesson still carries over:

- **Log gradient norms per batch, not just the loss.** A handful of samples with huge gradients is a symptom, whether it comes from a world model, a reward spike when the robot does something weird, or a physics glitch that launches a foot into orbit.
- **Clip relative to the median, not to a fixed number.** A fixed max-norm needs retuning whenever the reward scale changes. "Ten times the batch median" adapts on its own.
- **Ask "does this repeat?" before you believe it.** Replay the suspicious episode with a different seed. If the big reward or the big gradient vanishes, it was noise or an exploit, and the policy shouldn't learn from it.

Combined with yesterday's post, the rule is: test the measuring instrument, and distrust the readings that look too good.

## Robot learning lesson: estimate the disturbance, don't just randomise over it

**[PACE: Policy Augmentation and Compensation With Explicit Disturbance Estimation for Robust Locomotion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27061)** (Han, Wang, Jia, Chen, Jia, Guo, Yu), submitted 20 November.

The standard sim-to-real recipe is domain randomisation: randomise mass, friction, motor strength and pushes during training and hope reality lands inside the range. It works, but it has two costs. Tracking gets sloppier, because the policy hedges against every possible world at once. And it falls apart as soon as reality leaves the sampled range.

PACE does something older and more control-theoretic. It lumps payload, centre-of-mass shifts, worn actuators and external pushes into a single joint-space disturbance torque, and estimates it online with a nonlinear observer that doesn't need joint accelerations. That estimate is used twice: directly as a feedforward torque correction, and as an extra input to the policy so it can plan around it. Contact forces are also estimated from joint and IMU data, so no foot force sensors are needed.

The numbers: across eleven simulated dynamics conditions, velocity-tracking error drops by 18% to 57% against strong baselines. The real-robot result is the eye-catcher. Trained only on flat ground with at most 3 kg of added mass, the quadruped carried 11.5 kg, about its own mass, and handled 16 cm step-downs it had never seen and five outdoor terrains.

**For your humanoid.** A custom-built robot is exactly where this helps. Your actuators won't match their datasheets, your mass model will be off, and every hardware revision will shift the centre of mass. An observer that tells the policy "there's an extra 2 Nm on the left knee right now" is often cheaper than widening the randomisation ranges until the gait gets timid. It's also a good debugging tool: plot the estimated disturbance per joint on the real robot, and you'll see where your model is wrong.

## Two more papers worth a skim

- **[S-BFM: A Behavioral Foundation Model for Humanoid Control from Sparse Spatiotemporal Goals](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27552)** (submitted 20 November). Instead of a dense reference trajectory, you give the humanoid a target pose, a position and an arrival time, and it works out the motion in between. Reach success goes from 43.5% to 74.6%, and final position error from 1.67 m to 0.26 m. It was deployed on a full-size AGIBOT A3. This is roughly the interface your task phase will want after walking: "be there, in that posture, in three seconds".
- **[GenPA: Image Generators as Efficient Robot Policies](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27446)** (submitted 20 November). Take a pretrained image-generation backbone, add a 19M-parameter action head, keep the image-editing objective as an auxiliary loss, and never actually generate an image at test time. It scores 99.1% on LIBERO and 83.8% on LIBERO-Plus, at 20.9 ms per action chunk on one H100, 1.7 to 3.4 times faster than the VLA and world-action-model baselines. Their ablations find that predicting the current depth map is a simple, effective auxiliary target.

## Seeds for ideas

- **GenPA's quiet point is that the generator is just a pretrained visual prior.** The trend is to treat every big pretrained model (video, image, language) as a source of features for control, rather than building a robot model from scratch. A small-team idea: the cheapest good backbone for a specific robot form factor, distilled and fast enough for an onboard Jetson.
- **Training-signal hygiene as a product.** Today's paper and yesterday's both point at the same gap. Nobody sells a "lint for RL runs" that flags gradient outliers, reward exploits that don't replicate, and checker cases that pass for the wrong reasons. Every robot learning team builds a worse version of this internally.
- **Atlas is going to a chip fab.** [Boston Dynamics announced](https://www.therobotreport.com/boston-dynamics-atlas-humanoid-lands-first-non-automotive-trial/) that GlobalFoundries will be the first non-automotive customer to trial Atlas, starting early 2027. The tasks aren't specified yet. The interesting bit is the path: GlobalFoundries already runs Spot, whose two robots in Singapore covered nearly 900 km and did more than 90,000 sub-fab inspections at a 99% mission success rate. The first humanoid customers are customers who already trust the quadruped. A startup lesson: land with the simple robot, expand with the hard one.

## Industry: Europe's mobile robots get bought

Rockwell Automation has [agreed to buy AGILOX](https://www.therobotreport.com/rockwell-automation-acquiring-mobile-robot-maker-agilox/), the Austrian maker of pallet-lifting autonomous mobile robots. AGILOX was founded in 2017, has more than 2,500 robots deployed and over 250 employees, and Carlyle says revenue has quadrupled since its 2021 investment. Price undisclosed, closing expected in Rockwell's fiscal Q2 2027. Rockwell says it more than doubles its mobile robot business, adding a European customer base to the OTTO Motors line it got with Clearpath in 2023 for 609 million dollars.

The founder takeaway: a European robotics company that picked a narrow, boring job (moving pallets in tight spaces), sold it at scale and added robots-as-a-service ended up as a strategic acquisition. Not every exit needs a humanoid.

## Outside the bubble: Einstein breaks a triple bond

Every chemistry student learns that a triple bond is one sigma bond plus two pi bonds, and that those are different things. A team at Brown University led by Lai-Sheng Wang has [shown experimentally that this can fail](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261120075602.htm) (published in Science). In a carbon-bismuth molecular ion, CBi minus, they found one pi bond and two mixed sigma-pi bonds.

The reason is relativity. Bismuth's nucleus is so heavy that its inner electrons move at a meaningful fraction of the speed of light. That strengthens spin-orbit coupling, which mixes orbitals that are kept cleanly apart in lighter atoms. Theorists predicted this in the 1970s. The team cooled the ions to near absolute zero and used photoelectron spectroscopy, firing lasers at them and measuring how tightly each electron was held. In Wang's words, the sigma/pi boundary is "now sort of smeared".

Why care: bismuth is a candidate low-toxicity replacement for lead in solar cells and shows up in quantum materials, so the textbook picture was going to fail just where it's needed. And it's a nice cousin of today's main story. A model that works perfectly in its normal range (light atoms, small gradients) can be confidently wrong at the extremes, and you only find out if you measure there.
