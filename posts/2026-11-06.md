# Teach the legs to guess the next step

## Robot learning lesson: make the policy predict, not just react

**The problem.** A blind walking policy only needs proprioception: joint angles, velocities, IMU. Add a depth camera and things get messy. Most perceptive locomotion stacks either shove a height map into the network and hope, or train a teacher with perfect terrain info and distill it into a student that sees noisy depth. Either way, nothing tells the network *which* parts of the terrain matter for *this* body state. A step two meters ahead doesn't matter. The edge under your swing foot matters a lot.

**What [STEP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08097) does.** Youhao Lyu, Chenghao Sun and colleagues train perceptive humanoid walking in one stage (no teacher-student), with two auxiliary jobs bolted onto the RL policy:

- *Fuse with learned queries.* A small set of learned query tokens attends over depth and proprioception and produces a compact "policy state". Think of it as the network deciding what to look at.
- *Masked cross-prediction.* Randomly hide one modality and make the visible one predict the hidden one's latent features. Depth has to explain what the body is feeling; the body has to explain what depth is seeing. This forces the representation to encode the terrain-body relationship instead of two separate stories.
- *Predict the next state from the action.* An action-conditioned predictor maps current state plus action to the next fused state, with an exponential-moving-average target (the same trick BYOL and JEPA-style methods use to avoid collapse). That is a tiny world model living inside the policy's representation.

**Numbers.** On a 23-DoF Noetix E1 with onboard sensing: 98 of 100 hardware trials across stairs, a 40 cm platform, a 70 cm gap, stepping stones, and stairs walked *backwards* with no rear view of the terrain. That last one is the tell: the robot can only do it if its internal state remembers what it saw and predicts how it moves. In simulation STEP also degrades more gracefully under depth dropout and input delay than the fusion baselines.

**Caveats.** No code or project page yet, and no affiliations on the abstract page. The abstract compares to "evaluated fusion alternatives", so check in the paper which baselines they picked before believing the margins.

**The companion paper.** The same group also posted [Terrain-Indexed Motion Priors for humanoid parkour](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08056) on the same robot: progressive imitation with motion priors indexed by terrain type, 96.34% success in simulation, 29 of 30 real obstacle trials, zero real-world fine-tuning. Read STEP for the representation idea, this one for how they keep the gait human-looking on hard terrain.

**Why this matters for your walker.** You're starting blind, on flat ground, which is right. But the auxiliary-loss idea is free to borrow now:

1. *Add an action-conditioned prediction head today.* Even with proprioception only, predicting the next latent state from the current one plus the action costs a few lines and gives the encoder a reason to model dynamics. It tends to help most exactly where you'll hurt: sensor delay and noise on new hardware.
2. *Test with delay injected.* STEP measures robustness under input delay. Do the same in your sim before the first real run: add 1, 2, 3 control steps of latency and see where your policy falls over. Your real robot's bus and drivers will add some, and you want to know the cliff in advance.
3. *Plan the sensor slot.* When the team adds a camera later, the cleanest path is a policy that already has a fusion slot and an auxiliary loss, rather than retraining from scratch with a new input.

## The useful negative result: VLMs think jerky is pretty

[PhySA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07679) (Yunwan Fu, Sicheng He, Jiageng Mao, Yue Wang, CoRL 2026 workshop) tried something many teams have idly wished for: have a vision-language model watch rendered rollouts and reward gaits that *look* good, instead of hand-tuning twenty smoothness penalties.

The finding: across four VLMs, clips with *higher* joint acceleration got *higher* aesthetic scores, with a mean correlation of +0.66. VLMs apparently read jerky, energetic motion as lively. So the authors split the channels: a sparse, asynchronous VLM score for "looks good", and a separate, simulator-driven correction that adjusts the physical penalty weights by gait phase. On MuJoCo Humanoid-v5 that cut joint acceleration by 26 to 32% versus baselines, at the cost of shorter episodes. And then the honest line: the smoothness gain does not transfer to IsaacLab or the Unitree G1.

Takeaway for your team: when someone suggests "just let a VLM judge the gait", point them here. Smoothness lives in torques and accelerations you can measure directly. Penalize those, and use video only to catch things numbers miss, like a weird posture.

## New papers worth reading

### [HITCH: a VLM drives a humanoid through a whole-body harness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08039)
Instead of having a VLM output joint targets, Lu, Liu, Wang and colleagues give it a "harness": a whole-body controller with a small set of high-level handles, which the fine-tuned VLM operates. Without task-specific trajectories it completes 51% of SIMPLE and 42.5% of HumanoidArena episodes; with just five trajectories per SIMPLE task it jumps to 95%. It carried a box and walked on a real G1. This is the shape your project will probably take after walking works: a learned low-level controller with a clean command interface, and something smarter on top.

### [Spatial-UMI-Dream: a 5B world-action model from 605 hours of RGB-D](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07229)
Pretrains on handheld UMI gripper data with metric depth, and gets 79.1% in-domain and 72.2% under combined distribution shifts on RoboTwin-UMI, plus 85% across six real tasks. Good background reading for the Robot Learning Paper Club's world model edition on 26 November.

### Also new
- [LARBRID, arXiv 2611.07275](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07275): RL fine-tuning of pretrained diffusion or flow policies by treating the input noise as the action space. Reports 2 to 3 times gains on locomotion and manipulation tasks.
- [CRISP, arXiv 2611.07268](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07268): layered nonlinear MPC that treats torso, arms and legs as coupled subsystems for dual-arm work, running online on a G1. The model-based counterpoint to everything above.
- [AffectiveVLA, arXiv 2611.07712](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07712): handshakes, fist bumps and hugs on a G1, with an intent gate that lifts GR00T's macro-F1 from 0.75 to 0.90.

## Founder radar

### Seed: evaluation is the cheap moat
Two small papers today point at the same gap. [Kshirsagar and Rypkema](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07441) built a physics-based manipulation evaluation harness on serverless cloud GPUs: about 3.50 dollars and 38 minutes per full replication, eight parallel workers. With it they found that three action-chunked policies scored zero genuine successes in 780 rollouts, while single-step policies scored 2.6 to 11.8%. That's a result most small labs would never have the budget to discover. And [Kinematic Hypothesis Context](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07411) shows that a video codec conditioned on the robot's own joint state saves 23.9% bitrate, and at 6 kbps nearly doubles OpenVLA-OFT's success (22.8% to 43.1%) when the policy runs remotely.

Questions worth an evening:
- "Eval as a service" for robot policies: upload a checkpoint, get a few thousand physics rollouts and a report for a few dollars. Who sells this to the hundreds of student and startup teams who currently evaluate with ten real trials and a vibe?
- If big VLAs run in the cloud and the robot streams video up, the uplink is the bottleneck. A robot-aware codec (it knows where its own arm is, so it doesn't need to send it) is a narrow, technical, very sellable piece of infrastructure.
- PhySA says VLMs are bad gait judges. Is there a product in a *good* one: a learned critic trained on human ratings of real robot gaits, sold to every humanoid company that spends weeks tuning reward weights?

## Outside your bubble

### [Why your left brain runs your right hand](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261103003146.htm)
Every vertebrate is wired crosswise: the left hemisphere handles the right side of the body and vice versa. The usual textbook answer is a shrug, or a just-so story about some advantage of crossing. In a new paper in Frontiers in Neuroanatomy, Martin Wessendorf (University of Minnesota) and Daniel Wang propose a less flattering answer: there was no advantage. The crossing started more than 500 million years ago in the visual system of early fish, where the optic nerves cross and each side of the midbrain maps the opposite visual field. Every later circuit, for touch, pain, movement, and eventually the corticospinal tract that gives primates their fine finger control, was built on top of that layout instead of rewiring it. In their words, "a legacy of evolution building new brain circuits on an ancient foundation."

It's a hypothesis, not a proof. The evidence is that every living vertebrate shares the crossed visual layout and that several crossed pathways show up even in sharks and rays. The proposed test is to look for the optic chiasm in fossil skulls of jawless fish, which hasn't been done yet.

Why it's worth five minutes: it's the biological version of legacy code. Nature didn't pick the best architecture; it picked the first one that worked and then never refactored. Something to remember the day your team's quick first walking stack becomes the thing every later skill is built on. Choose your interfaces like they'll last 500 million years.
