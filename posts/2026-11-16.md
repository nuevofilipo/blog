# One recipe, many legs

*2026-11-16*

Good Monday. Here's a problem that sounds like bookkeeping but can quietly ruin your first walking policy: your custom humanoid's hips are not shaped like a Unitree G1's. Every open-source locomotion recipe you'll copy (the rewards, the action scales, the PD gains, the penalties) was tuned on someone else's joint layout. A paper from Friday measures exactly what goes wrong when you reuse a recipe on a robot with tilted hip axes, and gives a fix that's cheap enough to try this week. A student team building its own robot is pretty much who this was written for. Also today: a 27B reasoning model that learns to drive real robot arms entirely in simulation, a dog robot that knows when to stop trusting its eyes, the gearbox maker that just raised its profit forecast by 20%, and crows that make decisions in a quarter of a second.

## Robot learning lesson: give the policy a body it understands

**The paper.** [Reusing Locomotion Training Recipes Across Humanoids via Body-Aligned Virtual Joints](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18003) (Choi, Kim, Seo, Choi, Kim, Lee, posted 13 November).

**The problem.** A walking policy outputs joint targets in the robot's native coordinates. On a G1, the hip axes are nearly lined up with the body: yaw is yaw, roll is roll, pitch is pitch. On many other humanoids they aren't. On the PND Adam, the first hip-pitch motor is tilted 35 degrees, with opposite signs on each leg. When the policy moves that joint, it gets a bit of pitch, a bit of roll and a bit of yaw all at once. The policy has to learn this mixing by itself, and the reward terms, which were written with clean axes in mind, now penalise the wrong things. The paper also tests a third robot: a custom platform with diagonally mounted hips. That one should sound familiar.

**The fix.** Put a thin translation layer between the policy and the motors.
- **Pick a standard body convention.** The hip is yaw, roll and pitch around the body axes, applied in that order. The knee gets one angle, zero when standing, positive when bending. The ankle is roll, then pitch.
- **Compute the mapping from the URDF.** Attach virtual frames to the pelvis, thigh, shank and foot, calibrate them to the body frame in the standing pose, and read the virtual angles off the relative orientations. Going back to native targets means matching orientations, and a Jacobian converts velocities and gains. Nothing gets hand-tuned.
- **Train on a "body-aligned" twin of your robot**, meaning the same masses, inertias and limits but with the leg axes replaced by the standard ones. Apply the mapping only at deployment. Surprisingly, this beat applying the mapping inside the training loop, which was unstable for 15% of iterations on Adam.

**The numbers.**
- **Feet pointing the right way.** On Adam, the average angle between the feet and the walking direction was 4.8 degrees with native joints and 14.2 degrees with a task-space baseline. With virtual joints it was 0.5 degrees.
- **Tracking on the custom robot.** The task-space baseline had a velocity error of 0.477 m/s, so it barely moved when told to walk. Virtual joints got 0.029 m/s.
- **On real hardware (G1).** Heading drift fell from 5.3 to 0.9 degrees per second. The native-joint policy stopped after 5.1 metres. Every virtual-joint run reached the end of the 13.8-metre walkway, so the only limit was the size of the room.
- **Cost.** About 0.2 ms per step on a Jetson Orin NX, inside a 20 ms control loop.

**What to take for your walking project.**
- **Check your leg geometry first.** Open the URDF and see how far each hip and ankle axis is from the body axes. If it's close to G1 (about 10 degrees off), the paper found little difference in simulation, so don't bother. If you have tilted or diagonal hips, this is probably one of the easiest wins you'll get.
- **Measure what velocity error hides.** Log the foot-progression angle (are the toes pointing out?) and the phase error between legs (is the gait symmetric?). In this paper those exposed problems that tracking error alone missed.
- **Watch heading drift on hardware.** Track the IMU yaw rate when the robot is told to walk straight. The sim-to-sim tables didn't show the drift difference. The real robot did.
- **Recipes become portable.** If your policy works in a standard body space, you can borrow rewards and hyperparameters from G1 papers with much less retuning. That matters when your team has limited GPU hours and lots of ideas.

Caveats from the authors: legs only, velocity tracking only, three seeds per setup, and hardware tests on just one robot. Treat it as a strong hint, not a law.

The big idea: before blaming the reward function, check whether the policy is speaking the robot's language or the robot is speaking the policy's.

## New papers worth reading

- **[Training Reasoning Models in Simulation to Natively Use Real Robots](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18562)** (Tang, Zhang, Guo and others, Song Han's group). The authors take an open-weight 27-billion-parameter reasoning model and train it with reinforcement learning, only in simulation and with no demonstrations, to look at a scene, think in a chain of thought and output the next end-effector pose. On LIBERO, success goes from 1.6% to 74.4%. On a real dual-arm robot it goes from 0% to 60%. The most interesting number: on LIBERO-Pro, given a new task in a familiar scene, pi0.5 succeeds 0.75% of the time and this model 57.4%. Policies trained from demonstrations memorise. Policies that reason and learn from RL seem to generalise. It isn't fast enough for walking, but for the "now teach it tasks" phase, it's a serious alternative to collecting thousands of teleoperated demos.
- **[PROOF: Perceptive Robustness via Out-of-Distribution Fallback for Legged Locomotion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18305)** (Wang, Zhu, Lu, Xu, Wang, CoRL 2026). A teacher-student setup where the student estimates terrain features from depth along with how uncertain it is about them, and ignores vision when the input looks strange (occlusion, glare, reflective floors), falling back on proprioception. On a Go2 with a RealSense camera, it does parkour across gaps and beams with good vision and still walks steadily when the camera is mostly covered. It's for later, once your robot walks blind and you add a camera, but "know when your sensor is lying" is a design principle worth keeping in mind from the start.

## Founder radar

### The compiler between robots and recipes

Today's paper is one small piece of a bigger gap. Every humanoid has its own URDF, joint conventions, motor limits, gains and quirks. Every training recipe assumes one specific robot. Moving a policy or recipe from one robot to another is still manual work: someone reads both URDFs, changes signs, rescales actions and retunes rewards until it walks.

The seed: a "robot description compiler". You give it a URDF and your actuator specs, and it outputs a body-aligned training model, the virtual-joint mapping, sensible default action scales and gains, and a checklist of sim-to-real problems (diagonal axes, joint limits that will fire during walking, ankles that will saturate). Together with yesterday's idea of a drop-in safety layer, that's a real product for the dozens of new humanoid teams, both startups and university clubs, who all make the same mistakes in the same order. Nvidia sells the simulator and Unitree sells the robot, but nobody yet sells the translation between them. You're on a team that's about to need exactly this. Build it for the club, open-source it, and see who opens issues.

## Outside the bubble: crows decide twice as fast as you

Researchers at the University of Tübingen trained two carrion crows on a classic neuroscience task: watch a cloud of moving dots on a touchscreen and peck left or right depending on which way most of them are moving. The crows answered in about 235 milliseconds on average, roughly twice as fast as humans and monkeys on the same kind of task, and were close to 100% accurate when the motion was clear. Neurons in their equivalent of the prefrontal cortex started telling the directions apart about 70 milliseconds after the dots appeared, and their activity rose until it reached a threshold. That's the same "gather evidence until you're sure" pattern seen in primates, running on a brain with no layered cortex at all. The authors suspect flight is the reason: if you dodge branches at speed, slow decisions get you killed. ([ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261111005850.htm), [paper in Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aed6459)).

Why it matters beyond birds: it shows that the same decision algorithm can run on very different hardware and be much faster when the body demands it. The idea of separating the algorithm from the hardware it runs on is also the main idea of today's lesson.
