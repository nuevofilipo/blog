# Walk until the battery dies, not the knees

*2026-11-18*

Good morning. Here's a question nobody asks while the robot still can't stand: once it walks, what makes it stop? You'd guess the battery. On a lot of legged robots the answer is a single knee motor that gets too hot and trips a thermal shutdown while the pack is still half full. A paper from yesterday shows that you can fix this with a reward term and no new sensors. That's today's main thread. After that: Atlas on scooter wheels, a policy that learns how actions change rather than the actions themselves, an honest audit of a learned simulator, and what Nvidia's numbers say about robotics.

## The big one: moving the limit from the motors to the battery

**[From Temperature-Limited to Battery-Limited Legged Locomotion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22231)** (Ségas, Opra, Ghafari, Stewart), submitted 17 November.

The setup is a Unitree Go2 doing something deliberately boring: walking for a long time. Under sustained walking, heat piles up in a few joints. The authors carry a 5 kg payload at 1 m/s with a standard learned policy, and the robot hits thermal shutdown after 19 minutes and 1.1 km. The battery had plenty left.

Here's what they did, step by step:

1. **A thermal model you can fit in an afternoon.** They built a 13-node lumped RC network: think of resistors and capacitors standing in for heat flow between the motors, housings and body. They calibrated it on just two runs of one robot against measured electrical power. It predicts all 12 motor temperatures with a mean error under 1 °C, across different robots of the same model, gaits, payloads and speeds.
2. **The insight that makes RL possible.** Motors take minutes to heat up, which is far longer than any credit-assignment horizon PPO can handle. So penalising the actual temperature would give the policy almost no usable signal. But under a steady gait, the temperature each motor eventually settles at depends only on the gait-averaged squared torque and joint speed. You can compute that within one gait cycle.
3. **The reward.** Penalise the hottest predicted steady-state temperature. The policy never sees a thermometer. It just learns to spread the work across the joints and stop overloading the one that would cook first.

The results are big. With the same payload and speed, the new policy ran for 174 minutes and 10 km until the battery was empty, with its hottest motor 14 °C below the cutoff in a 26 °C room. On an unloaded factory circuit with stairs and ramps, the baseline overheated after 25 laps (6.2 km), while the new policy did 47 laps (11.2 km) on one battery, with 22 °C to spare.

**Why this is your problem soon.** A custom-built humanoid is worse off thermally than a Go2. The knees and hips carry the whole body's weight and spend much of each step at high torque. Custom actuators often have less thermal margin, because nobody has had time to characterise them. The likely failure isn't dramatic: the robot walks beautifully for four minutes in the demo, then a knee driver derates and the gait falls apart. Three cheap things to do now:

- **Log winding or driver temperature on every real-robot run,** starting with the first standing tests. It costs nothing, and it's the dataset this paper's method needs.
- **Add a torque-squared penalty per joint, not just a global one.** A plain sum of squared torques lets the policy dump the load on whichever joint is cheapest for the optimiser. The real issue is the worst joint, so penalise the maximum or weight the knees more.
- **Remember that a standing robot heats up too.** Quiet standing with bent knees is a constant high torque. If your idle pose keeps the knees deeply flexed, you are heating them for free.

The big idea: in long-horizon problems, find a quantity that responds quickly and predicts the slow one, then put that in the reward. That trick works far beyond motor heat.

## Atlas swaps its feet for scooter wheels

On 9 November, Boston Dynamics showed an Atlas with **off-the-shelf scooter rims bolted to its ankle actuators** via a single custom adapter, the only new part in the build ([The Robot Report](https://www.therobotreport.com/?p=590172), [Interesting Engineering](https://interestingengineering.com/ai-robotics/boston-dynamics-atlas-wheeled-robot)). A whole-body RL policy trained in simulation takes velocity commands and proprioception as input. It rolls on flat ground, switches to stepping over obstacles, and does sideways dashes and even a 360-degree jump. Before jumping, it rolls its lower body into position, which nobody scripted.

Boston Dynamics is clear that this isn't a product. It's a testbed for how sim-trained control holds up when actuators run close to their limits, and the lessons feed back into the regular Atlas. There are two takeaways for you. First, the same RL recipe gave the robot a new way to move just from a changed body. That's the real promise of learned control: you change the body, retrain, and keep going. Second, "actuators close to their limits" is exactly the regime the thermal paper above is about. Every serious team is now looking at the motor, not just the policy.

## New papers worth reading

- **[ODE Policy: Robot Learning via Action Dynamics, Not Actions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01729)** (Jia, Li, Zuo and others). Most policies output an action at every control step and hope the sequence turns out smooth. This one learns a neural ODE, a vector field that says how the action should change, then integrates each new action from the previous one, so the motion is continuous by design. A small "diversity head" randomises the field's direction to capture multi-modal behaviour. Against 14 baselines, after one epoch of imitation learning, it gets 5.9x the success rate of flow matching and generates actions 10.8x faster. It's also the best of the tested methods on 14 real tasks, including humanoid ones. In RL, it speeds up humanoid motion tracking and smooths the actions. For the walking phase, smooth actions mean lower torque peaks, and the section above explains why that matters.
- **[QTOP: Scaling Humanoid Motion Tracking with Quantile Critics](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20791)** (Peschl, Kianfar, Dijkman). The claim: humanoid motion tracking needs so much compute because training on many different motions makes them interfere with each other, much like multi-task RL. Their fix is a distributional critic (quantiles rather than a single value) that estimates advantage per state and reference pair. That gives up to 10% better tracking than on-policy and off-policy baselines, in simulation and on real robots. They also fit a scaling law and find that, at moderate GPU budgets, dataset size barely matters. That's encouraging for a club: more compute on the motions you already have is worth more than chasing a bigger mocap library.
- **[Auditing a Learned Simulator for Legged Robots: The Robotic World Model on ANYmal D](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23118)** (Singh). A one-author audit of the Robotic World Model, a learned dynamics model for ANYmal that came out of ETH's legged-robotics group. The author retrained 101 simulators and evaluated 200 pruned copies, on CPU only. The most useful finding: **one-step prediction error is a bad guide.** Pruning 30% of the weights kept 90% of one-step skill but only 39% at a 2-second horizon. All 18 fine-tuned copies predicted better than the released model, yet 13 of them produced policies that failed. The ensemble's uncertainty ranks errors well (Spearman 0.90), but it is badly overconfident: it covers 4.6% of outcomes at one sigma, against 68% for a calibrated estimate. As a bonus, the paper lists nine of the author's own claims that were withdrawn. That's rare and worth copying. Limits: one robot, one gait, simulated data only.

## Founder radar

### A test suite for learned simulators

The RWM audit points to a gap. Learned world models are becoming the way people train robots: Nvidia keeps expanding Cosmos, and every lab has its own dynamics model. But the only thing most people check is one-step prediction error, and that audit shows it can point the wrong way. "Does a policy trained inside this model work in reality?" is the only metric that matters, and almost nobody measures it in a standard way.

The seed: an open, framework-agnostic evaluation harness for robot world models. You plug in a learned simulator. The harness runs multi-horizon rollout error, uncertainty calibration and drift, and, most importantly, trains a set of reference policies inside the model and scores them in a trusted physics simulator or against logged real data. The output is a report card a reviewer, an investor or your own team can trust. The business could follow the path of LLM evaluation companies: an open-source benchmark that becomes the standard, then paid hosted evaluation for companies that need to prove their world model works before betting a fleet on it. A first version only needs MuJoCo, a few reference robots and this paper's methodology.

## Outside the bubble: the crash site has a new face

In 2022, NASA's DART spacecraft (580 kg, 6.1 km/s) slammed into Dimorphos, a 151-metre moonlet circling the 780-metre asteroid Didymos. It shortened the moonlet's orbit by about 33 minutes, more than anyone expected. It was the first time humans had measurably changed the orbit of a natural body in the solar system. Now ESA's Hera has arrived, after two years in transit and about 197 million km from Earth, to do the "crash site investigation" ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261116023351.htm)).

The first close-up shows the side of Didymos that DART never saw: an equatorial ridge, boulders, craters and possible grooves. The interesting hypothesis is that some debris from hitting the small moon crossed the roughly 1 km gap and resurfaced the big asteroid. Like deleting one file and corrupting a neighbour's, one impact may have marked two bodies. Detailed images of both asteroids are due on 25 November.

Why it's worth a minute: planetary defence is one of the few areas where humanity ran a real experiment on a problem it had only ever simulated. Then it sent a second spacecraft just to check how far the simulation was off. That's the same discipline as the sim-to-real logs and the world-model audit above, at a scale of 200 million kilometres.
