# Train with the lag you will have

*2026-10-26*

Monday. Your body is still on summer time, so here is a lesson about latency to keep it company. A small paper from last Friday asks a question that sounds too boring to matter: what happens to a learned humanoid controller when its commands arrive 40 milliseconds late? The answer is that most policies fall over, and the fix costs one line of config. That is today's lesson, because when your team's robot takes its first steps, the gap between "works in sim" and "works on the floor" will very likely include a delay nobody measured. Also: a bf16 trap that silently throws away most of your fine-tuning, a boxing humanoid trained in seven minutes, NVIDIA's data engine for GR00T, a big earnings week, and a liquid that stops cavities without a drill.

## Robot learning lesson: delay is a dynamics parameter

**The setup.** In simulation, the policy sees the state, picks an action, and the action is applied on the very next physics step. On a real robot, there is a chain: encoders get read, data crosses a bus (CAN, EtherCAT), the policy runs on a compute board, targets go back out over the bus, and the motor driver's own control loop finally acts. Every link adds a few milliseconds. Add them up and you easily get 10 to 40 ms between "the robot was in this state" and "the motor reacted to it".

**Why it bites.** A walking policy is a feedback controller. Feedback that arrives late is not just slower, it can be *wrong*: by the time the correction lands, the body has already moved on, and the correction pushes in a direction that no longer helps. Control engineers call the amount of delay a loop can tolerate before going unstable the *delay margin*. Learned policies have one too, they just never tell you what it is.

**What [Li and Sun](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32277) measured.** They trained motion-tracking policies for a 24-joint humanoid on five motions taken from monocular video, then tested them in a separate CPU MuJoCo harness, checked against the training model:

- *With the usual domain randomization (no delay)*, every policy finished at least 26 of 30 episodes. Looks great.
- *Add a constant 40 ms command delay*, and average survival drops to 15% (range 0 to 45%). The measured delay margin was only 20 to 30 ms.
- *Randomize delay between 0 and 50 ms during training*, and survival at 40 ms jumps to 95%, with the margin widening to 50 to 70 ms.
- *The control experiment*: train the original policy for the same extra iterations without delay. No improvement. So the gain is the randomization, not just more training.

It held across all five motions with three seeds each, and for a policy trained in PhysX. The cost: tracking got slightly looser (0.02 to 0.06 rad on four motions), and the most dynamic motion became less robust to other perturbations. Nothing is free.

**The detail I like most.** Their first sim-to-sim gap turned out not to be physics at all. It was the harness: a missing floor and a wrong timestep. That is the most common kind of sim-to-real bug, and it is worth a sticky note on your monitor.

**Three things to do before your robot walks.**

- *Measure the loop.* Command a step change to one joint and timestamp when the encoder sees it move. Do it with the full stack running, not the motor alone. That number, plus a margin, is your randomization range. The paper's point is that the 0 to 20 ms ranges common in locomotion configs may sit inside the margin the policy already had, which means they are not teaching it anything.
- *Randomize delay per episode, and maybe per step.* A constant delay sampled each episode is the cheap version. Jitter (delay that varies step to step) is closer to a real bus, and worth trying once the basic version works.
- *Build a second harness before you trust the first.* Running the policy in a different simulator, with its own model loading and timing, is what caught their floor and timestep bugs. If your policy only works in the code that trained it, you have not tested it yet.

This connects to yesterday's teleop post: there, latency was a budget you shrink. Here, it is a disturbance you train the policy to survive. On a real robot you will need both.

## New papers worth reading

### [Your VLA fine-tune may be throwing away 80% of its updates](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31859)
A one-author paper (Shubham Singh) with the most useful kind of finding: a bug hiding in defaults. If you fine-tune with trainable weights stored in bfloat16 and no fp32 master copy, any AdamW update smaller than about 1/512 of the weight gets rounded to nothing. The author finds this default or documented recipe in 9 of 12 open VLA codebases, including LeRobot's SmolVLA recipe and its π0 and π0.5 guides. In the LIBERO runs, at least 79% of updates to bf16 weights were erased. Keeping an fp32 copy took SmolVLA on LIBERO-Object from 4.3% to 49.3% success at a 10x lower learning rate, and π0.5 from 5.1% to 80.9%. At the default rate π0.5 still went from 89.0% to 97.7%. The fix is about one line. If your club ever fine-tunes a VLA for the task stage, check this first. The general lesson: when a model "doesn't learn", look at the numerics before you look at the data.

### [HoloBoxing: a new skill in seven minutes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32909)
Ma, Zhang, Li, Bao, Liu, Wang. Instead of training a boxing policy from scratch, they keep a pretrained whole-body controller frozen and train a small planner on top that asks for future body-point targets. A learned decoder turns a 32-dimensional action into a window of targets, the planner is first initialized by imitation, then refined with RL. The extra training is about seven minutes on one GPU, though that excludes pretraining the controller, data preparation and hyperparameter search, so read the headline with that in mind. The idea is what matters for your stage two: once walking works, keep it frozen and learn tasks *on top* of it, in a small action space, instead of retraining the whole body each time. Simulation only so far.

### [FLORA: the labelling machine behind GR00T](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31779)
Seventeen authors including Yuke Zhu and Jim Fan, so this is NVIDIA's GEAR group. Their claim is that robot foundation models need three kinds of labels: outcome rewards (did the task succeed?), process rewards (how far along is it?) for RL post-training, and timestamped captions for filtering and balancing pretraining data. FLORA produces all three, labels 200,000 hours of data per day on 512 GB300 GPUs, and they say it is about 125 times cheaper than a frontier model on their internal benchmarks. They plan to open-source it, no link yet. Read it next to yesterday's Progress Probes paper: both say that "how far along is the task" is becoming a basic signal, for monitoring and for training.

## Founder radar

### The tooling-audit business
Put today's two quieter papers side by side. One found a precision bug in the defaults of most open VLA codebases. The other found that the gap between two simulators was a missing floor and a wrong timestep. Neither needed a new model. Both needed someone to sit down and check the pipeline everybody copies.

Seeds:

- *A "lint" for robot learning configs.* Static checks that flag bf16 weights without a master copy, delay randomization narrower than measured latency, mismatched control and physics timesteps, missing contact on the ground plane. Every lab would run it, and it is the kind of tool a student team can actually build.
- *Sim-to-sim as a service.* Take a policy checkpoint and a robot description, replay it in two or three simulators with independent loaders, and report where they disagree. Today everyone builds this ad hoc, if at all.
- *Labelling is infrastructure now.* FLORA is NVIDIA saying in public that the data engine is as important as the model. Expect startups to sell reward and progress labelling for robot data the way Scale sold image labelling. The ones that win will probably own a niche (one kind of robot, one kind of task) instead of competing with NVIDIA on general captions.

## Outside your bubble

### [Painting cavities instead of drilling them](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261025222339.htm)
Tooth decay in small children is one of the most common chronic diseases in the world, and the standard fix is an unpleasant one: drilling, filling, and for very young kids often sedation or general anaesthesia. A Phase III trial published in JAMA Pediatrics tested something much simpler. A dentist paints a liquid, 38% silver diamine fluoride, onto the decayed spot with a tiny sponge. It takes seconds. The silver kills the bacteria behind the decay, the fluoride helps harden what is left of the tooth.

The trial, led by Margherita Fontana at the University of Michigan with NYU, Iowa and Indiana, enrolled 830 children under six with severe decay, recruited through dental clinics, paediatric practices and Head Start programmes. With a fresh coat every six months, decay stopped in more than half of the affected baby teeth. The manufacturer plans to use the results for an FDA application.

The twist that makes this interesting: the liquid is not new. The FDA approved it in 2014 for tooth sensitivity, and dentists have used it off-label on cavities for years. What was missing was a large, rigorous trial in the setting where it matters. A lot of medicine works like this: the breakthrough is not a new molecule but the expensive, slow work of proving an old one does what people suspected. The catches are real too. It stains the decayed part permanently black, which matters on front teeth, it stops decay rather than rebuilding the tooth, and the results are for young children, not adults.

There is a robotics parallel if you want one: delay randomization is also not new, and today's paper is mostly the careful measurement that tells you how wide the range should be. Unglamorous evidence is underrated in every field.
