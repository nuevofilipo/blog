# Let the student drive: distilling many teachers into one policy

*2026-10-18*

Almost every impressive humanoid controller from the last two years has the same secret ingredient, and it is not a bigger network. It is distillation: train one or more teachers that are allowed to cheat, then train a student that is not. Yesterday's box-hugging G1 did it. The tennis humanoid did it. Most "one policy for everything" whole-body controllers do it. Once your team's robot walks, distillation is how you will add tasks without throwing the gait away, so it deserves a proper Sunday lesson. Also today: a paper that applies the same trick to VLAs, a hand that learns from a few minutes of play, human video without retargeting, a robot that limps smarter, a Zurich internship in humanoid motion generation, a rough week for a robot stock, and the uranium cubes that show how far Heisenberg really was from a working reactor.

## Robot learning lesson: distillation, and why the student must drive

**Why distill at all?** RL is easiest when the agent sees everything. A teacher policy in simulation can observe the true friction, the exact terrain height map, the object's pose, the base velocity without drift. It learns fast and well. The real robot sees none of that. It has joint encoders, an IMU and maybe a camera. Distillation is the bridge: a student with only realistic observations (plus a history of them) learns to imitate the teacher's actions.

**The naive version fails, and in a predictable way.** The obvious approach is behavior cloning: roll out the teacher, record (observation, action) pairs, regress the student onto them. It works on the training trajectories and then falls over within seconds on its own. The reason is compounding error. The student makes a small mistake, ends up in a state the teacher never visited, has never seen a label for that state, makes a bigger mistake, and drifts off the data. For a biped, "a state the teacher never visited" usually means "halfway to the floor".

**The fix: let the student drive.** This is DAgger (Ross, Gordon and Bagnell, 2011), and its core idea fits in one sentence: **roll out the student, but label every state with what the teacher would have done.** Now the dataset contains exactly the states the student actually reaches, including its own mistakes, each with a correction attached. In massively parallel simulation the recipe is almost embarrassingly simple:

- Run 4096 environments with the *student* choosing actions.
- At every step, also query the teacher on the same state (with its privileged observations) and store its action.
- Minimize the difference between student and teacher actions (MSE, or KL for stochastic policies) on that freshly collected batch.
- Repeat. No reward, no value function, no PPO.

In practice, people often start with a few iterations where the teacher drives (pure behavior cloning, to get the student roughly sensible), then switch to the student driving. RMA, the ETH ANYmal teacher-student papers and most humanoid trackers use some version of this.

**What the student needs that the teacher does not.**
- *History.* The teacher reads friction directly. The student has to infer it from how its last few commands played out. Give it 5 to 50 steps of observation and action history, or a recurrent network. Without history, a student cannot match a teacher that sees hidden state, no matter how long you train.
- *The same noise and delays as the real robot.* Corrupt the student's observations in simulation exactly as reality will. The teacher can stay clean.
- *Capacity.* The student's job is harder, so it is often the bigger network, not the smaller one.

**The many-teachers version.** This is where it becomes a task strategy. Instead of one teacher, train several specialists: one walks, one gets up from the floor, one carries a box, one tracks dance clips. Each is easier to train alone, with its own reward and its own curriculum, than a single policy asked to do everything at once. Then distill them all into one student, with each environment labelled by the right teacher for its current task. You get one network you can deploy, and you never had to make five reward functions coexist inside one PPO run.

**Pitfalls your team will meet.**
- *The teacher cheats in ways the student cannot copy.* If the teacher steps precisely onto a hidden stair it can see and the student cannot, the student learns a blurry average. Limit what the teacher sees to things the student could at least infer.
- *Students inherit teacher bugs.* A teacher with a jittery ankle produces a student with a jittery ankle. Fix the teacher first.
- *Fine-tune after distilling.* A short PPO phase on the student (with a small learning rate, often with the distillation loss still present) recovers performance lost in imitation and fixes behavior the teacher never demonstrated.
- *Check the gap.* Always report teacher reward and student reward on the same evaluation suite. A gap above roughly 10 to 15 percent usually means the student is missing information, not training time.

**How it fits your roadmap.** Walking first: probably plain PPO with an asymmetric critic is enough, so no teacher needed. Then tasks: train a task teacher with privileged object information, distill into a student that sees only what the robot's sensors give. Then many tasks: one teacher per skill, one student for the robot. The infrastructure you build for the second step carries you through the third.

Further reading: [Ross, Gordon and Bagnell, DAgger](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686), [Kumar et al., RMA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.04034), and the `Distillation` runner and `StudentTeacher` modules in [rsl_rl](https://github.com/leggedrobotics/rsl_rl), which Isaac Lab uses and which implement the recipe above in a few hundred lines.

## New papers worth reading

### [Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation for Flow-Based VLAs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21522)
Today's lesson, applied to manipulation foundation models. The authors (Ni, Liu, Li and Fang) start with several domain-specialist VLAs and want one student that does all of them. The twist is that a flow-based VLA does not output an action in one go. It integrates a velocity field over several steps, so the student can drift off the teacher's distribution in two places: in the states it visits, and in the intermediate steps of its own action generation. Their fix is DAgger at both levels. The student acts in the environment, and the frozen teachers supply velocity targets along the student's own flow trajectories. Starting from joint supervised fine-tuning, this adds 22.6 points on LIBERO-40, 33.4 on CALVIN and 22.6 on Meta-World MT50, and beats both teacher-driven fine-tuning and matching only final actions. The bigger gains on longer CALVIN chains are the compounding-error story again: the longer the horizon, the more it pays to train on your own mistakes.

### [Dexterity from Supervised Play](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22340)
From a team including Jitendra Malik. The pitch: no task demonstrations, no sim-to-real, almost no human time. A human plays with objects through teleoperation for a few minutes. A behavior-cloned "play policy" then runs on its own for hours, poking and turning things, and that unsupervised interaction trains a world model. At test time, the same play policy proposes candidate actions and MPC picks the best one under the world model. Finally, a fast goal-conditioned policy is distilled from self-play inside the learned model. On real in-hand reorientation and pushing, it beats hindsight behavior cloning by 69.6% in success rate and matches RL while using 490 times less interaction data. The idea worth stealing: a mediocre policy is still an excellent *data collector*, as long as what it collects is diverse.

### [EgoJEPA: Beyond Retargeting for Dexterous Manipulation from Egocentric Videos](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20904)
A third angle on this week's big theme, human video as robot data. Instead of retargeting human hand poses to robot joints with hand-written geometry (which needs recalibrating per robot and passes hand-tracking errors straight into control), EgoJEPA learns latent actions defined by their *effect*: what visual change does this hand motion cause? A latent world model is trained on that, and each robot gets only a small action adapter. On five real tasks across three robots, it reaches 74% out-of-domain success versus 65% for retargeting, and cuts cross-embodiment compute from 743 to 393 hours. Put it next to KODA from yesterday: both teams conclude that the human data should be stored in a robot-agnostic form, and only translated to a specific body at the last moment.

### [Persistent Profile-Guided Reset-Free Recovery after Irreversible Leg Damage](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20838)
A small single-author paper with a good question. When a legged robot breaks a leg, it must find a new gait by trying some out, but each trial changes its state, there is no reset, and a gait that looks fine for five seconds can degrade over a minute. The method predicts how each gait will perform over *repeated* execution and is deliberately pessimistic when uncertain. Across 108 simulated damage scenarios it cuts low-quality failures by about 28 percentage points against an Intelligent Trial and Error baseline. Simulation only, so file it as an idea. The transferable lesson is about evaluation: short rollouts flatter policies. When your team compares walking checkpoints, run them for minutes, not seconds.

## Founder radar

### [Serve Robotics drops about 26% in a week](https://fool.com/coverage/market-movers/2026/10/17/why-serve-robotics-stock-plunged-this-week)
The sidewalk-delivery company's shares fell roughly 25.7% between 9 and 16 October, to 3.49 dollars, in a market that was also weak (the Nasdaq-100 lost 4.8% over the same stretch). The triggers were an analyst at Northland widening the 2026 loss forecast to 3.22 dollars per share, and a new equity registration that signals more shares, and more dilution, to come.

Hold that next to the private market. According to a [Financial Times trend summary via Stepmark](https://stepmark.ai/2026/10/16/weekly-newsletter-october-16th-2026/), PitchBook puts robotics and physical-AI venture funding at about 48 billion dollars so far this year. Private investors are paying for the promise of robot brains. Public investors, looking at a company that has to deploy robots at scale today, are asking how much cash each robot burns. Both can be rational at once.

The seed for you: the two markets reward different things, and your company will meet both eventually. The question to ask of any robotics idea early is "what is the margin per robot-hour, and what has to be true for it to be positive?" Startups that sell outcomes (units built, deliveries made) need that number to work long before their models are perfect. The ones selling tooling, data or software to robot makers can grow with the hype and leave the unit economics to their customers.

## Outside your bubble

### [The uranium cubes that exposed Nazi Germany's nuclear failure](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261015073218.htm)
In 1945 Werner Heisenberg's team built its last experimental reactor, the B8 pile, in a cave under a church in Haigerloch: a lattice of natural uranium cubes hanging in heavy water. Heisenberg later claimed it was close, needing only a bit more uranium. A team led by Timothy Koeth has now analysed two surviving cubes and the archival record, and published in PNAS Nexus. Their verdict: B8 never came near criticality. It would have needed about twice the uranium and more than twice the heavy water, and Germany's heavy water supply, mostly from the Vemork plant in Norway, was nowhere near enough. The graphite alternative was dead too, because German graphite carried boron impurities that swallow neutrons.

Why it is here: it is a story about how a brilliant team misjudged the gap to a working system, and it was not the theory that killed them. It was supply chains and material purity, details that look boring next to the physics. That pattern shows up everywhere, including in humanoids: the papers are about algorithms, but projects die on actuators, cables and the one sensor that drifts. It is also a reminder to distrust a researcher's own account of how close they were. Heisenberg was not lying, exactly. He simply had every reason to believe the optimistic version. So do founders, and so do you on the evening before a demo.
