# Let the flow guess, let the model check

*2026-11-01*

Sunday, so let's do a lesson that isn't about walking but about the thing right after it: making a robot that already walks do something useful while dragging the world behind it. Today's paper comes from Jemin Hwangbo's lab at KAIST (the RaiSim people) and has a quadruped tow a 25 kg wagon through tight outdoor courses. The trick is a division of labor worth stealing: a generative model proposes plans quickly, and a learned dynamics model plus a sampling optimizer checks and fixes them. Also today: a paper showing that flow policies are secretly almost Gaussian, a paper where more planning made things worse, a 40 million dollar round for lab robots, a correction on AMD's earnings date, a robotics founders' workshop and the ETH Entrepreneur Club's new startup program on Luma, and trees that can feel their own shape.

## Robot learning lesson: propose with a flow, refine with a model

**The setup.** [RAIBO-Wagon](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39267) (Yang, Kim, Hur, Jeon, Choi, Hwangbo) puts a RAIBO2 quadruped in front of a custom aluminum wagon, about 0.7 by 1.4 m and roughly 25 kg, attached through a 3-DOF passive joint with an encoder on the yaw axis. The wagon is underactuated: you can only steer it by moving the robot, and in a narrow corridor most paths that look fine for the robot swing the wagon into a wall. Everything runs onboard on an Intel NUC, with a Livox MID-360 lidar for mapping.

**The stack, top to bottom.**

- *Low-level walking (100 Hz):* a PPO policy with a GRU encoder, trained in RaiSim with only 200 parallel environments. It is blind to the wagon. It just tracks velocity commands while the curriculum randomizes wagon mass (up to 30 kg), inertia and center of mass. The GRU's job is to feel the load through the robot's own history.
- *Learned forward dynamics model (FDM):* given the robot's state and a sequence of 3-D velocity commands, a GRU predicts where the robot and wagon will go and whether they'll collide. Trained on about 500,000 simulated episodes with randomized mass, wagon size, terrain roughness and slipperiness. No real data.
- *Planner (2 Hz):* a conditional flow matching model (about 650k parameters, a plain MLP) takes the observation plus a waypoint 3 m ahead and samples 256 candidate 3-second command sequences in 10 Euler steps. Then MPPI runs 10 iterations, perturbing those candidates and scoring them with the FDM. The first command goes to the walking policy. Each cycle takes about 0.3 s: half flow, half MPPI.
- *Where the flow model's data comes from:* an expensive offline expert (A* plus a wagon-aware path optimizer plus MPPI with 1,024 samples and 25 iterations) that's far too slow to run live. The flow model learns to imitate it.

**Results.** On the real robot: 34 of 40 runs succeeded (85%) on 15 m courses across 8 terrain layouts, with loads of up to 40 kg, close to the robot's own body weight. On the hardest real course, the baselines got nothing: Hybrid A* with a rigid-trailer model went 0 for 5, and an end-to-end RL waypoint-following policy also went 0 for 5. In simulation, warm-starting MPPI with the flow model beat MPPI from scratch everywhere, and the gap grows with difficulty: 72.5% vs 29.5% on hard corridors, 96.5% vs 45.5% on hard slaloms.

**Why it works.** MPPI is a sampling optimizer. It is only as good as where it starts sampling, and in a narrow corridor almost all random command sequences crash, so MPPI from zero spends its whole compute budget on garbage. The flow model has seen thousands of expert solutions and puts its samples near the narrow band of feasible ones. But the flow model alone has a flaw: it only knows its training distribution. The ablations show it: taking the best of the 256 flow samples, without refinement, underperforms on unseen terrains, and the gap widens with difficulty. MPPI with the dynamics model is what pulls the plan back to reality when the map looks nothing like training. The generative model gives speed, the model-based search gives correctness.

**What to steal for your humanoid.**

- *Keep the walking policy dumb and robust, and put task smarts above it.* The walking policy here knows nothing about the wagon, the map or the goal. It tracks velocity under randomized loads. That is precisely the interface you want for your first walking policy: a clean velocity-command API that survives payloads and pushes. Everything task-level can then be built on top without retraining the legs.
- *Learn a dynamics model of your own walking policy.* Once you have a velocity-tracking policy, log commands and the resulting body motion in sim and train a small GRU to predict "if I command this, where do I end up". That model is cheap, and it turns every later task (footstep-aware navigation, carrying, pushing a cart) into a planning problem you can solve with sampling instead of another RL run.
- *Use generative models as proposal engines, not oracles.* This pattern (diffusion or flow proposes, a verifier or optimizer refines) generalizes way beyond wagons. If your team ever trains a flow-based manipulation policy, ask whether a quick model-based check on its samples would catch the bad ones.
- *Read the failure list.* Of the 6 real failures, 3 were mapping: on the sharpest turns the wagon swung into the part of the lidar view they masked out to avoid seeing the wagon. The other 3 were sim-to-real gaps in the lidar input. None were the walking policy falling over. Perception and plumbing break first.

**Limitations.** No back-and-forth maneuvers (it can't reverse to straighten the wagon, which any human with a trailer would do), no terrain reasoning beyond obstacles, and everything is trained for one wagon shape.

## New papers worth reading

### [Gaussian Distillation of Flow Policies](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40271)
Saied, Sheebaelhamd, Vernade and Fleuret look inside trained flow policies, the expressive action heads everyone uses for multimodal manipulation, and find that their action distributions are, in practice, well approximated by a single Gaussian. So they replace the last part of the network with a Gaussian readout, trained on just 0.02% of eligible states while everything else stays frozen. The result samples in one network pass instead of many, gives closed-form likelihoods (which RL fine-tuning needs), and matches the teacher's success at up to twice the speed. Two takeaways. First, that's the policy shape PPO uses for walking, so this is a bridge from imitation-trained flow policies back to RL-friendly ones. Second, before paying for expressiveness, check whether your policy actually uses it.

### [When More Planning Hurts](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37891)
A small, honest single-author paper on planning inside learned world models on the PushT benchmark. The headline: giving the planner more optimization steps (4, then 8, then 12 updates) gave 9, 8 and 7 successes out of 16. Extra planning rescued no failures, and in one scene cluster it lowered the predicted cost while turning successes into failures. In other words, the optimizer found plans the world model liked and reality didn't. It's the dark mirror of the RAIBO-Wagon lesson: a model-based check is only as good as the model, and a strong enough optimizer will find and exploit its mistakes. Fittingly, a released learned planner (LePlanner) solved all 16 in 5.7 seconds.

### Also new
- [Continuous-Action World Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40401): learning cross-embodiment dynamics priors with continuous actions, aimed at pretraining world models across robot types.
- [RoboSeed](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40026): a multidimensional benchmark for evaluating both VLAs and world action models, useful if you're trying to make sense of the flood of WAM papers.
- [How Much Should a VLA Trust Its Reasoning?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40002): treats step-level reasoning guidance as a measured action-value function, deciding when chain-of-thought helps the action and when it doesn't.

## Founder radar

### Opentrons: the boring robot that already won
Opentrons raised a 40 million dollar Series E led by healthcare investor Avego, with BroadOak Capital Partners ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/10/29/science-robotics-startup-opentrons-raises-40m-to-standardize-laboratory-automation/)). Founded in 2014, it makes benchtop pipetting robots: the cheap OT-2 for routine liquid handling and the enclosed Flex, which adds a gripper to move plates between steps. More than 11,000 robots are deployed in 80-plus countries, and the company says they're used at 90% of the top 20 pharma companies. The new pitch is "autonomous science": AI models now design experiments faster than humans can run them, so the physical lab is the bottleneck, and Opentrons wants to be the hands.

**Seeds to plant.**
- *An installed base is a data moat that takes a decade to build.* Eleven thousand robots that already run DNA extraction, PCR and ELISA protocols are eleven thousand places to ship a smarter version. A humanoid startup would kill for that distribution.
- *The constrained environment is a feature.* A pipetting robot works on a bench with known plates in known positions. That's why it works today. Lab automation is a market where "a little more general" (moving plates, handling odd consumables, opening freezers) is worth a lot, and where a mobile manipulator or a learned policy could be the step up from fixed scripts.
- *"AI designs, robot executes" is a whole category.* Self-driving labs need someone to write the bridge between a model's experiment plan and a robot's protocol. That's a software startup, not a hardware one.

## Outside your bubble

### [Trees can feel their own shape](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261030003006.htm)
Researchers at INRAE in France, publishing in New Phytologist, wanted to know how a tree that's been knocked sideways gets straight again. Trees bend by growing "tension wood", a special wood that contracts and pulls the stem toward it. The textbook story was that the tree simply follows gravity and light.

So they removed both. Young poplars were laid on their side until their stems curved upward, about ten days. Then the trees went into a machine that slowly rotates them inside a sphere lit evenly from all sides, so there's no consistent "up" and no direction to the light. The stems still straightened almost completely over several weeks. The tension wood on the upper side stopped forming, and the same kind of wood started forming on the opposite side, pulling the other way, like an opposing muscle. The only signal left was the tree's sense of its own curvature: proprioception. "What we have uncovered is a genuine sensorimotor loop operating in the woody parts of trees," says research director Bruno Moulia.

Why it's here: a tree is a controller with a sample rate of days and actuators it has to grow before it can use. It still runs feedback on its own posture, with agonist-antagonist actuation, and without any external reference. It's a humbling reminder that "control" is not a thing nervous systems invented. And on the practical side: the same understanding could help breed straighter timber and cereal crops that don't fall over before harvest, which matters more as storms get worse.
