# A walking policy before your coffee cools

*2026-11-20*

Good morning. Picture the usual loop when you train a locomotion policy: change a reward weight, start a run, go get lunch, come back, watch the robot do the worm across the floor, sigh, repeat. Now picture that run finishing in under a minute. A paper from Nico Bohlinger and Jan Peters claims humanoid motion tracking and rough-terrain walking in less time than it takes to read this intro. That's today's main thread, because the speed of your iteration loop matters more than almost any single trick you'll put into it. After that: a robot that adapts to a heavier backpack without retraining, humanoids getting trained inside a video model, a startup that sells house walls instead of robots, and a virus that has been hiding in medieval Bibles.

## The big one: humanoid locomotion in under a minute

**[XMimic and XSoma: Learning Humanoid Motion Tracking and Locomotion in Under a Minute](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20452)** (Bohlinger, Peters), submitted 16 November.

If the names sound familiar: the same authors gave us STRIDE ten days ago, which learned to walk from minutes of samples with an IMU-only reward. This one attacks a different bottleneck: wall-clock time.

What they did, from the abstract:

1. **Start from a model-based learner.** Both methods build on SuperMimic, which learns a world model of the robot and improves the policy by backpropagating analytic gradients through short imagined rollouts. No guessing which action was good; the gradient tells you directly.
2. **Kill the waiting.** One training iteration in that setup is a long chain of small, sequential GPU operations. Each one is quick, but the GPU spends most of its time waiting for the next tiny job. They shorten that chain and run steps concurrently on up to four GPUs.
3. **Compile everything.** The whole training loop, simulator included (MuJoCo Warp), is compiled with JAX. No Python round trips between "simulate" and "learn".
4. **Only learn what you have to.** For locomotion, XSoma uses the learned model only for the robot's own dynamics. Everything else, such as the terrain height under each foot, is computed exactly. It uses a one-second horizon and, notably, no critic at all.

The numbers: on four H100s, XMimic tracks all 40 LAFAN1 motion clips on a Unitree G1 in 0.86 minutes, 3.7 times faster than BlinkMimic on one GPU (we covered BlinkMimic on 14 November). XSoma hits the tracking target from Amazon FAR's Holosoma benchmark in 0.77 minutes, 12.3 times faster than FastSAC on one GPU, using about an eighth of the environment steps. The abstract reports simulation results only, and there's no code link yet, so treat "walks in a minute" as "trains in sim in a minute" for now.

**Why this matters for your walking phase.** You probably don't have four H100s sitting in the club room. That's fine. The lesson isn't the hardware, it's where the time goes:

- **Profile before you buy compute.** Run your current PPO setup and look at GPU utilisation. If it's sitting at 30%, your bottleneck is Python glue, logging, or small kernels, not FLOPs. Fixing that is free.
- **Iteration speed changes how you design rewards.** At an hour per run, you change five things at once and hope. At five minutes per run, you change one thing and actually learn which knob did what. That's the difference between debugging and praying.
- **Compute exactly whatever you can.** XSoma's choice to learn only the robot's dynamics and calculate the rest is a good general rule. Every quantity you can write down by hand is one the model can't get wrong or exploit.
- **Know the risk of model-based gradients.** A policy trained through a learned model will happily exploit that model's mistakes. Short horizons (here, one second) are how this family keeps it in check. If you ever try it, start short.

The bigger idea: training time is a design constraint, not a fact of life. The teams that iterate fastest tend to win, and right now someone is showing that "fastest" can mean minutes.

## Robot learning lesson: adapt without changing the weights

**[ECHO: In-Context Behavior Foundation Model for Humanoid Whole-Body Control](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25778)** (Wang, Li, Shi and others, [project page](https://mwondering.github.io/echo-bfm/)), submitted 19 November.

Here's a problem you will hit. You train a nice walking policy. Then someone straps a battery pack on the back, or the arms pick up a box, or one actuator runs a bit warmer than the others. The robot's dynamics have changed, and your policy was trained for the old ones. A general policy trained across lots of conditions tends to play it safe everywhere, which the authors call a "conservative compromise", instead of being precise in the conditions it actually faces.

ECHO's answer has two parts:

- **A frozen behaviour prior.** One large policy learns many skills through motion imitation. A sampling scheme balances practising hard motions with keeping broad coverage, all in a single pretraining stage.
- **A context module on top.** It reads the robot's recent interaction history, works out "what kind of body and situation am I in right now", and outputs residual corrections to the frozen prior. No weights change at test time. The adaptation lives entirely in the input.

On a Unitree G1 they show whole-body teleoperation including acrobatics and terrain contact, and adaptation to payloads during manipulation.

**The general principle** goes back to earlier work like Rapid Motor Adaptation: if the policy can see a short history of its own states and actions, it can infer hidden parameters (mass, friction, motor strength) the way you notice a backpack is heavy after one step. For a custom-built robot this is gold, because your simulated model of the motors and mass distribution will be wrong in ways you can't fully measure.

What to do with it now:

- **Give your policy a history window** (even 5 to 50 steps of joint positions, velocities and past actions), and randomise mass, friction and motor strength during training. This is the cheap version of the same idea.
- **Plan for a residual later.** When you move from walking to tasks, don't retrain the walker from scratch for every payload. Keep the walker frozen and train a small correction on top. Your future self will thank you during the "it worked yesterday" phase.

## New papers worth reading

- **[WEVO: World Model-Enabled VLA Optimization for Whole-Body Loco-Manipulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25251)** (Chen, Lin, Sun, Schwager, Kuo, Martín-Martín, [project page](https://qianzhong-chen.github.io/wevo.github.io/)). A humanoid VLA gets improved with RL inside an action-conditioned video world model instead of on the real robot. The world model is first fed simulated "play" data, then aligned with just 20 real rollouts per task. Result: mean success goes from 23.3% to 75.0%, compared with 48.3% for RL on the real robot. Their split is worth stealing for your task phase: simulation for initialisation and coverage, the world model for deployment-aligned polish.
- **[Stuck in Place: Stall Diagnosis and Remedies for JEPA Planners](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25415)**. The authors find that most failures of JEPA-style latent planners aren't wrong plans, they're stalls: the planner just stops making progress. Their fix is simple loop breakers. Good reading for the paper club on 26 November, as a counterweight to world-model hype.
- **[Bridging Vision-Language Reasoning and Learned Control for Legged Navigation in the Wild](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25215)**. A VLM draws semantic risk maps ("that slope looks slippery") and RL locomotion policies do the walking, for kilometre-scale hikes. The pattern of slow reasoning on top and fast reflexes below is the same one you'll likely use for your humanoid's tasks.
- **[WAMotor: Accelerating World Action Models via Speculative Inference](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25413)**. Borrows speculative decoding from LLMs to speed up world-action model inference by 2.86x to 4.28x without losing success rate. Another sign that the LLM inference toolbox is being moved into robotics wholesale.

## Founder radar

### Promise Robotics: don't sell the robot, sell the wall

On 17 November, Calgary's **Promise Robotics** raised a **C$40M Series B** led by White Star Capital, with Radical Ventures, Horizons and PSP Investments ([The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/promise-robotics-40m-cad-series-b-robot-homebuilding)). It runs robotic factories that make house components like wall frames from each builder's existing designs. Builders don't buy a robot; they buy the output. The company calls it "Factory-as-a-Service". It has more than 2,500 homes under contract, a multi-year deal with D.R. Horton (the biggest US homebuilder by volume), and a first US plant planned for Phoenix in 2027.

The seed: robot startups usually die in the gap between "the demo works" and "the customer's maintenance team can keep it running". Selling the output instead of the machine closes that gap, because the robot never leaves your building. Ask yourself which other products are made by hand today, in small batches, with designs that change every order. Custom furniture, electrical cabinets, cable harnesses and stage sets are all candidates. Any of them could be the next "we own the factory, you send us the drawing" company.

### Rheinmetall wants a soldier-shaped robot

In an RTL interview on 16 November, Rheinmetall CEO Armin Papperger said the company is developing military humanoids, with prototypes possibly ready in 15 to 18 months and engineering currently focused on the upper body ([defence-industry.eu](https://defence-industry.eu/rheinmetall-ceo-says-humanoid-robot-prototypes-for-military-use-could-be-ready-in-15-to-18-months-as-development-proceeds/)). No partners or roles were named. Whatever you think of it, it matters for you in two ways: European defence money is about to start competing for the same robot-learning engineers that startups want, and the ethics questions will reach student clubs and job offers sooner than most people expect. It's worth deciding your own line before someone asks.

## Outside the bubble: a virus written into the Bible

When medieval monks made a book, they wrote on parchment: animal skin, scraped and stretched. It turns out that skin remembers what the animal was sick with.

A team led by University College Dublin, with more than 30 institutions, recovered **sheeppox virus DNA** from medieval manuscripts, including the roughly 1,000-year-old **York Gospels** and the **Corpus Glossary** in Cambridge, one of the earliest English dictionaries ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261106174545.htm)). Combined with DNA from Bronze Age sheep teeth found on the Eurasian steppe, the record shows the virus has troubled sheep for more than 3,700 years, and its genome has barely changed in that time. Some traces turned up on calfskin and goatskin too, which could mean infections in other livestock or contamination during parchment making. It isn't only history: sheeppox is currently hitting farms in Greece.

Why it's worth a minute: every library in Europe turns out to be an accidental biobank. The data was there for a thousand years, waiting for someone to realise that the medium was also the message. That's a useful question to ask of any dataset, including the logs your robot writes: what else got recorded that nobody meant to record?
