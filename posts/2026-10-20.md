# Legs that don't care what the arms are doing

*2026-10-20*

Here is a moment your team will reach sooner than you think. The walking policy works. It survives pushes, it turns, it stops. Then someone plugs in an arm controller to pick up a box, the arms swing forward, the centre of mass moves ten centimetres, and the robot falls flat on its face. Walking with arms hanging still and walking while the arms do something useful are two different control problems. Today's lesson is about the cleanest fix that the field has converged on: train the legs to treat the arms as weather. A paper out this morning does exactly that on a Unitree G1 and pairs the same leg policy with two different VLAs. Also: a quadruped that learns to walk in under 20 minutes on one GPU, an LLM agent that cuts VLAs in half, a robots-as-a-service startup that rents its brain from Physical Intelligence, a new SLAM internship in Zurich, a physical AI hackathon in Paris in December, a heavy earnings Wednesday, and a mountain that fell into a fjord.

## Robot learning lesson: decoupled upper and lower body control

**The problem.** A humanoid's arms are not light. On a G1-sized robot, the two arms together are a meaningful fraction of the body mass, and when they reach forward or lift something, three things change for the legs: the centre of mass shifts, the inertia around the hips changes, and reaction forces from the arm motion push back on the torso. A walking policy that only ever saw arms in a fixed pose during training has never experienced any of this. To it, a reaching arm is a disturbance it has no model of.

**The tempting wrong fix.** Train one big policy that controls all joints and does walking plus manipulation. It can work, but every new task means retraining the balance controller too, and the hard-won walking behaviour gets dragged around by whatever the manipulation reward wants. It also makes it impossible to swap in someone else's arm policy.

**The decoupled fix.** Split the robot at the waist:

- The *lower-body policy* outputs targets for the leg joints (and usually the waist). It is trained with RL in simulation, exactly like your walking policy.
- The arm joint positions and velocities go into its *observation*, but not its *action*. It sees what the arms are doing and has to stay upright anyway.
- The *upper-body controller* can be anything: a motion-tracking policy, teleoperation, a VLA. The two are combined only at the motor command level.

**The trick that makes it work: an upper-body pose curriculum.** During training, the arms are driven by randomly sampled target poses, and the range of those poses grows over training. Early on the arms barely move, so the policy learns to walk. Later the arms swing to extreme reaches, fast, while the policy is walking and being pushed. The legs learn to compensate for whatever the arms throw at them. This is the same idea as a terrain curriculum, just applied to your own body instead of the ground. ExBody (Cheng et al., RSS 2024) used a version of this split to make a humanoid dance with its arms while walking, and HOMIE (2025) used an upper-body pose curriculum with an exoskeleton cockpit for teleoperated loco-manipulation.

**Today's paper: DUET.** The new paper (link below) is a clean, minimal version of the recipe on a 23-DoF G1. The locomotion policy controls 12 leg joints and waist yaw and tracks forward and sideways velocity, turning rate and pelvis height. The 10 arm joints are observations only. The same locomotion checkpoint is then paired, without any retraining, with two VLAs, GR00T N1.7 and pi0.5, and in simulation the lower body never falls. The ablation is the most useful number: without the upper-body pose curriculum, average episode survival drops from 20.0 to 7.4 seconds. The curriculum is not a detail; it is the method. The robot also survives pushes up to 1.0 m/s within the payload range it was trained on.

**What to take for your team.**
- *Design the interface now, even before the first walk.* Decide which joints belong to the walking policy and put the arm states in its observation from day one. Retrofitting this later means retraining from scratch.
- *Sample arm poses during walking training, even if you only want to walk.* Cheap insurance: your arms will never be perfectly still on hardware anyway, because cables, low-gain arm PD and imperfect zero positions all make them wiggle.
- *Randomize payload in the hands.* A box in the gripper is a mass at the end of a lever. Add random point masses at the wrists, scaled up by the curriculum.
- *Know the limit.* Decoupling means the legs cannot help the arms. Picking something up from the floor needs squatting and leaning, which is a whole-body motion. That is when you move to the motion-tracking and whole-body controllers from earlier posts. Decoupled control is the first step, not the last.

Further reading: [ExBody, Expressive Whole-Body Control for Humanoid Robots](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16796) and [HOMIE: Humanoid Loco-Manipulation with Isomorphic Exoskeleton Cockpit](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13013).

## New papers worth reading

### [DUET: Decoupled Upper- and Lower-Body Execution for Task-Agnostic Humanoid Loco-Manipulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23872)
Covered in the lesson. The seed worth keeping: a locomotion policy that any VLA can drive the arms on top of is a *product*, not just a paper. Whoever ships the most robust "legs as a service" layer for humanoids gets to be the foundation that every manipulation model runs on.

### [Towards Reinforcement Learning for Space Robotics: Fast Training and Reliable Real-World Transfer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24241)
A quadruped learns to cross varied terrain, including a lunar-analog test facility, after less than 20 minutes of simulated training on a single consumer GPU, and the policy transfers to hardware. The headline is the training budget. If your team's iteration loop is "train overnight, test in the morning", you get one experiment a day. At 20 minutes, you get twenty. Read it for the engineering choices that make training that fast; it is directly relevant to a club running on student hardware.

### [CODES: Weakly Supervised Discovery of Motion Styles for Compositional Robot Control](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24021)
Discovers distinct motion styles from recordings with only weak labels, then uses RL to combine them with velocity commands on a Unitree Go2, and transfers to hardware. Useful later when the team wants the humanoid to walk in more than one way (careful, brisk, carrying) without writing a separate reward for each.

### [AutoPruner: Agentic Depth Pruning for Vision-Language-Action Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23880)
An LLM agent searches over which transformer layers to delete from a VLA backbone, and how to recover performance afterwards. It removes between 16.7% and 87.5% of layers while keeping task success above 90%, with up to 2.5x speedups. Two takeaways: VLAs are much more redundant than their size suggests, and "LLM agent as research assistant running a search over model surgery" is a pattern that will spread. Onboard compute on a humanoid is tight, so pruning matters for anyone putting a VLA on the robot itself.

## Founder radar

### [Ultra raises $62 million to lease packing robots, with Physical Intelligence as the brain](https://fortune.com/2026/10/09/ultra-raises-62-million-fast-growing-robots-service-tie-up-ai-research-firm-physical-intelligence/)
Ultra, from Brooklyn, builds robots that pack goods for shipping and installs them at third-party logistics warehouses. It does not sell them. Customers pay an integration fee up front, then a monthly fee for the hardware and support. The money: a $50 million Series A led by Framework Ventures, on top of a $12 million seed led by Y Combinator and NextView. The company says its robots have packed over half a million orders and that demand let it raise prices. The interesting part is the split: Ultra builds and installs the body, Physical Intelligence provides the model that adapts to each customer's setup, and in return PI gets real deployment data.

Put this next to yesterday's news that PI opened its API, and a template for a 2026 robotics startup appears:

1. Pick one boring, high-volume task that warehouses pay for (packing, not general-purpose helping).
2. Rent the brain.
3. Sell outcomes monthly, not robots once, so customers never face a capital purchase decision.
4. Own the installation and the data.

The question to chew on: what other single task has a large number of identical sites, a labour shortage, and a manager who would rather sign a monthly invoice than a capex request? Laundry folding in hotels, sample handling in clinical labs, returns processing, kitchen dishwashing. Each one is a possible company with exactly this shape.

## Outside your bubble

### [A mountain fell into an Alaskan fjord, and the water climbed 481 metres](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261018205047.htm)
Just after 5 a.m. on 9 August 2025, a huge section of mountainside collapsed into Tracy Arm, a fjord near Juneau. The wave it pushed up ran 481 metres up the opposite wall, higher than the top floor of Taipei 101, making it the second-highest tsunami ever recorded. It then raced down the fjord and stripped the walls to bare rock. A tour ship had passed the spot about twelve hours earlier. Nobody was in the fjord when it happened, which is the only reason this is a science story and not a disaster.

The new analysis in *Science*, led by the Alaska Earthquake Center, is about the warnings. The glacier below the slope had retreated more than half a kilometre in two months, removing the ice that had been propping the slope up. Heavy rain had soaked into the cracks. And in the days before the collapse, seismometers recorded thousands of tiny tremors from the slide area, which merged into a continuous seismic hum. The authors propose a traffic-light system like those used for avalanches: yellow when weather and glacier retreat raise the risk, orange when the tremors surge and boats should stay out, red when the slide is happening, which seismic waves can detect within a couple of minutes.

Why it is here: as glaciers retreat in Alaska, Greenland, Norway and the Alps, mountains lose the ice that held them up, and this kind of event will become more common. But the deeper idea is one you will recognise from robotics. The failure was not silent. The system was broadcasting its state for days, and nobody was listening to that channel. Half of safety engineering, for slopes or for walking robots, is deciding which signals you log and who looks at them before the fall.
