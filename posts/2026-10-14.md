# After the first steps: motion tracking as the road to tasks

*2026-10-14*

Two weeks of lessons have been about one thing: getting a custom humanoid to walk without falling over, lying to itself or setting a motor on fire. Today we look one step past that, because "then teach it tasks" is where most club projects stall. The most popular answer in 2026 is a little surprising: do not train each task, train the robot to copy humans, then steer the copying. Also today: a humanoid that learns to carry boxes from YouTube-style video, a NAO that teaches itself football in 30 minutes, a terrain paper that tests which of its own ideas actually matter, a Chinese humanoid maker raising its fourth round in six months, a Zurich paper club with two seats left, and the octopus genome.

## Robot learning lesson: motion tracking, the universal task interface

**The problem with one reward per task.** Your walking policy has a reward built by hand: track the velocity command, keep the torso upright, do not slam the feet, and twenty other terms you tuned for days. Now imagine doing that again for "pick up the box", "wave", "kick the ball", "climb the step". Each task means a new reward, new weights, new failure modes. It does not scale, and it is exactly how teams burn a semester.

**The alternative: one reward that says "do what this human did".** Motion tracking (the lineage starts with DeepMimic in 2018) gives the policy a reference motion, a sequence of target joint angles and body poses from motion capture or video, and rewards it for matching that reference while staying physically balanced. The reward is the same for a cartwheel and a handshake: pose error, velocity error, end-effector error, plus your usual regularizers. The task lives in the data, not in the reward.

**The 2025 to 2026 recipe has three pieces.**

1. *Get human motion.* Public datasets (AMASS, LAFAN1), a mocap suit, or plain video run through a pose estimator like GVHMR.
2. *Retarget it to your robot.* A human and your humanoid do not share limb lengths or joint limits. Retargeting solves for robot joint angles that put the hands, feet and torso where the human's were, without breaking the robot. The open-source [GMR](https://github.com/YanjieZe/GMR) (General Motion Retargeting, MIT license, about 2.8k stars) does this in real time on a CPU for 18 humanoids, from SMPL-X, BVH and FBX inputs. Your robot is not on the list, but adding one is mostly a matter of a URDF and a body-mapping table, which you already audited on 10 October.
3. *Train one tracking policy over many clips.* [BeyondMimic](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.08241) from Berkeley's Hybrid Robotics group (Liao, Sreenath, Karen Liu and colleagues) showed that one setup with shared hyperparameters learns sprints, spin-kicks and even aerial cartwheels, and transfers zero-shot to a Unitree G1. Their training code, [whole_body_tracking](https://github.com/HybridRobotics/whole_body_tracking), runs on Isaac Lab and RSL-RL, the stack you are probably already using.

**Then steer it.** A tracking policy only replays motions. To get tasks, you put something on top that produces the reference: a joystick, a teleoperation rig (the reference comes live from a person wearing a suit or a VR headset), or a generative model. BeyondMimic trains a diffusion model over the tracker's behavior and uses guidance at test time to do obstacle avoidance or joystick control without retraining. Today's carrying paper (below) shows another way: start with tracking, then relax it until the robot solves the task its own way.

**What this means for your club.**
- Keep the walking policy as its own thing for now. A dedicated locomotion policy is still more robust than a general tracker for getting from A to B.
- Everything you built for walking carries over: the actuator model, the domain randomization, the state estimation discipline, the bring-up gates. Tracking is just a different reward and a different command.
- Retargeting quality is the hidden bottleneck. If the retargeted clip has a foot sliding on the ground or a knee past its limit, the policy learns to fight physics. Filter clips before training, and watch them on the robot model in the viewer first.
- A cheap first milestone after walking: track ten short upper-body motions (wave, reach, point) while standing. It exercises the whole pipeline with low risk of falls.

**Rule of thumb:** hand-write rewards for the one skill everything depends on (walking), and get every other skill from data through a tracker.

## New papers worth reading

### [From Video to Vision: Learning Generalizable Humanoid Carrying from Human Motion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14561)
A clean example of the recipe above, in three stages. Human carrying motion is extracted from video, retargeted with GENMO and GMR, and augmented for different object sizes. A teacher learns to carry objects along the human trajectories with a DeepMimic-style tracking reward. An "assistant" stage loosens the tracking and changes the reward, so the robot learns to pick objects up from starting positions the human never used. Finally a student is distilled with DAgger plus PPO so it sees the object through an onboard RGB-D camera, with no motion capture. It runs on a real humanoid. The interesting move is the middle stage: imitation gets you a natural motion, then RL is allowed to drift from it to generalize.

### [Reinforcement Learning from Real-Robot Interaction without High-Quality State Estimates](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14042)
Gao, Hanna and colleagues train a NAO to dribble a ball into a goal using only real-world experience, about 30 minutes of it, with no mocap. The onboard Kalman filter loses the ball whenever it leaves the camera's view, so they learn a recurrent state-space model whose latent only gets corrected when the ball is visible, and plug it into the off-policy RLPD algorithm. Result: 100% success, while a memoryless policy with the same budget scores 0%. Read it as a sequel to yesterday's state estimation lesson: when the estimate is bad, give the policy memory and let it learn what to trust.

### [Terrain Traversal Without Motion Priors: Expert-Routed Humanoid Locomotion at Deployment Fidelity](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14061)
A humanoid crosses an 85 m course of stairs, hurdles and rough ground end to end 18 times out of 20, using a mixture of five experts switched by a terrain classifier, plus a footstep planner that refines each foothold with a few gradient steps. Worth reading for its honesty more than its architecture: a single expert did as well as five on the training terrains, label-free routers collapsed to uniform, and the self-supervised terrain embedding was worse than a privileged label. Most papers hide this kind of finding. Note the confidence interval on the 90% (70 to 97%): that is what 20 trials actually buys you, a good thing to remember when your club reports "it works" after three tries.

## Founder radar

### [Astribot closes its fourth round in six months](https://techstartups.com/2026/10/13/startup-funding-news-today-october-13-2026-astribot-carbonfarm-ubitium-epyr-more)
Shenzhen's Astribot (Stardust Intelligence) raised about 1 billion RMB (roughly 150 million dollars) at a 15 billion RMB valuation, up 5 billion in three months, led by capital linked to Shenzhen Investment Holdings. Its robots use cable-driven actuation instead of motors at every joint, which makes the arms lighter and safer around people, and it builds its own models (Lumo) and operating system. It claims orders for thousands of units. Claims are cheap: orders are not deliveries, and capacity is not output.

In the same day's list, two quieter deals are more instructive for a student founder. Sankai Electromechanical raised a Series B for precision planetary gearboxes for robots, and Düsseldorf's Ubitium raised a 14 million dollar seed for processors aimed at robots and drones. Every humanoid brain needs a body, and every body needs gearboxes and compute. The picks-and-shovels layer keeps getting funded while the headline valuations fight it out.

**Idea seed.** Retargeting is the quiet bottleneck of today's lesson. Every new humanoid (and dozens of club and startup robots are appearing) needs its own mapping from human motion to robot joints, plus a cleaned motion library, and today that is a week of fiddling per robot. A service or open tool that takes a URDF and returns a validated library of a few hundred retargeted, physically filtered clips (no foot sliding, no joint-limit violations, with a report) would save every humanoid team that week. Start by doing it for your own robot and publishing the pipeline.

## Outside your bubble

### [Octopus intelligence may come from folding the genome differently](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261011211552.htm)
University of Vienna researchers (Thea Rogers, Oleg Simakov and colleagues, in Nature Communications) mapped the 3D shape of octopus, squid and cuttlefish genomes, how the DNA physically folds in the cell. Hundreds of millions of years ago, cephalopod chromosomes were shuffled so that regions that used to be far apart ended up touching. That can change which genes switch on without changing a single letter of the sequence. Big folded domains stayed stable over evolution, while small loops varied between species and tissues, and many of the flexible loops sit near genes for nervous system development. The authors are careful: this is a possible mechanism, not proof that it caused octopus intelligence.

Why it is worth a minute: octopuses are the other experiment in intelligence on this planet, built on a body plan with most neurons in the arms, not the head. It is the purest example of decentralized control in nature, eight limbs that partly decide for themselves. And the idea itself, same code, different wiring, different behavior, sounds a lot like what a good training curriculum does to a fixed network.
