# Who checks the success checker?

*2026-11-22*

Good Sunday morning. Today's thread is uncomfortable: the number that says your robot succeeded may be wrong, and nobody checked the code that produces it. A new audit of three popular simulated manipulation benchmarks found 61 bugs in their success checkers. Some passed runs that never finished the task. Others failed runs that did it correctly, just not the way the expert demo did. A second paper this week shows a terrain classifier going from 38% to 99% accuracy without getting any better. They only changed how the data was split. And a third shows that "the robot didn't finish the forbidden task" is not the same as "the robot was safe".

This matters for you right now. Before your humanoid walks, someone in the club will write a script that decides whether a run counts as a success. That script decides everything that follows. After that: a tutorial on how simulators really handle feet touching the ground, a 500 million dollar Series A for a Paris robotics company, an update on the first humanoid stock, and a 54-year-old theory in biology that turned out to rest on mutations nobody could see.

## The big one: phantom successes and silent failures

**[Phantom Success, Silent Failure: Auditing the Success Checkers of Simulated Manipulation Benchmarks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25179)** (Song, Zhang, Lu, Ma, Chang, Chen), submitted 18 November.

Every simulated benchmark has a small piece of hand-written code that decides whether an episode succeeded. "Is the cup inside the drawer?" becomes something like "the cup's position is inside this bounding box and the gripper is open". Papers report success rates to one decimal place, and almost nobody reads that function.

The authors built SimAudit, a pipeline that does read it. An LLM gets the task instruction and the checker code and guesses where they might disagree. It then sets up those cases in simulation, runs the original checker on them, and renders the scene from several angles to confirm what actually happened. They ran it on RoboTwin 2.0, RoboCasa365 and RoboDojo.

The results:

- **61 distinct defects across 49 tasks**, after visual checks and removing duplicates.
- **40 phantom successes**: the checker passes a run that didn't finish the task.
- **21 silent failures**: the checker fails a run that did the task, just not the way the demonstration did.
- **Each benchmark failed in its own way.** RoboCasa365's defects mostly accepted incomplete runs, RoboDojo's mostly rejected valid ones, and RoboTwin 2.0 had both.

Note the bias. A checker built around how the expert did it punishes a policy that finds a different, equally good solution. That's exactly the kind of policy RL tends to produce. And a checker with phantom successes rewards the shortcuts you were trying to rule out. If you train or select on it, you're optimising the bug.

**Why this matters for your walking phase.** Your version of the checker is the eval script: "walked 10 metres without falling", "tracked the commanded velocity within some tolerance", "survived the push". Before you trust any number it prints:

- **Write the phantom cases on purpose.** Can the robot pass by sliding on its knees? By shuffling with its feet never leaving the ground? By falling forward across the finish line? Script those cases, or record them from a bad policy, and check that the evaluator fails them.
- **Write the silent-failure cases too.** A valid gait that looks nothing like your reference motion, or a run that briefly leaves the tolerance band and recovers, should still pass. If it fails, your metric is measuring "looks like the demo" rather than "walks".
- **Watch the videos for a random sample of passes and fails.** Ten of each per evaluation round costs very little. It's the same check the paper does with multi-view renders, except you do it by eye.
- **Version the evaluator with the policy.** When you change the checker, old numbers stop being comparable. Store which checker version produced each result.

The general lesson is worth keeping for life: when a number drives decisions, test the code that produces it as carefully as anything else in the system.

## Robot learning lesson: what your simulator does when a foot touches the ground

**[A Tutorial on Complementarity-Based Contact Models for Robotics](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26600)** (Beker, Geist, Posa, Toussaint, Carpentier, Martius), submitted 19 November.

When you train a walking policy, the simulator settles one question thousands of times a second: is this foot touching the ground, and if so, is it sticking or sliding? MuJoCo, Isaac and the rest each have an answer, and the policy will exploit whatever that answer gets wrong. This tutorial is a self-contained treatment of the maths most of them build on, written by people behind Pinocchio and contact-implicit control, among others.

The core ideas, in plain language:

- **Complementarity** is a neat way to encode contact. Either the gap between foot and floor is zero and the floor pushes back, or there is a gap and the force is zero. Never both. The same trick handles friction: the foot either sticks, with the friction force inside its allowed cone, or slides, with friction pushing back at its limit. You don't have to tell the solver which mode the foot is in. Solving the equations works that out.
- **Exact versus relaxed.** The exact problem is hard to solve, so many fast simulators solve a convex relaxation instead. That's faster and more stable, but the tutorial lists the artifacts it causes: feet that **lift off** slightly when they should stick, and slow **creep**, where a foot that should be planted drifts.
- **Non-uniqueness.** Some contact situations have more than one physically valid answer, for example a foot with four contact corners on flat ground. The solver quietly picks one. Two simulators can pick differently, and the policy can learn to rely on the choice.
- **Solvers and their trade-offs.** First-order solvers are cheap per iteration but need many iterations. Newton-type solvers cost more per step and converge faster. Regularisation buys robustness at the cost of fidelity.

**How to use this.** You don't need to write a contact solver. You do need to recognise when a sim-to-real gap comes from the contact model:

- If the simulated robot walks with tiny, oddly frequent steps, or its feet slowly slide while "standing", suspect creep or soft-contact effects that the policy has learned to use.
- Train in one simulator and evaluate in another (MuJoCo against Isaac, say) before going to hardware. A policy that only works in one is often exploiting that simulator's contact quirks.
- Randomise contact parameters (friction, and solver softness where the simulator exposes it), not just mass and motor gains.

Bookmark the paper and read the first half properly. It's the background most robot learning people skip, and it's the reason "it worked in sim" so often fails on hardware.

## New papers worth reading

- **[A Practical Proprioceptive Data Collection Utility for the Unitree GO1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26146)** (Smitterberg, Chen). The tool itself is modest: an open-source GUI that logs joint states, IMU, foot force and gyro data from a Go1 without needing ROS. The interesting part is a warning in the results. A terrain classifier gets about 38% on held-out recordings across ten terrain classes, where chance is 10%. Split the data by window instead of by recording and accuracy jumps to about 99%, because neighbouring windows from the same run leak into the test set. Keep this in mind the first time someone in the club trains a contact or terrain estimator on logged robot data: split by run, never by sample.
- **[Teaching Vision-Language-Action Models to Stop Safely](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26239)** (Liu, Wang, Guo and others). Policies trained to refuse a forbidden task from the start can still finish it if you hand over control mid-task. Even when they don't finish, they may drop whatever they're holding. So "task not completed" doesn't mean "safe". Their fix, SAFI, trains an explicit fallback: on forbidden-task demonstrations the target is to hold the arm still and keep a consistent grip. Built on OpenVLA-OFT, it brings success on forbidden tasks after a mid-task handover down to 1.3% on LIBERO, with no recorded physical incidents. It's the same lesson as the main story, applied to safety: define the outcome you want, not just the one you're trying to avoid.
- **[PGX: Physically Grounded Execution for Long-Horizon Humanoid Loco-Manipulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26596)** (Lu, Ma, Havoutis, Oxford; [project page](https://kl-research.github.io/pgx)). Instead of one big VLA handling the whole task, a state machine handles timing: it moves between phases based on physical conditions, like "object grasped" or "base within reach". A separate layer keeps positions anchored in world coordinates. The VLA only runs during the manipulation phases. Tested in three sim tasks and one real task on a Unitree G1. For the club's "teach it tasks" phase, it's a reminder that a few well-chosen checks for "has this phase actually finished?" can replace a lot of model capacity. They're also success checkers, so they need the same scrutiny.

## Outside the bubble: the mutations nobody could see

In 1972, biologists proposed a neat idea: organisms protect their most important genes by making them mutate less. A stronger version says mutation isn't random at all, and that essential genes, like the ones that copy DNA, are shielded while genes that might help an organism adapt mutate more.

Sofia Radrizzani and Laurence Hurst at the University of Bath tested it on the largest set of spontaneous mutations collected so far, from E. coli and baker's yeast ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261119194538.htm), PLOS Biology). They used mutation-accumulation experiments, which keep natural selection out of the way so that almost every mutation that happens gets recorded. The result: essential genes don't mutate any less. Neither protein abundance nor how fast a gene evolved over time predicted its mutation rate. Mutation rates do vary across the genome, but for known mechanical reasons. For example, genes copied late in DNA replication pick up more mutations.

So why did the protection look real? The authors suggest "ghost mutations". Harmful mutations in essential genes do happen, but selection removes them before anyone can count them. The measurement was filtering the data before scientists ever saw it, and the filter looked like biology.

That's today's main story again, in a different field. If the thing that records your outcomes also removes some of them, whether it's a success checker, a data split or a selection process, you'll find patterns that aren't there. When a result looks too clean, ask what never reached your dataset.
