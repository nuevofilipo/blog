# End the episode, not the robot

*2026-11-21*

Good Saturday morning. Here's a reinforcement learning trick that is almost too simple to be a paper: when the robot starts cheating, stop the episode. No extra reward term, no penalty weight to tune. The episode just ends, and the robot loses every reward it would have collected afterwards. A group just used little more than that to teach a small humanoid to ride a skateboard, with no demonstrations at all. That's today's main thread, because termination conditions are something you'll write a lot of for your first walking policy, and most people don't treat them as a design tool. After that: a whole-body control benchmark that's worth a weekend, a quadruped that picks its own gait, a startup that sends people out with capture rigs as a business model, and a billion years of Earth's history that went missing.

## The big one: a humanoid on a skateboard, no reference motions

**[Reference-Free Humanoid Skateboarding via Termination-Shaped Reinforcement Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26054)** (Soenessardien, Przystupa, Luck), submitted 19 November, accepted to IEEE-RAS Humanoids 2026.

Skateboarding is a nasty control problem. The thing under your feet moves, steers when you tilt it, and leaves the moment you get your weight wrong. The usual ways to learn it are motion capture of a human skater, or a hand-derived model of how the board steers, which needs careful system identification. This paper does neither. It's plain model-free RL on a Booster K1 humanoid, with task rewards plus what the authors call "exploit-blocking terminations".

The episode ends when the robot:

- loses its planted foot on the board,
- stands with both feet on the board (the easy way out: just ride along and never push),
- twists too much,
- separates from the board, or
- makes the board move unstably.

The interesting part is what happens over training. Early on, almost every episode ends for one of the stance reasons. As the policy learns to keep its footing, those terminations get rarer, and the later ones (unstable board motion) start to dominate. The authors describe this as an implicit curriculum: first learn to stand on the thing, then learn to move with it. Nobody wrote that curriculum down. It falls out of the termination list.

The abstract gives no success rates or comparisons, so read it as a method story, not a benchmark win.

**Why this matters for your walking phase.** Your first locomotion env will have terminations: base height too low, torso tilt too large, maybe a contact on anything other than the feet. People usually copy these from an example repo and never touch them again. Treat them like rewards instead:

- **A termination is the strongest penalty you have.** It costs the robot all the future reward it would have collected, and that loss scales with your discount factor and how much is left of the episode. That's why it shuts down exploits fast, and also why too many terminations make a robot timid. If your policy learns to stand still and never take a step, check whether every step risks an episode end.
- **Name the exploit, then block it.** "Both feet on the board" is the skateboard version of the shuffling, foot-dragging, or knee-walking gaits you'll see. When the policy finds a cheap trick, a precise termination is often cleaner than another reward term with a weight to tune.
- **Log why episodes end.** Plot each termination reason over training. The shift from "fell over" to "tilted too far while walking fast" is your curriculum showing up, and it tells you where the policy is stuck far better than mean reward does.
- **Bootstrap correctly on timeouts.** When an episode ends only because the time limit ran out, the robot didn't fail, so the value target should still include an estimate of future reward. Mixing that up with real failures is one of the most common silent PPO bugs. Most frameworks have a flag for it. Check that yours is set.

The bigger idea: "what counts as failure" is part of the reward design, and it might be the part you change least often while it does the most work.

## Robot learning lesson: a benchmark you can actually train on

**[LocoMuJoCo: A Comprehensive Imitation Learning Benchmark for Whole-Body Control](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26293)** (Al-Hafez, Tateo, Kaidanov, Bhatt, Zelik, Luo, Peters), submitted 19 November. Code: [github.com/robfiras/loco-mujoco](https://github.com/robfiras/loco-mujoco), MIT licence.

If you've worked through the last week of posts, you've seen motion imitation everywhere: tracking LAFAN1 clips, behaviour priors, retargeted human motion. LocoMuJoCo is a place to get your hands dirty with all of it in one codebase:

- **16 bodies.** 9 humanoids, 3 (musculo-)skeletal models and 4 quadrupeds, each humanoid with a curated mocap dataset.
- **Retargeting built in.** It retargets from SMPL (AMASS) and LAFAN1, and between robots. That last one matters for a custom robot: once you have a MuJoCo model of your humanoid, you can retarget existing motion onto it instead of collecting your own.
- **One API, CPU or GPU.** Plain MuJoCo on CPU, or MJX and MuJoCo Warp on GPU, with compact JAX implementations of RL and imitation learning algorithms you can compile end to end.
- **Swappable pieces.** Observations, rewards, control modes, terrains and domain randomisation are plug-ins.
- **A path to hardware.** A companion repo, loco-mujoco-real, handles deployment to real robots.

**A weekend exercise.** Pick the humanoid closest to yours in size, and train a plain walking imitation policy on its mocap. Then swap out one piece at a time, the reward or the terrain or the randomisation, and watch what breaks. You'll learn more about what each piece does in a weekend of that than from a month of reading papers. When the club's robot has a MuJoCo model, try retargeting onto it. If the retargeted motion looks wrong (feet sliding, knees bending the wrong way), you've found a modelling problem before it reaches the hardware.

## New papers worth reading

- **[Learning optimal gaits and styles for terrain-adaptive quadruped locomotion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25958)** (Bellegarda, Ijspeert, EPFL; [project page](https://guillaume10.github.io/terrain-gaits)). RL on top of oscillator networks modelled on spinal central pattern generators learns 11 quadruped gaits. A second policy picks the best one for whatever you're optimising: energy cost, base stability or joint smoothness. The finding: every gait is the best choice somewhere, for some mix of terrain, metric and speed. It transfers zero-shot to a robot about five times heavier, and one extra pitch-tracking reward gets it walking on two legs. The idea to steal: "best gait" depends on the metric you choose, so decide what you're optimising before you argue about how the robot walks.
- **[Awaken-HI: Scaling Humanoid Intelligence with Whole-Body Human Experience](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26420)** (EngineAI; [project page](https://engineai-robotics.github.io/Awaken-HI)). About 50,000 hours of egocentric stereo video paired with whole-body human motion, across more than 100 skills. A loco-manipulation VLA predicts both the human motion and the retargeted robot actions through a shared action expert. They report a scaling law, where more human data means lower robot action error, and 93.75% mean success on eight real tasks on their T800, ahead of GR00T N1.7 and pi0.5. Remember this one for the "teach it tasks" phase.
- **[Hindsight Self-Distillation: Teaching Robots to Act from the Present](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25777)** (He, Liu, Shi and others). During training, a teacher gets to see future frames from the demonstration (what actually happened next), and a student with only current frames learns to match the teacher's visual summary. At test time only the student runs. They report 98.5% on LIBERO and 92.3% on RoboTwin 2.0. It's privileged-information distillation, the same teacher-student idea legged robots use for terrain, applied to the time axis.
- **[Learning Ankle Exoskeleton Control for Reactive Standing Balance Through Neuromusculoskeletal Simulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25480)**. Balance control learned in a simulated human body. A reminder that "robot learning for legs" also covers people's legs, and worth a skim if push recovery ever comes up in the club.

## Founder radar

### Humyn Labs: the data company that sends people, not robots

[Humyn Labs](https://www.therobotreport.com/humyn-labs-raises-47m-to-collect-real-world-data-for-robotics/) raised a 47 million dollar Series A on 18 November: 26 million in equity led by Accel, plus 21 million in venture debt. Founded in early 2026 by Ishank Gupta and Manish Agarwal, it says it already has more than 50 million dollars in annualised revenue after six months.

The product isn't a robot. It's people with proprietary capture rigs recording vision, sound, touch and motion while they do real jobs, through ground partners in more than 20 countries, mostly in Latin America and across South, Southeast and East Asia. A data engine turns the captures into labelled, training-ready datasets with verified provenance, built to each lab's spec.

Read it next to Awaken-HI above. Both bet that human experience at scale is the raw material for robot skills. One is a robot company building its own dataset, the other sells datasets to everybody. If the scaling laws hold, whoever owns cheap, diverse, well-documented human data has the ingredient everyone else has to buy.

The seed for you: "provenance" is doing a lot of work in that pitch. As robot datasets become a commercial product, there's a gap for tooling that proves where a clip came from, who consented, and what's in it. Boring, necessary, and nobody owns it yet.

## Outside the bubble: a billion years, missing

Go to the Grand Canyon and find the layer where roughly 540-million-year-old Cambrian sandstone sits right on top of crystalline rock that is far older. In some places the gap is 200 million years. Elsewhere it's more than a billion. Geologists call it the Great Unconformity, and nobody has had a satisfying explanation for why it shows up across several continents.

A [Nature paper](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261116023403.htm) led by Santosh Dhungana and Nicolas Flament at the University of Wollongong offers one: the deep mantle. Their mantle convection simulations show the old cores of North America, northern Europe and North China being pushed up between about a billion and 540 million years ago. Raised land erodes, and that erosion scraped away a huge stack of rock. Later those regions sank, seas flooded them, and fresh sediment buried and preserved the gap. Where the model predicts uplift and then sinking is where the gap is best documented. The authors say mantle flow may not be the whole story, but it's one explanation you can test.

Why it's worth a minute: a missing record isn't the same as nothing happening. Sometimes the gap is the evidence, and a slow, hidden process explains a dozen local mysteries at once. That applies to debugging, too: when several unrelated-looking failures show up together, look for one slow thing underneath them all, like a drifting clock, a heating motor, or a sensor going stale.
