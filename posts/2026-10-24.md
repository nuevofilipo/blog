# Point at the rung, don't copy the dancer

*2026-10-24*

Saturday, so arXiv is quiet, which makes it a good day to read one paper properly instead of skimming ten. And the paper worth reading comes from about two kilometres from your desk: the Robotic Systems Lab at ETH, Marco Hutter's group, with Vladlen Koltun, has a new method that teaches a Unitree G1 to climb boxes, go up ladders, cross stepping stones and vault, all with one policy and no reference motion at deployment. The trick is to stop handing the robot a full motion clip to copy and hand it something much smaller: a list of where its hands, feet and knees should touch the world next. That is today's lesson. Also: Fanuc's results are in, and its slide deck lists humanoid robot components as a demand driver; a Swiss robot-brain startup raised 30 million dollars at pre-seed; a fall-recovery paper that lets you choose how the robot gets up; a humanoid tested on the Moon (in simulation); and some sobering numbers on Swiss glaciers.

## Robot learning lesson: contacts as the interface

**Two camps, two failure modes.** There are roughly two ways to get a humanoid to move well.

- *Reference-free RL:* give it a velocity command, a reward file and a height map, and let PPO find a gait. It adapts to terrain beautifully, but whole-body skills such as putting a hand on a box and swinging a knee up almost never come out of reward design alone. Exploration simply never finds them.
- *Motion tracking:* retarget human motion capture onto the robot and train a policy to follow the clip joint by joint. The motion looks natural, but the robot is now a mime. Move the box ten centimetres and the clip is wrong.

[Humanoid Mobility via Attention-based Contacts](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31608) (He, Wen, Zhang, Schwarke, Zhang, Qu, Hu, Mueller, Koltun, Hutter) splits the difference. Motion data is used only during training, as a prior. What gets passed from planning to control is not a full pose trajectory but contacts.

**The pieces.**

- *Contact-aware retargeting.* Human clips of climbing, stairs, rough ground and stepping stones are retargeted to the G1, and each frame is labelled with which of nine bodies is touching something: two feet, two knees, two hands, two elbows, the pelvis.
- *A tracker that only sees contacts.* A plain MLP (512-256-128) gets proprioception, a small local height scan, the goal, and nine contact targets, and outputs joint offsets at 50 Hz for PD control at 200 Hz. No joint-angle reference. The ablation is the interesting part: contacts alone are enough, at 98.5% or better success on every reference terrain category. Most of what a motion clip "says" turns out to be "put this limb there".
- *A planner that points instead of guessing.* For each body, a query attends over a 17 by 11 grid of terrain points and picks one, plus a flag for "a new contact is coming within 1.5 seconds". Picking a point from the map, rather than regressing free x, y, z coordinates, means the planner can only choose places that actually exist. That matters a lot for a ladder rung that is a few centimetres thick. The training targets are soft Gaussians over the points (sigma 0.1 m), so a near miss is not punished like a total miss.
- *RL fine-tuning with hindsight.* Then come procedurally generated platforms, gaps, beams, ladders and pallets. The planner proposes, the tracker executes, and successful rollouts are fed back to supervise the planner. Reference clips keep being sampled so earlier skills are not forgotten. This stage adds 54.8 percentage points of success on sparse whole-body terrain, and on eight composite obstacle courses the method averages 92.5% against 58.8% for the MGMT baseline.

It runs fully onboard: Livox MID-360 lidar on the head, lidar-inertial odometry, a neural height map, all on a Jetson Orin.

**Three ideas to steal, even before you can walk.**

- *Choose your interface.* Hierarchical controllers live or die by what passes between the levels. Joint angles are too specific, velocity commands too vague. Contacts are low-dimensional, physically meaningful and easy to check. When your team gets to stage two (tasks), ask the same question about your own skill interface: what is the smallest thing the high level could say?
- *Classify over the map instead of regressing into space.* Whenever the answer is "one of these places", make the network pick from the candidates. It is the same move that made pointer networks work, and it removes a whole class of impossible outputs.
- *Curriculum State Initialization.* The tracker is pretrained by starting episodes near the end of each clip and moving backward over training. You learn the last step of the climb first, when success is close, then the second-to-last. For any long skill where the policy never reaches the end by chance, starting near the goal is a cheap fix worth knowing.

**Honest caveats.** All of this is 2.5D terrain: no hanging, no overhead grips, no manipulation. It does local goal-reaching, not navigation. The comparison against some related methods is only qualitative. And no project page or code yet. Still, it is a direct line from what RSL does in Zurich to what your club will want in a year, so it is worth reading the full HTML version this weekend.

## New papers worth reading

### [RISE: steering how a humanoid gets up](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30061)
Your robot will fall. Most teams treat getting up as one fixed routine. Azulay, Hsieh and Yu (Stella Yu's group) note that one fallen pose allows many ways up, and the right one depends on the scene. RISE learns a vocabulary of recovery movements, a visual diffusion planner predicts future body states as "intent", and task costs like heading, speed or path steer that prediction at test time, without retraining. Shown on a real Unitree G1. [Project page](https://osheraz.github.io/RISE/). The seed: once you train a get-up policy, ask whether you could steer it with a cost at inference instead of training a new one for each situation.

### [Do Earth-trained humanoid controllers walk on the Moon?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30080)
Twelve policies on a simulated G1, varying gravity, terrain and soil model. A nice natural experiment in robustness. Earth-trained policies keep 64 to 75% survival on flat lunar ground; Moon-trained ones cannot stand on Earth at all. NVIDIA's generalist SONIC controller manages at most 10% on the Moon. Rough-terrain training lifts Earth survival from 53% to 98%, and gravity randomization holds up in both worlds. The lesson is not about space: training on harder, more varied conditions transfers downward, rarely upward. Same reason you randomize mass and friction before sim-to-real.

### [Robot priors from non-robotic synthetic data](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31275)
Amanda Prorok's group at Cambridge pretrains a transformer on procedurally generated mathematical abstractions of control, with no robot data at all, and gets non-trivial zero-shot action prediction on real robot tasks through in-context learning. A compute-matched model trained on real data still wins, but only on its own datasets. Cheap data that teaches structure rather than content is an idea with legs.

## Founder radar

### [Enact Intelligence](https://siliconangle.com/2026/10/22/robotics-startup-enact-intelligence-nabs-30m-funding-round/): a Swiss bet on brain and muscles
A Swiss startup (legal name Eta Robotics AG) launched this year by Manuel Yves Galliker, previously an engineer at Physical Intelligence and 1X, has raised a 30 million dollar pre-seed. The angels are an unusual group: Yann LeCun, Mistral's Arthur Mensch and Hugging Face's Thomas Wolf, plus VCs. The team includes former Google DeepMind and Amazon people.

What they are building is the interesting part, because it is two bets at once:

- *Models pretrained from scratch*, not fine-tuned from an open VLA, and meant to work across robot bodies. Their job ads include a pre-training researcher.
- *Their own actuators* that "sense force and touch". They have not said how; SiliconANGLE guesses sensing through motor current, which Google and Allegro have explored. Job ads also mention FPGAs.

Seeds:

- *Full-stack again.* Last week the fashion was model-only companies selling an API. Enact says the brain is limited by what the muscles can feel. If force sensing in the actuator is cheap and good, the policy gets a sense that cameras give it badly.
- *30 million at pre-seed in Switzerland* is a signal that European investors now price physical-AI teams like Bay Area ones, if the founders come out of the right labs. Worth knowing when you think about your own timing.
- They will be hiring. A small, funded Swiss team building models and actuators is about as close to your profile as an employer gets.

## Stocks on the radar

Not investment advice, just names worth watching, with reasons and risks.

- **Fanuc (6954 Tokyo, FANUY): the results are in, and robots were not the story.** For July to September, orders came in at 306.0 billion yen, up 49% from a year earlier. But look at the split: factory automation (CNC controllers) orders more than doubled (+130%), Robomachine was up 72%, and robot orders rose only 12.6% to 103.4 billion. Robots are now just 34% of orders, down from 45% a year ago. Fanuc raised its full-year forecast to 1,013.7 billion yen of sales, its first year above a trillion, and operating income to 240.7 billion. The detail worth underlining: the company's own market slide names "humanoid robot components" in China as one of the forces behind CNC demand, next to AI data centres and semiconductors. In other words, today the humanoid boom pays the companies that make machine tools for robot parts, not yet the classic robot-arm makers. On the robot side, the Americas look better, and automotive investment is "starting to gain momentum". Risk: the profit forecast assumes 150 yen to the dollar for the second half, against 159 in the first half, and China is doing a lot of the lifting. [Fanuc 1H FY2026 results (PDF)](https://www.fanuc.co.jp/en/ir/announce/pdf/2026/reference202609_e.pdf).
- **Teradyne (TER)** reports Tuesday 27 October after the US close. After Fanuc's numbers, the question is sharper: does Universal Robots also show only a modest robot recovery, or is the cobot side growing faster than the industrial-arm side?

## Outside your bubble

### [A fifth of Switzerland's glacier ice, gone in five years](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261022081316.htm)
GLAMOS, the Swiss glacier monitoring network run from ETH and the universities of Fribourg and Zurich, has the 2026 numbers, and ETH put them out again this week. Swiss glaciers lost more than 5% of their remaining volume this year. That is less than the record of 2022, but it brings the five-year loss to nearly 20%. The Rhone, Aletsch and Clariden glaciers had their worst melt on record. Two small ones, Bella Tola in Valais and Griessfirn in Glarus, are simply gone.

The mechanism is a feedback loop worth understanding. Winter 2025-26 was among the ten least snowy on record, and snow is the glacier's sunscreen: white snow reflects sunlight, dark bare ice absorbs it. With thin snow, the ice was exposed early, and by September it was bare even at 3,500 metres. The freezing level sat above 4,000 metres on 76 days, more than twice the usual and a Swiss record. From July to September the glaciers released about 2.2 trillion litres of meltwater, more than four times what Swiss households drink in a year.

That meltwater is the counterintuitive part: right now, melting glaciers ease summer drought, because they are spending savings. The account is finite. Hydropower, Rhine shipping and Alpine farming are all planned around a reservoir that is being drawn down faster than any model assumed twenty years ago. It is a useful pattern far outside climate, too: a system can look healthy precisely because it is consuming the buffer that kept it healthy. Startups burning runway and robots running on a fully charged battery during a demo both know that feeling.
