# Carry the coffee without drifting left

*2026-11-11*

Wednesday. Yesterday's lesson ended with "add symmetry augmentation, it's free sample efficiency." Today arXiv returns with the catch: the moment your humanoid holds something in one hand, it isn't symmetric anymore. A paper on carrying asymmetric loads shows how to keep the mirror trick and still walk straight. Also today: a paper that asks how much of your fancy VLA is really just nearest-neighbour lookup (answer: a lot), a robot that ships as a floor lamp, a lidar company quietly compounding, Demo Night tomorrow, and a dead star with two rings.

## Robot learning lesson: when the mirror lies

**The setup.** Your club's roadmap is walk first, tasks second. Tasks usually mean picking something up and taking it somewhere. In the navigation stack, a high-level planner sends velocity commands ("0.4 m/s forward, turn slightly left") and your locomotion policy executes them. That sounds like a solved interface until the robot carries a 2 kg box in its right hand.

**What goes wrong.** A one-handed load is a *persistent* asymmetric disturbance. It's not a push you recover from; it's a constant torque on the torso. A policy trained without it shows a small, steady bias: it drifts a few centimetres to one side every metre, or turns slightly when told to go straight. Over a 10 m walk, small biases add up to a wall collision. The navigation policy never asked for that drift, so it can't correct it.

**What [ReACT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13929) does** (Wang et al., tested on a Unitree G1):

- *Estimate the wrench, feed it in.* The policy gets an estimate of the force and torque the load puts on the body and uses it to modulate its internal features. Think of it as telling the policy "you're carrying something heavy on the right" instead of making it guess from the wobble.
- *Make it reflection-consistent.* This is the interesting bit. Symmetry augmentation assumes that mirroring the robot's state mirrors the correct action. With a load in the right hand, the naive mirror creates a robot with a load still in the right hand but walking as if it were in the left. That's a wrong training sample. ReACT mirrors the *disturbance* too: a right-hand load becomes a left-hand load in the reflected sample. So symmetry still holds; you've just added the load to the things that get flipped.
- *Cross-load consistency.* During training they run the same velocity command with and without the load and penalize differences in the achieved velocity. The policy learns that "0.4 m/s forward" should mean the same thing regardless of what's in its hand.

**Results.** In simulation: better velocity tracking and less directional bias across a range of asymmetric loads. On the real robot: 40 out of 40 navigation trials reached the goal without collision, with a load in one hand and a fixed, unchanged navigation policy on top. The last detail matters. They didn't retrain the planner to compensate. They fixed the layer below so the planner's assumptions held.

**The general lesson.** Every augmentation is a claim about invariance. "Mirror the robot and the action mirrors too" is true only if *everything* relevant is mirrored, including payloads, terrain tilt, command direction and any asymmetric hardware quirk (one weaker motor, a cable bundle on one side). When an augmentation silently breaks, you don't get an error. You get a policy that's slightly worse in a way that's hard to trace.

**How to use this on your humanoid.**

1. *Write down your mirror map as a function and test it.* Take a state, mirror it twice, check you get the original. Then add one unit test per observation term asking "does this flip?" Commands, gravity vector, angular velocities and any privileged terms like external forces all need explicit handling.
2. *Measure drift now, before tasks.* Command 2 m straight, 10 times, and record the lateral error and heading change. Do it on the unloaded robot first. A biased walker is an asymmetric-hardware bug detector, and you want that baseline before arms start carrying things.
3. *Plan for the arm stage.* When you add manipulation, randomize payload mass and attach point in training from day one, and put an estimated external wrench (or at least arm joint torques) in the observation. It's much cheaper to design the interface now than to retrofit it.

A sibling paper from the same listing: [RedirectMimic](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14049) edits specific arm motions in a whole-body tracking controller without retraining everything. Unwanted motion content drops to 2.7%, versus 58.9% for simply zeroing the reward, with 94.2% joint success on the parts you keep. Worth knowing when you start doing motion imitation and need to remove "the bit where the mocap actor scratched their nose."

## How much of your VLA is kNN?

The most uncomfortable paper of the week for anyone who loves big models: [How Much of Your VLA Is kNN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13160), by Denis Grachev, Damien LaRocque and Omar Moured.

The experiment is almost rude in its simplicity. On LIBERO, the standard manipulation benchmark where modern VLAs now score above 95%, they skip policy training entirely. For each new observation they find the nearest demonstration in the dataset and replay its action chunk. Result: 91.7% success. And under LIBERO-Plus perturbations (lighting, camera shifts and similar), retrieval shows the *smallest* drop of all policies tested.

They then looked inside nine public VLAs. Fine-tuning mostly turned the vision-language backbone into a better retrieval key, and the actions the models output stayed close to the demonstrations. In other words, much of what looks like generalization is very good lookup.

They also propose a metric, Local Action Consistency: do observations that look alike in the model's representation also lead to similar future actions? It's computed offline, predicts retrieval success, and can be used to train a better key. The code is at [sereact/lerobot_knn](https://github.com/sereact/lerobot_knn), built on LeRobot.

**Why you should care.** When your team moves from walking to tasks, a kNN baseline is the first thing to run. It costs an afternoon, and it tells you whether your benchmark is testing generalization or memorization. If kNN gets 90% on your task suite, your task suite is too easy, or your demos already cover it. Either is useful to know before you spend a week of GPU time on a VLA.

**Seed for an idea.** LAC is basically a data-quality metric. A tool that scores a robot dataset ("these 300 demos contradict each other: similar scenes, different actions") would be valuable to every team collecting teleop data right now. That's a small, sellable product.

## New papers worth reading

- **[Few-Shot Residual Adaptation of Pretrained Latent World Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13592)** (Suliga, Vanjani, Nilavadi, Burgard, Lioutikov). Keep a pretrained world model frozen, learn small corrections from prediction errors. With three adaptation episodes on a real robot, success recovers from near zero to 90% under a centre-of-mass shift and 100% under changed friction. Correcting in two places (inside the predictor and on its output) beats either alone. Interesting for the day your robot gets a new battery pack and its CoM moves.
- **[ExRL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13482).** Instead of fine-tuning a long-action-chunk policy's actions, it learns *how long to execute each chunk* before replanning. A tiny, low-dimensional action space for online RL, tested in sim and on real robots. A clever way to adapt a big policy without touching its weights.
- **[AHEADance](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13370).** A G1 dancing to streaming audio, generating motion before the beat arrives. Their "Commit Forcing" cut low-activity time from 83.5% to 10.3%. Mostly fun, but it's a clean example of the generator-plus-tracker architecture you'll see everywhere in humanoid control.
- **[CIALLO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13732).** A shared action codec across eight embodiments that adapts to a new robot from about 10 seconds of recording. 76.7% average real-robot success with 20 demos per task, versus 70.0% for a fully fine-tuned pi0. Relevant if your club's custom humanoid ever wants to borrow skills from public datasets recorded on other robots.

## Founder radar

### SYNCERE: the robot that ships as a lamp

[SYNCERE](https://thenextweb.com/news/syncere-lume-robot-lamp-11m-a16z), a Palo Alto startup founded by University of Toronto alumni Aaron Tan and Angus Fung, raised an 11 million dollar pre-seed led by a16z Speedrun, with Draper Associates and others. Their product, LUME, is a floor lamp with a robot arm. It starts at 2,699 dollars, and it folds laundry, tidies and makes beds. It already made TIME's Best Inventions of 2026 and reports millions of dollars in pre-orders ([Pulse 2.0](https://pulse2.com/syncere-raises-11-million-pre-seed-round-led-by-a16z-speedrun-for-home-robotics)).

The clever part isn't the arm. It's the go-to-market. Everyone agrees home robots need real home data, and nobody can get it because nobody lets a humanoid into their flat. SYNCERE's answer: make the robot useful as a *lamp* on day one, so it earns its place in the room even when the robot part is mediocre. Then collect data in real homes and improve via software updates. The robot is the long-term bet; the lamp is the Trojan horse.

**The seed for you.** Ask of any robotics idea: what's the dumb, already-valuable thing this robot can be while it learns to be smart? A shelf, a camera, a lamp, a cart. The first job buys deployment; deployment buys data; data buys the second job.

## Outside your bubble

### [A dead star with two rings](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261104012250.htm)

In a few billion years the Sun will swell into a red giant, throw off its outer layers and shrink into a white dwarf, an Earth-sized ember. What happens to its planets? A team led by Nicola Pietro Gentile Fusillo (University of Trieste and INAF), publishing in *Nature Astronomy*, found a preview.

Using the X-Shooter spectrograph on ESO's Very Large Telescope, they studied the white dwarf J1328-1450. Debris discs around white dwarfs are known, and they're usually one flat ring of crushed rock. This one has *two* concentric rings with a clear gap between them. The team's explanation: two surviving bodies on separate orbits are sculpting them. The inner one may be a few kilometres across, asteroid-sized. The outer one could be as big as Pluto or the Moon. Neither has been seen directly; they're inferred from the shape they leave, the way you'd infer a ship from its wake.

The chemistry adds a twist. Both rings contain calcium, oxygen, magnesium and iron, the signature of rocky bodies. But the inner ring is rich in magnesium gas and the outer ring has almost none. Either two different planets were torn apart, or one planet was, with its crust in one ring and its deeper layers in the other. A planet, sorted by depth, laid out in orbit.

Why this belongs in a robotics blog: the astronomers never see the objects they're studying. They reconstruct hidden state (bodies, orbits, compositions) from indirect, noisy measurements of their effects. That's state estimation, the problem behind every walking robot that infers its own velocity from an IMU and joint encoders. The universe is one long partially observable environment, and astronomers have been doing belief-state inference for centuries.
