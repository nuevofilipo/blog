# Learn the physics, then differentiate through it

*2026-10-21*

The standard robot learning recipe is the same everywhere: thousands of simulated robots in parallel, PPO, and a lot of GPU hours. PPO never looks inside the simulator. It tries an action, sees a reward, and nudges the policy in a direction that worked on average. That is robust, but it wastes most of what the simulator knows. Today's lesson is about the alternative that keeps coming back, now with a striking result from Berkeley: a humanoid tracking policy trained in 3 hours on one GPU that beats a baseline trained for 7 days on 128 GPUs. The trick is to learn a model of the physics and take gradients through it. Also: a motion retargeter 136 times faster than the usual tools, a $90 million seed for a robot brain you call over an API, NVIDIA putting money into German robotics, two NVIDIA internships, a big earnings night, and why your relatives on statins have sore legs.

## Robot learning lesson: gradients through a learned world model

**What PPO is actually doing.** Model-free RL treats the simulator as a black box. To find out whether "bend the knee a bit more" is good, it has to try it in many environments and average the returns. The gradient estimate is noisy, which is why you need huge batch sizes and why a walking policy takes hours or days. Your team will feel this the moment you move from "walk forward" to "track a reference motion" or "carry something".

**The alternative: analytic policy gradients.** If you had a dynamics function that is differentiable, you could roll the policy forward a few steps, compute the tracking error, and backpropagate through the whole chain: error, then the next state, then the dynamics, then the action, then the policy weights. That is backpropagation through time, exactly like training an RNN. One rollout gives you an exact gradient instead of a noisy estimate. In principle that is orders of magnitude more sample-efficient.

**Why nobody just does that.** Two reasons:

- *Real simulators are not nicely differentiable.* Contacts make dynamics discontinuous. A foot is either touching the ground or not. Differentiable simulators exist (Brax, MJX, Warp, Newton), but gradients through contact are often useless or explode.
- *Long horizons blow up.* Backpropagating through hundreds of steps of a chaotic system gives exploding or vanishing gradients, the same problem RNNs had.

**The SuperMimic idea.** Instead of differentiating the simulator, learn a neural network that imitates it:

1. Run a normal rigid-body simulator and log transitions (state, action, next state).
2. Fit one differentiable world model to all of them. The neural net is smooth where the simulator is not, which is a feature: its gradients are informative even around contact.
3. Train the tracking policy by unrolling the world model for a *short* imagined horizon and backpropagating the tracking loss. Short horizons keep gradients stable.
4. Keep both in one loop: as the policy improves, it visits new states, the simulator labels them, and the model gets better where the policy needs it.

The real simulator stays the source of truth. The world model is just a smooth, fast stand-in that gives good gradients.

**The numbers.** On a benchmark of 129,514 human motions, SuperMimic fails on 25, against 179 for a baseline trained with 1000 times more GPU-hours, and it reaches zero failures after about 10 hours on two GPUs. The model's prediction error stays flat as the motion data grows from 1 to 264 hours, so one model covers everything instead of needing a specialist per motion. And it runs on a real Unitree G1, including acrobatic clips. Authors include Pieter Abbeel and Koushil Sreenath, and it is a CoRL 2026 oral.

**What to take for your team.**
- *Do not switch your first walking policy to this.* For plain velocity-tracking locomotion, PPO in Isaac Lab or MuJoCo Playground works and is well documented. Get the robot walking with the boring tool first.
- *Remember it for stage two.* When you move to motion tracking (imitating human clips for tasks), compute becomes your bottleneck. That is exactly where this method shines.
- *Log your transitions now.* Whatever you train, save (state, action, next state) from simulation. It costs disk space and nothing else, and it is the training set for a world model later.
- *Know the risk.* A policy trained on a learned model will exploit the model's mistakes. SuperMimic avoids this by keeping horizons short and refreshing the model with real simulator data. If you ever try this yourself, those two things are not optional.

Further reading: [SHAC, Accelerated Policy Learning with Parallel Differentiable Simulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.07137), the paper that made short-horizon analytic gradients practical, and [DreamerV3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.04104) for the world-model side of the family.

## New papers worth reading

### [SuperMimic: Scaling Humanoid Whole-Body Control with One Differentiable World Model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28480)
Covered in the lesson. The seed: if training cost drops by 1000 times, whole-body control stops being something only big labs can afford. A student club with one gaming GPU could train policies that needed a cluster a year ago.

### [FRDV: Fast Motion Retargeting Pipeline for Scalable Humanoid Robot Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28470)
Before any motion-tracking policy can learn from human motion capture, someone has to map a human skeleton onto your robot's joints. That step is called retargeting, and it is usually slow and full of artifacts like feet sliding or sinking into the floor. FRDV reaches up to 9,156 frames per second, about 136 times faster than GMR and ProtoMotions, then optionally checks each motion against full physics and fixes foot skating and floating. The authors evaluate it the right way: by training tracking policies on each retargeter's output and comparing results, with transfer to a G1. For a custom robot this matters a lot. You cannot download G1 motion datasets for your own robot; you will have to retarget everything yourselves.

### [PACT-VLA: verifying action chunks during inference delay](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28106)
VLAs output chunks of actions, and while the next chunk is computing, the robot keeps moving. PACT-VLA generates several candidate chunks and picks the one that agrees best with a fresh observation, without any training. Real-robot success with pi0.5 goes from 60% to 82%. Useful context for later: latency is not a deployment detail, it changes what the policy should output.

### [Action Influence Maximization for world models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27387)
A familiar failure: an action-conditioned world model learns to predict the future from the images and ignores the actions. This paper collects data actively to force the model to pay attention. 500 active episodes on top of 3,000 demos match a model trained on 300,000 expert episodes, an 85 times reduction. It is the data side of today's lesson: a world model is only as good as what you show it.

## Founder radar

### [Dream Labs raises a $90 million seed for a robot brain sold over an API](https://shattered.io/dream-labs-90m-seed-robot-foundation-model-2026/)
A San Francisco startup founded by former NVIDIA robotics researchers, announced 19 October. B Capital led, with Kindred, Unusual Ventures, SV Angel and Wing among others. Their model, Dream-1, is trained partly on videos of people doing everyday tasks rather than only on teleoperation data. The product is the Dream API: send a camera feed and a plain-English instruction, get motor commands back. They claim more than 20 hardware partners. Big caveat: no public benchmarks, no latency figures, and every performance claim comes from the company.

Count the "robot brain over an API" companies this month: Physical Intelligence opened its API, Ultra rents PI's model for its packing robots, and now Dream Labs launches with an API from day one. When three teams sell the same layer, it becomes a commodity soon after. The interesting questions are the layers around it:

- *Evaluation.* If you rent a brain, how do you know it works on your task before you buy a robot? Whoever builds the "Consumer Reports" for robot models has customers on both sides.
- *Latency and edge.* An API in the cloud is fine for packing, not for balance. Someone has to run these models on the robot. See PACT-VLA above for why that is hard.
- *Embodiment adapters.* Each brain claims to handle unseen hardware. The work of making a specific robot look like something the model understands is a services business today and a software product tomorrow.

## Outside your bubble

### [Why statins make muscles hurt: three molecules wedging a door open](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261019093447.htm)
More than 200 million people take statins to lower cholesterol, and a meaningful share of them stop because their muscles ache. Nobody knew exactly why. A team at the University of British Columbia, with the University of Wisconsin-Madison, now has a picture of it, literally: using cryo-electron microscopy, they imaged atorvastatin bound to RyR1, the channel that releases calcium inside muscle cells when they contract.

What they saw is oddly mechanical. Three statin molecules fit into one pocket of the channel. The first one binds while the channel is closed and makes it easier to open. The next two hold it open. Calcium then keeps leaking into the muscle cell when it should not, and over time that damages the cell. The study is in *Nature Communications*. Nothing changes for patients yet, but a precise structure is what drug designers need to make a statin that still lowers cholesterol and leaves muscle alone.

Why it is here: this is a debugging story. A side effect that hundreds of millions of people have lived with for decades was a hidden interaction between a drug and a component it was never meant to touch. Every system with many parts has these. Robots too: the controller that works perfectly until a cable heats up, or a motor driver that slows the IMU readings. The lesson is the method: when a side effect is common and unexplained, stop arguing about it and get a direct image of what is happening.
