## Robot learning lesson: motion tokens, a shared vocabulary for bodies

**The problem.** Your walking policy will soon be the bottom layer of something bigger. Phase two (tasks) needs a layer on top that decides *what motion* the body should produce, and the motion-tracking lesson from earlier covered how the bottom layer follows it. The open question is what the top layer outputs. Raw joint angles at 50 Hz are high-dimensional, noisy, and specific to one robot. Human motion data, which is where most of the useful behavior lives, is in a different body entirely.

**What [Social Humanoid](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08407) does.** Vongani Maluleke, James Ni and colleagues build a two-layer system for a 29-DoF G1 that reacts to a human partner:

- *A shared codebook.* Human poses and robot poses each go through their own encoder, but both are snapped (vector-quantized) to the same learned dictionary of discrete tokens, updated with an exponential moving average. Each body has its own decoder back out. The result: "raise right arm for a high-five" is the same token whether a human or the G1 does it. That's what makes 260 hours of human mocap usable as robot training data.
- *A generator that predicts both people.* On top sits a Diffusion Forcing Transformer that predicts future tokens for the human and the robot together. In diffusion forcing, every future frame gets its own noise level, so the model can be clean about the near future and vague about the far future, and roll forward autoregressively. Because it models the partner too, it can keep acting sensibly when the partner is briefly occluded.
- *A tracker underneath.* The generated motion is executed by SONIC, Nvidia's general whole-body tracking controller. The high level never touches torques.

**The retargeting trick is the underrated part.** To build the dataset they retarget in two passes. Pass one maps the human to the G1 with contact-adaptive weights, so joints that matter for the interaction (the hand that's about to touch someone) get more accuracy than joints that don't. Pass two "co-morphs" the *partner* to the G1's shorter body so that reach, contact and relative scale still make sense. Naive retargeting gives you a robot shaking hands with air ten centimetres to the left. The result is a 260-hour, 28.1-million-frame Human-Humanoid Interaction benchmark built from CHI3D, Hi4D, Harmony4D, Inter-X and Embody3D.

**Honest caveats.** The project page shows 14 hardware demos (handshakes, hugs, waves, bows, walking together) but no quantitative real-robot numbers, and code and data are marked "soon". Treat this as a design pattern, not a benchmark result.

**Why this matters for your team.**

1. *Retargeting is your first data problem.* If you plan to use human mocap for style rewards or motion tracking on your custom robot, its proportions will differ from both humans and the G1. Weighting joints by what matters for the task (feet during walking, hands during manipulation) is a cheap idea you can copy into any retargeting optimizer today.
2. *Keep the interface clean.* Walking policy at the bottom, a motion-level command interface on top. If your walking controller can track a short window of reference poses (not just a velocity command), you can later swap the top layer from a joystick to a learned generator without retraining the legs.
3. *Learn the word "codebook".* VQ tokenization is how motion, images and actions become inputs to transformers. If you understand one VQ-VAE end to end, half of the 2026 robot foundation model papers stop being mysterious. A weekend exercise: train a tiny VQ-VAE on your robot's own sim rollouts and look at what the tokens mean.

## New papers worth reading

### [La-JEPA: a small policy in a big encoder's feature space](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08156)
Mateo Clémente and colleagues freeze a pretrained V-JEPA 2.1 video encoder and train a compact policy transformer from scratch in its feature space. During training it predicts future features as well as actions; at inference it outputs only actions. It reports state of the art among policies without embodied pretraining on RoboTwin 2.0 and LIBERO-Plus, and it is much smaller and faster than Fast-WAM. The pattern matches yesterday's STEP: predict the future as a training-only regularizer, then throw the predictor away at deployment. Code is out: [github.com/mt-clemente/lajepa](https://github.com/mt-clemente/lajepa).

### [Latent RL priors: let RL learn the hand, let demos teach the eyes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08458)
Yixiao Wang, Masayoshi Tomizuka and colleagues distill large-scale simulated RL rollouts into a flow-matching action decoder conditioned on proprioception and a latent task context. Then they freeze the decoder and train only a visual module that maps images to that context, using a handful of demonstrations. It beats full fine-tuning of pi0.5 and GR00T N1.7 when data is scarce, matching full-data baselines with 10x fewer demos on door lifting and 5x fewer on bimanual assembly. Why read it: it's a clean recipe for phase two. Your walking RL already gives you a strong low-level prior; the question is how little real data you need on top. [Project page](https://yixiaowang7.github.io/latent-rl-prior-page/).

### Also new
- [SLA, arXiv 2611.08373](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08373): continues pretraining a pi0.5 policy while aligning its intermediate visual embeddings to a frozen 3D perception model. The extra parts are used only during training, so the deployed VLA is unchanged.
- [GPWM, arXiv 2611.08338](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08338): a generative world model that represents the scene as 3D particles, covering rigid, deformable, cloth and granular stuff. 31% lower prediction error on average across six physical domains, and planning for pushing and picking.
- [T3R, arXiv 2611.08413](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08413): training-free token pruning for VLAs. It drops 85% of scene-camera tokens on LIBERO-Spatial with roughly unchanged success and up to 3.3x faster LLM forward passes. Relevant the day you try to run a VLA on the robot's own computer.

## Outside your bubble

### [A third kind of magnet](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261104012256.htm)
You learned two kinds of magnetism at school, or one and a half. Ferromagnets (fridge magnets) have their atomic spins lined up, so they have a net field. Antiferromagnets have neighbouring spins pointing in opposite directions, so the fields cancel. A SLAC-led team has now published in *Science* what it calls solid experimental evidence for a third class, altermagnets, in manganese telluride, a material that had long been filed as an ordinary antiferromagnet.

How they saw it: at the European Synchrotron in Grenoble they shone left-handed and right-handed circularly polarized X-rays at MnTe and found it scatters the two differently. That "handedness" in the magnetic excitations is the fingerprint altermagnetism predicts and plain antiferromagnetism doesn't. They ran it in 2024 and repeated it in 2025 to be sure.

Why anyone outside physics should care: ferromagnets are easy to read but easy to disturb, which is bad for stored bits. Antiferromagnets are robust but hard to read. Altermagnets promise both: zero net magnetization, so they're stable, yet they can produce spin-polarized currents that carry information. That's a path to denser, more efficient memory, which matters a lot when data centers are the biggest line item in AI. Devices are still far off; the next step is controlling these states with light and electricity.

The meta-lesson fits robotics too. MnTe was "known" for decades. A new category appeared not because the material changed but because someone asked a sharper question with a better instrument. Your robot will have its own MnTe: a behavior you've already labeled and stopped looking at.
