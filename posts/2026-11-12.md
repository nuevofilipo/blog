# When the brain is slower than the legs

*2026-11-12*

Thursday. Your club's roadmap has two chapters: make the robot walk, then make it do things. Chapter two hides a timing problem that nobody warns you about. The walking policy runs at 50 Hz and answers in a millisecond. The model that decides *what* to do (a VLA, a diffusion policy, anything with a vision backbone) needs 100 to 300 ms per answer. One part of the robot thinks slowly and another reacts fast, and if you wire them together naively the robot stutters, pauses, and jerks every time the slow model finishes thinking. A NeurIPS workshop paper this week treats this as a control problem rather than a learning problem, which is the right way to see it. That's today's lesson. Also: a world action model hitting 99.5% on LIBERO, a passive walker that argues for soft ankles, a startup turning furniture renders into robot training data, a 40-hour physical AI hackathon in Zurich with 10,000 euros in prizes (finally, something new on Luma), two fresh internships, and why your left brain moves your right hand.

## Robot learning lesson: asynchronous inference, or how to keep moving while you think

**The naive loop.** The simplest way to run a learned manipulation policy is synchronous: take an image, run the model, get an action chunk (say 50 future joint targets), execute it, stop, take a new image, run the model again. Action chunking was popularised by ACT (Action Chunking Transformer) and is now standard in VLAs like π0 and SmolVLA, because predicting many steps at once gives smoother motion and fewer compounding errors than predicting one step at a time.

The problem is the "stop" in the middle. While the model computes the next chunk, the robot either freezes or keeps executing stale actions. On a fixed arm, freezing just costs time. On a humanoid holding a box while balancing, a 200 ms freeze in the upper body is a disturbance the legs have to absorb, and it makes your demo look like a buffering video.

**The async fix.** Run inference in parallel with execution. When the current chunk is, say, 70% done, start computing the next one from the latest observation. When it arrives, switch over. Hugging Face's LeRobot ships exactly this as an "async inference" mode for SmolVLA, with the policy on a GPU server and a thin client on the robot.

**The new problem: seams.** The new chunk was computed from an observation taken 200 ms ago, and the robot has moved since. Its first action may not line up with where the robot is now. If you just switch over, you get a jump in the joint targets at every boundary. A position-controlled joint with stiff PD gains turns that jump into a torque spike. You can hear it, and on a humanoid you can feel it in the balance.

There are three families of fixes, and they're worth knowing because they come up in every robot learning codebase:

1. **Blend the overlap.** ACT's temporal ensembling averages all chunks that predict the same timestep, weighting older ones more. It's simple and smooth, but averaging can produce motions no single chunk proposed, like averaging "go left of the cup" and "go right of the cup" into "hit the cup."
2. **Constrain the generator.** Physical Intelligence's real-time chunking (RTC) treats the next chunk as an inpainting problem: the actions that will run while the model thinks are frozen, and the flow-matching policy fills in the rest so it fits that prefix. The seam goes away because the model is forced to agree with the past. This needs a generative policy and changes inference, but not training.
3. **Smooth after the policy.** Treat the stream of predictions as a reference signal and put a controller between the model and the motors. This week's paper takes this route.

**The paper.** [Control-Aware Command Generation for Asynchronous Control and Action Inference](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11089) (Takayuki Osa and Kazuki Ota, 8th Robot Learning Workshop at NeurIPS 2026) states it clearly: generating continuous target positions from *discontinuous* model predictions is a tracking control problem. The policy proposes waypoints, and a low-level command generator produces a smooth, feasible trajectory that tracks them, so jumps get absorbed instead of passed to the motors. Their second trick is **adaptive action skipping**: when the model is confident (low variance across its predicted positions), skip ahead through the chunk and move faster; when it's uncertain, slow down. Tested with ACT and OpenVLA-OFT on a bimanual robot, they report substantial speedups with task success held. The abstract gives no exact numbers, so read the PDF before quoting any.

**Why this matters for your humanoid.** You'll eventually have a three-layer stack: a slow task policy (1 to 10 Hz), a whole-body or locomotion policy (50 Hz), and PD loops on the motor drivers (1 kHz or more). Every boundary between layers runs at a different rate, and each one is a place where seams can form. Three practical rules:

- **Never send raw chunk boundaries to a stiff joint.** At minimum, interpolate. Better, run a small tracking filter (a critically damped second-order system per joint is enough to start with) between policy output and PD targets.
- **Log the action discontinuity at chunk boundaries** (the norm of the jump between the last executed action and the first new one). It's the cheapest sim-to-real health metric you'll find. If it's large in sim, it will be worse on hardware.
- **Train the lower layer to expect a jittery upper layer.** If the walking policy will eventually take arm targets from a VLA, add realistic upper-body noise and delayed, step-wise arm commands during locomotion training. A walking policy that has only seen smooth arm motion in sim will be surprised by real chunk boundaries.

The big idea: inference latency is not an ML problem you fix with a smaller model. It's a systems problem, and the fix often lives in the controller, not the network.

## New papers worth reading

- **[LaST0.5: A Generalist World Action Model with Interaction-Centric Latent Reasoning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14048).** A world action model (a model that predicts both future world states and actions) with a Mixture-of-Transformers design and an extra "latent expert" that focuses on local robot-object interaction rather than the whole scene. It's pretrained on over 14,000 hours of robot data and reports 99.5% on LIBERO and state-of-the-art results on ten RLBench tasks. Given yesterday's paper showing kNN can nearly match VLAs on LIBERO, treat 99.5% as "the benchmark is saturated" and look for the RLBench and real-robot numbers instead. The interesting idea is architectural: the world model and the action model don't need the same view of the scene.
- **[WAM is capable in BEHAVIOR-1K](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13818).** The second-place solution to the 2026 BEHAVIOR Challenge (100 household mobile manipulation tasks), built on NVIDIA's Cosmos 3 world action model. It's useful as a practical engineering report: LoRA adapters per task group, a separate progress estimator that routes to skill-specific policies, and "rolling inpainting" of action chunks, which is the RTC idea from the lesson above in a competition setting. They had to hit 1 FPS inference, so they also show what you give up to meet a latency budget.
- **[Ego Guidance](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13685).** Can human egocentric video help a robot policy without any action labels? Their architecture (SPAR) adds a future-frame prediction objective next to action prediction on a pretrained VLM, and they find the video-learned representations improve out-of-distribution robot tasks, with gains that grow with video scale. Relevant for chapter two: human video is the cheapest manipulation data your club will ever get.
- **[DexSeed](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14092).** Takes human hand-object interaction data and expands it into many physically executable robot demonstrations by keeping the grasp, varying the object and placement, and realising the motion in simulation with RL-controlled fingers. It's data augmentation for dexterous VLAs, and it avoids teleoperation.
- **[Softness is something you also need!](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13779)** A theory paper, no hardware. It starts from McGeer's passive compass-gait walker, adds compliant knees and ankles, and estimates the volume of the "viable" region of state space (the states from which the walker keeps walking) with Monte Carlo and kernel methods. The argument: compliance enlarges that region, so a soft body makes control *easier*, not harder. No numbers in the abstract, so read it as a perspective. But for a team with a custom-built robot, it's a useful reminder that ankle stiffness is a design choice that changes how hard your RL problem is, not just a mechanical detail.

## Founder radar

### Physicl: furniture renders become robot gyms

[Physicl](https://runtimewire.com/article/physicl-raises-10m-ecommerce-3d-robot-training-data) raised a 10 million dollar seed round led by Inovia Capital, announced 6 November. The founder is Alex de Vigan, who previously built nfinite, a company that makes photorealistic 3D product images for e-commerce (the "this sofa in a sunny living room" pictures you see online). Physicl reuses that 3D pipeline to generate labelled synthetic scenes for world models and robot training, and [FourWeekMBA's write-up](https://fourweekmba.com/ai-physicl-raises-10m-to-build-robots-rare-failure-cases/) frames the pitch around building the rare failure cases robots almost never see in real data.

**The seed for your brain.** This is a classic pivot pattern: an asset built for market A (thousands of accurate 3D models of real household objects) turns out to be the bottleneck in market B (robot training data). Questions worth asking about your own work:

- What does your club produce as a by-product that someone else would pay for? A calibrated actuator model of a specific motor, or a validated URDF and sim setup of a custom humanoid, are things other teams spend weeks building.
- Which old industries are sitting on 3D or motion data they don't value? Game studios, film VFX houses, ergonomics labs, physiotherapy clinics with motion capture. Each is a possible Physicl.

## Outside your bubble

### [Why your left brain moves your right hand](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261103003146.htm)

Your left hemisphere controls your right hand. Your right hemisphere sees the left half of your visual field. Pain from your left foot is processed on the right. Almost all vertebrate wiring crosses the midline, and nobody has a fully satisfying explanation of why. Crossing costs longer wires and extra complexity, so evolution should have needed a reason.

Martin Wessendorf (University of Minnesota) and Daniel Wang propose in *Frontiers in Neuroanatomy* that maybe there isn't a reason, at least not a current one. Their hypothesis: the crossing began more than 500 million years ago in the visual system of early jawless fish. Every living vertebrate has an optic chiasm (where the optic nerves cross) and an optic tectum organised around the opposite visual field, which suggests both were present in Cambrian ancestors. Later systems for touch, pain and movement were then built on top of that crossed layout and kept the crossing. The corticospinal tract, which gives primates fine finger control, may be a repurposed older sensory pathway. It's a hypothesis, not a result, and the authors propose testing it by looking for traces of an optic chiasm in fossil skulls of jawless fish.

Why it belongs here: this is legacy code. The crossing isn't elegant, just early, and once a few hundred million years of systems depend on an interface, you never refactor it. The same thing happens in robot software stacks. The coordinate frame convention or message format chosen in week two of a project ends up living for years because everything was built on top of it. Choose your club's interfaces (frame conventions, joint ordering, action normalisation) carefully now. In three years, they'll be your optic chiasm.
