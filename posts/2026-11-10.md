# Walk by feel, not by GPS

*2026-11-10*

Tuesday, and arXiv has a paper that reads like it was written for your club. Nico Bohlinger and Jan Peters (TU Darmstadt) teach humanoids to walk in simulation with only 14 minutes of experience, and they do it by changing one thing most of us never question: what the reward measures. Also today: Apple quietly built a humanoid stack that runs on a Mac mini strapped to a G1's back, a Chinese data company raised 100 million dollars to record humans doing chores, Unitree's stock moved on a paper from Cupertino, and the brain science of why you learned to like black coffee.

## Robot learning lesson: reward what the robot can actually feel

**The problem.** Almost every walking policy you'll see is trained on a velocity-tracking reward: "move the base at 0.5 m/s forward, here's your bonus for being close." In simulation that's free, because the simulator knows the true base velocity. On hardware it's not. Base velocity is something you *estimate*, from leg kinematics plus IMU, or from a mocap room, or from a learned estimator. So the reward you trained on is built on a quantity the real robot never measures directly. That's fine for sim-only training, but it gets in the way the day you want to fine-tune on hardware, and every bias in your estimator becomes a bias in your behavior.

**What [STRIDE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08857) does.** Instead of rewarding the robot for tracking a base velocity, it rewards following a *commanded acceleration profile*. Acceleration is what an IMU measures directly. So the reward can be computed from onboard sensing alone: no mocap, no calibrated state estimator. Three ingredients come with it:

- *An off-policy learner.* They use XQC, an off-policy RL algorithm, instead of the PPO you're probably using in Isaac Lab. Off-policy methods reuse old experience from a replay buffer many times, which is why sample counts can drop so dramatically.
- *High-gain joint position controllers.* The policy outputs joint targets and stiff PD loops track them.
- *Left-right symmetry augmentation.* Every transition is mirrored, so the robot learns its left and right legs from the same data, which roughly doubles the useful experience and pushes toward a symmetric gait.

**The numbers.** In simulation, STRIDE learns to walk on both the Unitree G1 and the Booster T1 within 14 minutes of samples (simulated time, not wall-clock compute). The high-gain controllers and symmetry augmentation together cut falls during training by 81%, and without them learning takes over an hour.

**Why "falls during training" is the number to stare at.** In simulation, a fall costs you nothing. On a real robot, a fall costs a gearbox, a cracked shell, or a week of your club's time. A method designed around IMU-only rewards, few samples and few falls is clearly aimed at the real world. Read it as a step toward training (or fine-tuning) walking *on the robot itself*. The paper as listed reports simulation, so don't assume hardware results until you've read the PDF.

**How to use this on your custom humanoid.**

1. *Audit your reward for "sim-only" quantities.* Make a two-column list: terms computable from onboard sensors (IMU, joint encoders, foot contact if you have it) and terms that need privileged sim state. Every term in the second column is something you can never use for on-robot fine-tuning. Even if you stay with PPO, knowing this split is useful.
2. *Add symmetry augmentation now.* It's a few lines of code if your robot is mirror-symmetric: write the mapping that swaps left and right joints and flips the lateral axes, then mirror observations and actions. It's one of the cheapest sample-efficiency wins in legged RL, and asymmetric limping gaits are a classic first-week problem.
3. *Time-to-walk is a useful team metric.* If your pipeline takes 6 hours to produce a walking policy, you get four experiments a day. At 30 minutes you get twenty. Most of the gait quality in this field comes from iteration count, not cleverness.

A related paper to read alongside: [DKWBC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09605) splits whole-body control the old-fashioned way. Inverse-dynamics feedforward plus PD feedback do the motion tracking, and a learned residual policy only adds the torques needed for balance. One balancing policy then generalizes across motion datasets, payloads and physical interaction, in simulation and on hardware. The abstract gives no numbers, but the design question is worth asking: how much of your policy's job could a model already do? [Project page](https://robbyant-research.github.io/DKWBC/).

## Apple built a humanoid brain, and it runs on a Mac mini

Apple doesn't usually publish robotics. Last week it posted [HALO](https://www.humanoidsdaily.com/news/apple-halo-humanoid-synthetic-data-unitree-g1) ("Perceptive Humanoid Motion from Simulation", arXiv 2611.06908, Siyuan Hu through Vladlen Koltun). It's a clean blueprint for exactly the phase-two stack your team will need: walking first, tasks on top.

**Three layers, three speeds:**
- *Orchestrator*, about every 2 seconds: a 4-bit Qwen3.6-35B-A3B vision-language model that splits "go sit on the sofa" into subtasks and decides when to retry.
- *Motion generator*, about 3 times per second: a frozen Qwen3.5-2B vision encoder plus a 49M-parameter diffusion transformer that plans 5 seconds of whole-body motion.
- *Motion tracker*, at 50 Hz: an RL policy trained in Isaac Lab that follows the plan using depth, proprioception and IMU, and outputs targets for the G1's 29 joints.

The top two run on a Mac mini (M4 Pro, 64 GB) mounted on the robot's back; the tracker runs on the G1's built-in Jetson Orin NX. Fully untethered.

**The data is almost all synthetic.** 1,000 procedurally generated rooms (Infinigen), human-like motions from a text-conditioned motion diffusion model, ray-traced rendering in Isaac Sim through a fisheye camera matched to the real one, and heavy randomization of blur, noise and exposure. That's 162,698 episodes, 296 hours.

**Results:** 91% success over 360 real trials in six unmapped environments across six skills (crawl under tables, sit on sofas, push boxes and so on). The ablations are the real lesson. Training on one room instead of 1,000 drops sitting success from 84% to 43%. Swapping the diffusion decoder for plain regression drops it to 19%. Removing depth from the tracker gives 74%.

**Caveats:** no comparison against other published systems, chained tasks fall to 70%, and none of the skills involve grasping. Code for the pipeline, training and deployment (MuJoCo and real G1) is on [GitHub](https://github.com/apple-aiml-research/ml-halo); weights and dataset aren't listed.

**Takeaway for your team:** the 50 Hz tracker is the piece you'll build first anyway. If it can follow a short window of full-body reference motion, not just a velocity command, everything above it becomes swappable.

## New papers worth reading

- [ForPC, arXiv 2611.08664](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08664): a G1 pushing a 30 kg cart at 0.5 m/s, zero-shot from sim, with no force sensors. A residual policy infers force and state latents from onboard sensing. Up to 68.2% lower cart-velocity error in sim-to-sim. A good model for the first "task" after walking: something that pushes back.
- [Q-OIL, arXiv 2611.08576](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08576) (Natasha Jaques, Abhishek Gupta and colleagues): looked at real human intervention data and found people take over when progress stalls, often with imperfect corrections. Their RL method treats interventions optimistically and reports 10 to 50% higher success in sim and on real robots. Relevant if your team ever does on-robot learning with a person holding the e-stop.
- [Can LLMs debug robots by editing their weights? arXiv 2611.09376](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09376): an LLM edits 65 token-embedding rows in pi0.5 to fix instruction misunderstandings, with no gradients or new demos. Success on 765 held-out paraphrases goes from 86.3% to 92.0%. Small gain, strange and fun idea.
- [VLA Handover, arXiv 2611.08987](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08987): a small on-robot VLA runs by default and hands control to a big server model only when it predicts failure. On LIBERO it gains 11 to 22 points over the small model while cutting server calls by 49 to 77%. A practical pattern for robots with weak onboard compute.

### GenRobot: selling shovels in the robot data gold rush
Beijing-based [GenRobot AI](https://technode.com/2026/11/02/robot-training-data-startup-genrobot-ai-raises-100-million-in-series-a-funding/) disclosed a 100 million dollar Series A+ last week, with strategic investors reported to include SAIC, Baidu and Galbot. That brings it past 200 million in about a year. It doesn't build robots. It builds wearable capture gear (Gen DAS) and pipelines that record humans doing tasks, then sells the data to robot companies. It claims more than 30 AI customers and plans to deliver 10 million hours of data in a single quarter in 2027.

Put that next to Apple's HALO, which got to 91% on almost entirely synthetic data. The two bets are opposite: *the bottleneck is real human data* versus *the bottleneck is better simulation*. One of them will look obvious in hindsight.

Seeds to think about:
- What's the Swiss version of this? ETH has mocap labs, a robotics club and a lot of skilled hands. Who's collecting high-quality data for one narrow, valuable task (lab automation, watchmaking-style precision assembly, hospital logistics) and selling it?
- Data quality probably matters more than hours. A startup that scores and cleans robot demonstrations ("this demo is noisy, this one teaches the policy something") could sell to both camps.
- Notice the investor list: a carmaker, a search giant, a robot maker. Strategics fund picks-and-shovels when they're unsure which robot will win.

## Outside your bubble

### [Why you learned to love bitter coffee](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261108233100.htm)
Nobody likes their first black coffee. Most people who drink it now eventually love it. A UCSF team led by Zachary Knight, publishing in *Nature* ("The neural basis of flavour-nutrient learning"), found a likely reason.

They fed mice sugar water or a milkshake-like liquid directly into the stomach, which separates the nutrients from the taste, and tracked dopamine across the brain. Nutrients triggered a *slow, prolonged* dopamine signal in the anterior basolateral amygdala. That's different from the famous fast dopamine burst, which arrives within seconds of a reward. When they blocked the slow pathway, mice still ate normally but couldn't learn that a flavor predicted calories. When they artificially activated it after a calorie-free flavor, mice came to prefer that flavor. Human PET and fMRI data showed activity in the same region, oddly stronger on the left side in both species, which nobody can explain yet.

So the coffee "starts to taste a lot better," as Knight puts it, because your brain eventually connects the taste with the caffeine that arrives twenty minutes later.

Why this is in a robotics blog: it's a credit assignment problem, solved by biology. The reward (calories, caffeine) arrives long after the action (tasting), and the brain uses a separate, slow learning signal to bridge the gap instead of stretching the fast one. Your walking policy has the same issue in miniature. A stumble at step 12 can cause a fall at step 30, and discounting is a blunt tool for that. Evolution's answer was two reward channels with different time constants. Keep that in mind the next time a reward term "should work but doesn't".
