# Is the robot in simulation the robot on the floor?

*2026-10-10*

Every walking policy is trained on a description file, not on the robot. If the URDF says the shin weighs 400 g when it weighs 900 g, or a knee axis points the wrong way, PPO will happily learn a gait for a robot that does not exist. Today's lesson is a pre-training audit of the robot description for a custom humanoid. Plus a humanoid world-action model pretrained on 10,000 hours of human data, an ETH RSL field report on legged robots in lunar regolith, a boat that learned sim-to-real from a CAD model and a few minutes of data, a warehouse startup built on someone else's foundation model, and a quantum state predicted in 1931 finally seen in a gas.

## Robot learning lesson: audit the robot description before training

**Why this comes first.** The earlier lessons on actuator modeling (6 October) and domain randomization assume the rigid-body model is roughly right. Randomization covers plus or minus 20 percent on a mass; it does not cover a link that is off by a factor of two, a wrong joint sign, or an inertia tensor that is physically impossible. Those errors do not show up as a bad reward. They show up as a policy that walks beautifully in Isaac Lab and falls over in the first second on hardware.

**Where custom-robot descriptions go wrong.** CAD exporters are the usual source, and the failure modes are predictable:

- **Mass from the wrong material.** CAD assigns a default density, so 3D-printed parts come out as solid aluminium or as nothing at all. Motors, batteries, cables and electronics are often missing entirely. Fix: weigh each real link (or each subassembly) and override the masses. Total robot mass in the file should match the scale within a few percent.
- **Inertia tensors.** Check that each one is positive definite and satisfies the triangle inequality (each principal moment is no larger than the sum of the other two). Exporters that zero out off-diagonal terms or that put the inertia at the wrong frame produce links that spin strangely. Simulators silently accept many of these; MuJoCo will refuse some and Isaac Sim will clamp others without telling you.
- **Center of mass.** For a biped the whole-body CoM height and its forward-back position matter more than any single link. A CoM that is 2 cm too far forward changes the ankle torque needed to stand. If you can, measure it on the real robot by balancing it on a knife edge or with two scales under the feet.
- **Joint axes and signs.** Command a small positive angle on every joint, one at a time, in simulation and on the real robot, and film both. Any joint that moves the opposite way needs its axis flipped or its sign changed in the deployment code. Do it for the left and right side separately; mirrored exports are a common source of flipped signs, and the symmetry augmentation from 7 October will amplify the error.
- **Joint limits and zero position.** The zero in the file must be the zero the motor encoders report after calibration, and the limits must be the mechanical hard stops minus a margin. A policy that learns to rest a knee at a limit that the real robot reaches 5 degrees earlier will slam into the stop every step.
- **Collision geometry.** Train with simplified collision shapes (boxes, capsules, spheres), not the visual meshes. Foot geometry deserves special care: the contact patch decides how the foot rolls and where the support polygon ends. A flat box foot with the real sole dimensions is a good default.
- **Armature and damping.** The reflected rotor inertia of a geared motor (rotor inertia times gear ratio squared) is often larger than the link inertia at the distal joints. If the file omits it, the simulated leg swings faster than the real one. Isaac Lab and MuJoCo both have an armature field per joint; fill it in.

**A one-hour audit script.** Load the URDF in MuJoCo or with a URDF parser and print, for every link: mass, CoM, principal moments and the triangle check. Print the total mass and whole-body CoM at the default standing pose. Then, with gravity on and all joints locked at the standing pose, drop the robot from 1 cm and check that it stands without drifting. Finally run a passive test: release all joints with damping only and confirm the robot collapses symmetrically. Asymmetric collapse almost always means a left-right mass or axis error.

**Close the loop with hardware.** Hang the robot on a gantry, command a slow sinusoid on one joint at a time, and overlay the measured joint trajectory on the simulated one with the same PD gains. Mismatch in phase points to armature, damping or motor lag; mismatch in amplitude points to mass, inertia or torque limits. The 9 October post on IDTD covered how to choose these excitation trajectories so each test exposes the parameters you actually care about.

**Tools worth knowing.** [onshape-to-robot](https://github.com/Rhoban/onshape-to-robot) exports URDF and MJCF directly from an Onshape assembly and lets you override masses per part; Rhoban built it for their own humanoids. [MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/stable/modeling.html) can render inertia boxes and the CoM in its viewer, which makes a misplaced link obvious at a glance.

**Practical recommendation.** Before the first walking run, write the audit as a test that runs every time the robot description changes, and keep a short table in the repo: measured mass per link, measured total CoM, verified joint directions. It is boring work that removes an entire class of sim-to-real bugs before they can hide behind the reward function.

## New papers worth reading

### [Being-M0.7: A Latent World-Action Model for Humanoid Robots](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11283)
Submitted 8 October. A three-stage recipe for humanoid loco-manipulation: pretrain a model on more than 10,000 hours of human-centric video and motion data to predict future latent visual states and motion, mid-train it on robot viewpoints and body dynamics, then train a small action expert that reads the frozen prior through gated cross-attention and outputs whole-body commands. It leads the compared baselines on the SIMPLE benchmark and matches the strongest baseline on real Unitree G1 tasks. Why it matters for you: this is a concrete map of the stage after walking, when the club wants the robot to do tasks. Note the structure, a frozen general prior plus a cheap, robot-specific action head, because that is the version a student team could afford to fine-tune. It is also the fourth human-data paper in a week, after HumanVerse-500, Workhorse and Mecka.

### [Toward Lunar Legged Robots: Field Deployment Lessons at LUNA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12276)
Submitted 8 October, from Marco Hutter's group with Hendrik Kolvenbach. ANYmal-D and Magnecko walked on regolith simulant and crater-like terrain during the 2025 LUNA analogue campaign. Locomotion worked, but sinkage and slip degraded it, foot contacts generated dust, and harsh lighting (overexposure, hard shadows, low texture) broke perception. A field report rather than a method paper, and that is the value: it lists what simulation did not model. Read it with today's lesson in mind; the ground is part of the robot description too, and the authors call for regolith-aware policies precisely because the contact model was wrong.

### [Sim-to-Real RL for ASVs using SysID](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12202)
Submitted 8 October. Not a legged robot, but the most relevant pipeline of the week for a custom robot. Starting with unknown dynamics, the authors get initial parameters from a CAD model, refine hydrodynamic and thruster parameters from a short set of real trajectories, train in a parallel simulator, and transfer zero-shot to a BlueBoat for path following and station keeping. [Code is public](https://github.com/UMich-CURLY/ASTRA_RL). Replace "hull" with "leg" and "thruster" with "actuator" and this is the workflow your club needs: CAD prior, a few minutes of real data, identify, train, deploy.

## Founder radar

### [Ultra raises a 50 million dollar Series A for warehouse robots running Physical Intelligence models](https://humanoidsdaily.com/news/ultra-50-million-series-a-warehouse-robots-physical-intelligence)
Announced 9 October. Framework Ventures led, Y Combinator participated; total funding is 62 million dollars. Ultra, from Brooklyn, sells a stationary dual-arm workstation robot for packing, sorting and kitting that plugs into a normal outlet. It does not train its own foundation model: it runs Physical Intelligence's models, and in February reported one full shift packing real orders at 96.4 percent autonomy. Customers pay an integration fee plus a monthly fee, and human operators step in when the model fails.

The founder lesson is the split of labor. Physical Intelligence gets real-world data from robots outside its lab; Ultra gets a frontier model without a frontier research budget and focuses on hardware, installation and customers. Every human intervention becomes training data. This is the Android-for-robots pattern from the 5 October post seen from the app maker's side.

**Idea seed.** If foundation-model companies need deployment partners, the scarce skill is not modeling but picking a workflow where 95 percent autonomy plus remote human fallback is already profitable. A useful exercise: list ten repetitive tasks in Swiss hospitals, labs or workshops, and for each estimate the cost of one human intervention per hundred actions. The ones that still pay are candidate companies.

Also this week: Boston Dynamics named former Amazon AI lead Rohit Prasad as CEO on 7 October ([report](https://humanoidsdaily.com/news/boston-dynamics-rohit-prasad-ceo)), a strong signal that the company sees its future in learned models rather than in hand-built controllers.

## Outside your bubble

### [A quantum prediction from 1931 seen in an ultracold gas](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261006234517.htm)
Reported 9 October. In 1931 Hans Bethe solved a one-dimensional model of interacting particles and predicted "Bethe strings": clusters of particles bound only by their mutual interaction, which can exist only in one dimension. A team at the University of Innsbruck led by Hanns-Christoph Nägerl cooled cesium atoms to a few billionths of a degree above absolute zero, confined them in thousands of thin tubes so they could move only along one line, and switched their interaction from repulsive to attractive. Clusters of six or more atoms formed. Inside the tubes, the strings collided and survived; when the tubes were removed and the atoms could spread in three dimensions, the strings broke and the released binding energy made the atoms expand faster. That difference was the fingerprint. The paper is in Nature Communications.

Two things to take away. First, dimensionality is not a detail: the same atoms with the same forces behave qualitatively differently when constrained to a line, which is a useful intuition for any system, including a robot whose walking is very different once you constrain it to a sagittal plane. Second, the exact solution existed for 95 years; what was missing was an apparatus clean enough to test it. Many good ideas wait for the instrument, not for more theory.
