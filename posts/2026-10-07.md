# Left is right: symmetry and style for a first gait

*2026-10-07*

The last few days built the pipeline: rewards, observations, PPO, actuator models, deployment. Today is about a problem you will see within the first hour of training: the policy walks, but it limps, drags one foot, or uses a gait no human would. Two tools fix most of it. Mirror symmetry, which is nearly free, and adversarial motion priors, which cost more but replace a pile of hand-tuned style rewards. Plus a Sergey Levine paper on why behavior cloning behaves strangely, a European robotics unicorn, and a Nobel for a telescope made of Antarctic ice.

## Robot learning lesson: symmetry and style

**The problem.** A biped is (almost) mirror symmetric, but PPO does not know that. Nothing in the reward says the left leg should do what the right leg did half a step earlier, so the policy often settles on an asymmetric local optimum: one leg does the pushing, the other just catches. It can score well in simulation and look terrible on hardware, where the overloaded side hits torque limits first.

**Tool 1: mirror the data.** Write a function that maps a state to its mirror image, and one for actions. For a humanoid this means:

- Swap left and right joints (left hip pitch becomes right hip pitch, and so on).
- Flip the sign of joints that rotate about axes that flip under mirroring: typically hip roll, hip yaw and ankle roll. Pitch joints usually keep their sign. Check this against your URDF axes rather than assuming.
- In the observations: negate the lateral velocity command and the yaw rate command, negate base angular velocity around x and z, negate the y component of projected gravity. If you use a gait clock, shift its phase by half a period.

Then use it in one of two ways, both studied in [Mittal et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.04359) from ETH's Robotic Systems Lab:

1. **Data augmentation.** Every rollout batch is duplicated with mirrored observations and actions before the PPO update. The policy sees both versions of every experience and learns a symmetric gait faster. One subtlety: the importance ratio for the mirrored samples uses the policy's probability of the mirrored action, which works as long as the task itself is symmetric.
2. **Mirror loss.** Add a penalty on the difference between pi(mirror(s)) and mirror(pi(s)). Softer: it nudges toward symmetry without forcing it.

The RSL-RL library behind Isaac Lab's locomotion examples supports both through a symmetry config on the PPO algorithm, so the main work is writing the mirror function correctly. Test it before training: mirror a state twice and you should get the original back; mirror a recorded walking clip and it should still look like walking. A sign error here produces a policy that is confidently wrong in a way that is very hard to debug later. A third option is building the symmetry into the network architecture itself (equivariant networks), compared against augmentation in [Su et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17320); for a first gait, augmentation is the simpler choice.

**Tool 2: adversarial motion priors (AMP).** Symmetry does not make a gait human-like. The usual fix is to add style rewards one by one: foot clearance, torso uprightness, knee bend, arm swing. Each needs a weight, and they fight each other. AMP ([Peng et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.02180)) replaces them with a learned one. You collect a few clips of reference motion (human motion capture, retargeted to your robot's joints), and train a discriminator that sees pairs of consecutive states (s, s') and tries to tell reference transitions from policy transitions. The policy gets a style reward for fooling it, added to the task reward (track the velocity command). [Escontrela et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15103) showed this transfers to real quadrupeds and produces more energy-efficient gaits than hand-tuned rewards.

Costs to know before you commit: you need retargeted reference data for your custom kinematics (retargeting is its own small project), the discriminator can overpower the task reward so the robot imitates style but ignores commands, and GAN-style training adds hyperparameters. Reference data also has to be physically plausible for your robot, or the policy is pushed toward motions its actuators cannot produce.

**Practical recommendation.** Add mirror augmentation from the first training run; it costs a day of careful work and helps every run after. Keep the gait style rewards from the 2 October post for the first gait. Move to AMP when walking works and the remaining complaints are about how it looks, or when you start the task phase and need many motions without writing rewards for each.

## New papers worth reading

### [Behavioral Cloning Mystery](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07056)
Seohong Park and Sergey Levine, submitted 5 October. Anyone who has trained an imitation policy has run into strange behavior: success rates keep improving long after validation loss says the model is overfitting, and some architectures only work with specific tricks nobody can explain. The authors build OCBench, a GPU-accelerated manipulation benchmark whose scripted demonstrators are designed to have the messy properties of human demonstrations, and reproduce these effects in a controlled setting where hypotheses can actually be tested. Read it before the task phase of the project, when you will likely train on teleoperated demonstrations and need to know which validation metrics to trust.

### [BiGym 2.0: Benchmarking Learned and Agent-Developed Policies for Humanoid Household Manipulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07594)
Zhang et al. (Stephen James's group), submitted 6 October. Twenty whole-body household tasks on a simulated Unitree G1, with 60 human VR demonstrations per task, and a head-to-head of VLA fine-tuning, imitation learning, RL, and programs written by coding agents. VLA fine-tuning did best on average; agent-written programs beat RL on some tasks, notably bimanual reaching; stacking across workspaces, box picking and multi-object transport stayed hard for everyone. Everything is open-sourced, which makes it a ready-made sandbox for the club to compare approaches before collecting its own data.

**Idea seed.** Writing the mirror function by hand is tedious and error-prone, and every lab does it for every robot. A small tool that reads a URDF or MJCF, pairs left and right links by name and geometry, derives sign flips from joint axes, and emits tested mirror functions for observations and actions (plus a visual check that renders a mirrored clip) would be useful to every legged robotics team. It is small enough to build in a weekend for the club's robot, and a good open-source calling card.

## Outside your bubble

### [Nobel Prize in Physics 2026: Francis Halzen and IceCube](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2026/summary/)
Announced yesterday: Francis Halzen of the University of Wisconsin-Madison, "for decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin." Neutrinos barely interact with matter, which makes them perfect messengers from distant violent events (they pass through everything on the way) and nearly impossible to catch. Halzen's idea was to use a cubic kilometer of Antarctic ice as the detector: thousands of light sensors lowered into holes melted deep into the ice at the South Pole watch for the faint flash produced when a neutrino occasionally hits an atom. It opened a new kind of astronomy, seeing the universe in particles instead of light. Worth noticing as an engineer: when the signal is rare, the answer was not a better sensor but an absurdly larger one, built from material that was already there. Read the [CNN explainer](https://www.cnn.com/2026/10/06/science/nobel-prize-physics-francis-halzen-winner-intl) for the story of how it was built.
