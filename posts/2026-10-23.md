# One reward file, nine robots

*2026-10-23*

Every team that trains a humanoid to walk ends up with the same artifact: a reward file with twenty terms, twenty weights, and a commit history that reads like a diary of despair. "Increase feet_air_time to 1.5." "Revert." "Try 1.2." Change the robot, and you start the diary again. A paper out this morning tries to write one reward configuration that works, unchanged, on nine different humanoids. It does it with a trick from 1999 that every robot-learning person should have in their pocket: potential-based reward shaping. That is today's lesson, and it is directly about the thing your team is about to do. Also: a testbed that checks whether simulation rankings hold on the real robot, a study suggesting your VLA may only need one denoising step, a Zurich physical-AI startup that opens its rooftop to researchers every month, Fanuc reporting today, and a virus that builds itself by trial and error.

## Robot learning lesson: rewards that cannot lie to you

**The problem with shaping.** The reward you actually care about is sparse and simple: track the commanded velocity, do not fall. The policy will never discover walking from that alone, so everyone adds helper terms: stay at the right height, stay upright, keep the feet symmetric, do not twist the hips. These terms speed up learning, but each one is also a new objective. Weight "stay upright" too high and the robot learns that the best way to be upright is to stand still. That is not a bug in PPO. You told it standing still is worth more than walking.

**The 1999 trick.** [Ng, Harada and Russell](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~pabbeel/cs287-fa09/readings/NgHaradaRussell-shaping-ICML1999.pdf) proved that there is exactly one kind of shaping term you can add without changing which policy is optimal. Pick any function of the state, a "potential" Phi(s), that is high in states you like. Then do not reward the potential itself; reward its *change*:

F = gamma * Phi(s') - Phi(s)

Why this is safe: sum F over a trajectory and almost everything cancels, like a telescoping sum. What is left depends only on where you started. So the robot cannot farm the bonus by hovering in a high-potential state; standing still gives zero shaping reward, because nothing changes. The shaping tells it which direction is good (getting more upright pays, getting less upright costs) without moving the finish line.

**What MPBRS does with it.** [Nguyen and La (University of Nevada, Reno)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30334) use three potentials, all bounded and smooth:

- *Height:* a Gaussian bump around the desired base height, measured by a ray to the ground.
- *Uprightness:* how close the gravity vector in the body frame is to straight down.
- *Joint regularization:* left/right ankle symmetry, left/right hip-pitch symmetry, hip yaw near zero.

Every potential gets weight 1.0, on every robot: Fourier N1, TienKung2, Unitree G1 and H1, Booster T1 (the one ETH NomadZ plays football with), Fourier GR3, Kuavo S45, HighTorque Hi and the open-source [Berkeley Humanoid Lite](https://github.com/HybridRobotics/Berkeley-Humanoid-Lite). Only robot metadata changes: joint names, default pose, target height. The setup is Isaac Lab with PPO from [rsl_rl](https://github.com/leggedrobotics/rsl_rl), the ETH library you probably already use; a run takes about 50 minutes on an RTX 2060.

The headline result is robustness, not speed. In a sweep from weight 0.1 to 500, MPBRS at 500 still converged and tracked commands, while ordinary dense shaping at the same weight produced a robot that stood still. Convergence gains are described by the authors themselves as modest, the comparisons are mostly plots, and there is no real-robot test. Treat it as a well-built recipe, not a breakthrough.

**Three things to check if you try this on your robot.**

- *The gamma has to match.* The cancellation proof needs the same gamma in F as in your PPO discount. MPBRS uses 1.0 in F while PPO uses 0.99. Do the algebra and the leftover is a small dense reward of about 1% of the potential per step. That is harmless and probably even helpful, but it means "provably does not change the optimal policy" is no longer strictly true. Know which version you are running.
- *Episode ends are where shaping breaks.* When the robot falls and resets, the next "s'" belongs to a new episode. Paired wrongly, the policy gets a big fake shaping reward for teleporting back to standing. MPBRS caches potentials and zeroes shaping on reset steps. Do the same, and set the potential to zero for terminal states.
- *Potentials only guide; the task reward still decides.* If your velocity-tracking reward is badly scaled, no amount of clever shaping saves you. Shaping helps the policy find the good behavior faster; it does not define what good is.

**For your team.** Before you tune weight number seventeen, try converting your "stay upright" and "keep height" penalties into potentials. If training gets less sensitive to the weights, you have bought yourself a reward file that might survive the next hardware revision of your robot. That is worth more than a slightly faster learning curve.

## New papers worth reading

### [LocoManiLab: a unified testbed for humanoid loco-manipulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30876)
For your stage two. 234 household tasks in simulation, 51.5 hours of simulated teleoperation, twelve policies evaluated with a shared low-level controller, and then two policies swapped across five controllers. The finding you want: for three tasks rebuilt in the real world, the ranking of three policies in sim matched the ranking on the real robot. If that holds more broadly, you can pick policies in sim instead of burning hardware hours. The warning in the same paper: success drops on unseen scenes and falls further with visual perturbations. The seed: "does my sim ranking predict my real ranking" is a cheap, underused sanity check for any team.

### [STAMP: robot motion transition with a human motion prior](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30817)
You have a clip of the robot waving and a clip of it walking. How do you get from one to the other without a twitch? STAMP goes robot to human to robot: a learned model picks where, facing which way, and over how long the transition should happen, a human motion prior generates the in-between, and the result is retargeted back and used to train a tracking policy. Shown on a Unitree G1 and a ROBOTIS K1. [Project page](https://childtoy.github.io/STAMP-project). The abstract gives no numbers, so look at the videos and judge for yourself.

### [Do flow-matching VLAs need more than one step?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30735)
A study of six flow-matching VLA policies finds that at scale, one Euler step to generate actions roughly matches multi-step sampling. If it holds up, that is a free cut in inference latency, which on a robot means a faster control loop. A good reminder that defaults in a codebase are often copied, not measured.

### [WholeBodyMem: memory before goal](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31166)
A whole-body VLA for humanoids that remembers what it saw on the way to the goal, so the robot can find the cup it walked past earlier, with a new benchmark called HuMeGo. Mostly relevant later, but memory is the piece most humanoid demos quietly leave out.

## Founder radar

### [Forgis](https://www.unisg.ch/en/newsdetail/news/hsg-startup-forgis-has-raised-45-million-dollars-in-pre-seed-funding/): the boring layer that makes factories smart
A Zurich startup founded by HSG people (CEO Federico Martelli, with Camilla Mazzoleni and Riccardo Maggioni) and based at DARE Campus in Schlieren. Their pitch is unglamorous on purpose: edge software that connects machines, PLCs and robots from different brands into one layer that AI agents can control. They raised a pre-seed of about 4.5 to 5 million dollars (sources differ) reportedly in 36 hours, in late 2025.

Why it is interesting: everyone wants to build the robot brain, but most factories cannot even get their machines to talk to each other. A humanoid on a factory floor is useless if it cannot ask the conveyor belt what it is doing. Seeds:

- *The integration tax is the moat.* Whoever owns the boring connector layer gets to sit in every factory before the robots arrive.
- *Physical AI needs a "USB" moment.* Robots, grippers, sensors and PLCs each speak their own dialect. A standard, open interface for skills (not just messages, like ROS) is still unclaimed.

They also run a monthly research night, see below. Founders who host research events are usually recruiting.

## Outside your bubble

### [A virus that builds itself by trial and error](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261021231248.htm)
A virus shell is a beautifully symmetric cage of protein, and nobody hands the proteins a blueprint. How do they get it right? A team at Oxford led by Philipp Kukura, with Roi Asor and Dan Loewenthal, watched it happen one molecule at a time, published in *Nature*. Their tool is mass photometry, which weighs single molecules by how much light they scatter, plus a new way of trapping a single growing particle so they could keep weighing it as pieces arrived.

What they saw is not a factory but a negotiation. Pieces bind weakly and fall off again, wrong combinations come apart, and only once enough pieces close into a ring, a pentagon of five building blocks, does the structure lock in. After that first stable ring, each next stable stage needs fewer extra pieces, so the build speeds up. The caveat: it is an engineered, 60-unit virus-like particle, not a wild virus, and talk of new antivirals is a long way off.

Why it is here: it is the same idea as this morning's lesson, in biology. Weak bonds are a way of exploring without committing, and stability is a potential the system climbs, one closed ring at a time. Nature's assembly lines do not plan; they make mistakes cheap to undo and keep only what holds. That is not a bad design principle for a learning robot, a codebase, or a startup.
