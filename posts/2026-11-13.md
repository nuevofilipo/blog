# Rewards that know what stable means

*2026-11-13*

Friday the 13th, which is a good day to talk about falling over. Anyone who has trained a walking policy knows the reward file. It starts with five clean terms. Three weeks later it has twenty-three, each with a weight like 0.37 that someone tuned at 2 a.m., and nobody remembers why `feet_air_time` is negative. Today's lesson is about a cleaner idea from control theory that just got its camera-ready update for Humanoids 2026: instead of listing everything good walking looks like, reward the robot for getting *more stable* at every step. Also: a paper showing that a robot's memory can secretly be a clock, a linear-model trick for model-based RL, why your diffusion policy may need 100 denoising steps instead of 10, a Korean startup selling autonomy as a bolt-on kit, a humanoid agent internship in Zurich, and what acorns have to do with Lyme disease.

## Robot learning lesson: control Lyapunov functions as rewards

**The problem with reward soup.** The standard legged-RL recipe (legged_gym, Isaac Lab, mjlab) tracks a velocity command and adds a list of regularisers: torque penalties, action rate, foot slip, base height, orientation, foot clearance, air time, and so on. It works, but every term is a heuristic, they interact with each other, and when the robot does something weird you can't tell which term caused it. On a custom robot with different leg lengths and mass distribution from a Unitree G1, you usually re-tune most of them from scratch.

**What a Lyapunov function is.** In control, a Lyapunov function V(x) is an "energy of being wrong": it's zero when the system is exactly where you want it and positive everywhere else. If you can show V always goes down along the system's motion, the system is stable, because it can only roll downhill toward the goal. A *control* Lyapunov function (CLF) is one where, for every state, there exists *some* control input that makes V go down fast enough, typically written as a decay condition like dV/dt ≤ -λV. Classical bipedal control (Aaron Ames's lab at Caltech is the home of this line of work) used CLFs inside a quadratic program: at each timestep, solve for the torques that make V decrease, subject to torque and contact limits.

**Moving it into the reward.** [Chasing Stability: Humanoid Running via Control Lyapunov Function Guided Reinforcement Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19573) (Olkin, Li, Compton, Ames; accepted to Humanoids 2026, v2 posted 10 November) takes the CLF out of the online optimiser and puts it in the RL reward. The recipe has two parts:

1. **A good reference.** They run trajectory optimisation on the full-order dynamics of the robot, respecting its hybrid structure (stance phases, flight phases, impacts), to get dynamically feasible running gaits. Using full dynamics rather than a kinematic approximation improved position tracking by up to 1.7x.
2. **A stability-shaped reward.** The tracking error relative to that reference defines V. The reward then pays the policy not only for small error, but for error that *decays at the rate a stable controller would produce*. In plain terms: "be close to the reference" becomes "be close, and if you're knocked away, come back the way a stable system would."

The result runs on a Unitree G1 with a real flight phase (both feet off the ground), on a treadmill and outdoors, using only onboard sensing, and it survives pushes to the torso and feet. The arXiv page doesn't spell out the exact CLF formula, so read the PDF before copying it.

**Why the decay term matters.** A plain tracking reward like exp(-error²) only cares about *where* you are. Two robots with the same error get the same reward, even if one is recovering and the other is diverging. The CLF term rewards the *direction of motion* in error space. That's what you actually want from a walking controller after a stumble, and it's a much denser learning signal than "didn't fall."

**What to take for your humanoid.** You don't need to adopt the whole method to steal the useful parts:

- **Write down what "wrong" means as a single function.** Even without a CLF, define one scalar error (weighted base orientation, CoM height, and joint tracking) and log it per step. If your policy looks fine on average but the error never decays after pushes, you've found a robustness problem before the hardware does.
- **Try a decay reward as one term.** Something like rewarding max(0, V_prev - V_now) when the robot is perturbed, or penalising violations of V_now ≤ (1 - λΔt) V_prev. It's a few lines in the reward file and costs nothing to try during push-recovery training.
- **Invest in references early.** The paper's big gain came from better references, not a cleverer network. For your first walk, a reference from a simple model (a linear inverted pendulum or a hand-tuned gait) is fine. For running or jumping later, physically feasible references from trajectory optimisation will save you weeks of reward tuning.
- **Know the trade-off.** Reference-based rewards make the policy depend on the quality of the reference. Velocity-tracking rewards without references give more freedom but more reward soup. Many teams start reference-free for the first walk and add references when they want a specific style or gait.

The big idea: a reward is a specification. Control theory has spent fifty years learning how to write down "stable" precisely, and RL works better when you borrow that definition instead of rebuilding it from twenty heuristics.

## New papers worth reading

- **[Is Your Robot's Memory Just a Clock?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14488)** (Zhang and An). Memory-augmented VLAs often store frames sampled at fixed time intervals with timestamps. On RoboMME counting tasks, simply pausing the robot or wiggling its wrist (which changes nothing about the task) made a released policy stop early. Success dropped from 47 to 16 out of 50 on one task and from 44 to 10 on another. The policy had learned that "time passed" means "progress happened," because demonstrations move at a steady pace. Removing timestamps didn't help, and training with injected pauses hurt counting. What worked was indexing memory by *events* rather than clock ticks, which recovered half or more of the lost success without retraining. This is a shortcut-learning story worth remembering for chapter two: demonstrations from teleop are always smoother and more regular than deployment.
- **[Koopman Model-Based Reinforcement Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14186)** (Hao, Heredia, Mou). Koopman theory says you can make nonlinear dynamics look linear if you lift the state into the right feature space. This paper learns such a lifted linear model, so policy gradients through the model become a constant matrix product, and only uses one-step predictions plus a critic to avoid compounding model errors. They report better sample efficiency than model-free and model-based baselines on HalfCheetah and quadruped locomotion, and a real quadruped that stands up on its rear feet, walks bipedally, and comes back down. The abstract gives no exact numbers. Worth knowing as the "linear models are back" counterpoint to giant world models.
- **[One Trajectory, Two Requirements](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13311)** (Rautela). If you've ever lowered the number of Euler steps in a flow-matching policy to save latency, read this. On a frozen π0.5 LIBERO checkpoint, getting the final action within 5% error took 4 to 9 steps, but getting within 1% took 87 to 112. The paper also shows that accuracy and numerical stability can demand different step counts, so an offline accuracy sweep alone doesn't prove your chosen count is safe. Practical takeaway: when you pick a step count, measure the error of the actions themselves, not only task success.
- **[One Policy for 100 Household Tasks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14432).** A single π0.5-based VLA for all 100 BEHAVIOR tasks, rather than a separate model per task group. The score on 50 shared tasks went from 0.260 to 0.371, and the full 100-task score is 0.392. It pairs nicely with yesterday's second-place BEHAVIOR report, which went the opposite way with per-task adapters.
- **[Elastic-WAM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14257).** A world action model that spends more denoising compute on hard moments and less on easy ones, to cut latency without losing control quality. It's the adaptive-compute version of yesterday's asynchronous inference lesson.

## Founder radar

### URobotics: autonomy as a bolt-on kit

[URobotics](https://dealroom.co/news/170189-robot-autonomy-startup-urobotics-raises-29-9m-series-a-at-a-tenfold-valu/), a 2024 spin-off from KAIST's Urban Robotics Lab in South Korea, announced a Series A on 5 November (the article cites both 29.9 and 33.5 million dollars, so the exact figure is unclear). Kiwoom Investment and K2 Investment Partners co-led, with Hyundai's Zer01ne participating, at more than ten times its seed valuation.

What it sells is the interesting part. Its product, E-DONG-KIT, is a modular, hardware-agnostic autonomy kit that attaches to humanoids, quadrupeds and other robots and lets them assess and traverse difficult terrain on their own. Current customers do autonomous inspection on construction and energy sites, and the plan is to expand to shipbuilding, steel and defence reconnaissance.

The seed for you: everyone is building robots, but most robot makers don't want to build terrain-aware autonomy themselves, the same way most car makers buy their ADAS from suppliers. "Sell the capability, not the body" is a valid strategy for a small team, and a lab spin-off is exactly where that capability lives. Ask yourself which capability your club will build that other robot teams would pay for: a robust locomotion training pipeline, a calibration tool, a sim-to-real test harness. Some of the most durable robotics companies sell picks and shovels to the gold rush.

## Outside your bubble

### [Lyme disease starts with acorns](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261106174518.htm)

If someone asked you what drives Lyme disease, you'd probably say ticks, and then deer, since the tick is literally called the deer tick. A 35-year study at the Cary Institute of Ecosystem Studies in New York, summarised this month in *PNAS* by Richard Ostfeld and Shannon LaDeau, says deer barely matter. Over the full dataset, deer numbers had no statistically significant link to the number of nymphal ticks.

The real chain is longer and stranger. Oak trees produce huge acorn crops in some years (called "masting"). The next year, white-footed mice, which feast on acorns, boom. Mice are excellent hosts for tick larvae and very good at passing on the Lyme bacterium. The year after that, those larvae have become infected nymphs, which are the stage that bites people. Years with lots of mice were followed by about 40% more nymphs. So a big acorn year predicts a bad Lyme year roughly *two years later*. Other animals like squirrels, skunks and opossums feed ticks but rarely infect them, so a diverse forest dilutes the risk. Warm years, oddly, were followed by fewer nymphs, so the net effect of climate change is unclear.

Why it belongs here: this is credit assignment with a two-year delay, through three intermediate variables, where the obvious suspect (deer) is a red herring. It's the same structure as your walking policy falling over 40 steps after a bad foot placement, or a startup dying in year three from a hiring decision in year one. The variable you can see easily is rarely the one that causes the outcome. And the only way the researchers found the real chain was by measuring the same forest patiently for 35 years. Long logs beat clever guesses.
