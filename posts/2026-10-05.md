# Getting the walking policy off the GPU and onto the robot

*2026-10-05*

Today's lesson is the one promised yesterday: deployment, meaning how a trained walking policy actually runs on the robot's onboard computer, and how to do the first tests without breaking anything. Two papers from this week fit it well. One is a careful post-mortem of everything that silently goes wrong in a staged humanoid training pipeline, the other shows that bolting a safety filter onto a learned controller after training is not enough. Plus a Swiss innovation conference with an AI and robotics track, and a 30-year forestry experiment.

## Robot learning

### [Beyond Reward Hacking: four places where a humanoid RL pipeline lies to you](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03196)
Arunabh Bora; posted 2 October.

A single-author paper that reads like a lab notebook of hard-won lessons. The setup is close to yours: PPO, 2048 parallel environments, one consumer GPU (RTX 5070 Ti), a simulated 1.91 m humanoid with 11 actuated leg and waist joints, trained in four stages (standing, speed curriculum up to 2 m/s, arm motion, leg reference motions) over about 14 hours. The point is that rewards are not the only proxy that can drift from what you actually want. The author finds four:

- **Reward.** A Gaussian tracking reward like exp(-|c - v|^2 / sigma^2) is flat near the target, so at a zero-velocity command any small incentive elsewhere wins and the robot drifts. Adding an L1 penalty on base velocity that is active only when the command is zero cut drift from 0.2 m/s to 0.01-0.04 m/s.
- **Curriculum gate.** Promoting to the next level when the joint error averaged over all joints is small hides a single joint that misses by radians. Gate on the worst joint instead, plus a contact check (for example, a foot in swing must carry under 1 N).
- **Measurement.** Evaluating with exploration noise on gave 61-69% "foot lifted" success, while the deterministic policy lifted its foot zero times: the noise was doing the lifting. And because all environments reset together, pushes always hit at the same gait phase, which made a 0.5 m/s push look deadlier than a 2.0 m/s one. Randomize push timing over at least one gait cycle.
- **Reference motion.** A hand-made one-leg-lift motion had the center of mass 0.21 m outside the support foot; seven rounds of reward shaping could not fix a physically impossible target. Check feasibility first.

There is also a neat trick for adding new observations mid-project without retraining: append the inputs and set the matching first-layer weights to zero, so the network computes exactly the same function and training continues from there. Worth reading in full before your first long runs; most of these bugs produce plots that look healthy.

### [CoFiT: train the policy knowing a safety filter will edit its actions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02341)
Pranit Mohnot, Christian Helten, Daniele Gammelli, Marco Pavone (Stanford); posted 1 October.

A common recipe is to train a whole-body tracking policy, then put a safety filter in front of the motors that modifies any action that would violate a constraint (joint limits, self-collision, workspace bounds). The authors show this is a mismatch: the filter changes the actions that are executed and therefore the states the robot ends up in, which the policy never saw in training. Their fix, CoFiT, fine-tunes the policy with the filter in the loop under explicit constraints. On the TWIST2 teleoperation tracker, time spent violating constraints drops by 91% in simulation and by 83% on hardware, with no operator interventions needed in their tests. The lesson for deployment: anything you put between the policy and the motors on the real robot (clipping, rate limits, filters) should already be there in simulation.

## Learn

**Deployment: from a trained checkpoint to a policy running on the robot**

The earlier lessons covered actions, rewards, domain randomization, observations and the RL algorithm. Once a policy walks in Isaac Lab, here is the path to the real robot.

**1. Sim-to-sim before sim-to-real.** Load the exported policy into a second simulator, usually MuJoCo, with the same robot model, and run it there. Different contact models and integrators expose policies that only work because of quirks of the training simulator. Humanoid-Gym made this step standard, and it is cheap: if the gait falls apart in MuJoCo, it will fall apart on hardware. It also catches the most common deployment bugs, which are plain mismatches: joint order, sign conventions, default joint angles, observation scaling, action scaling.

**2. Export the network.** The actor is a small MLP (a few hundred thousand parameters), so it does not need a GPU. Export it to TorchScript or ONNX (rsl_rl and Isaac Lab have export scripts) and run it with ONNX Runtime or LibTorch on the onboard CPU. Inference takes well under a millisecond. Export only the actor in deterministic mode, meaning the mean action, without exploration noise; the paper above shows how different the stochastic and deterministic policies can be, so evaluate the deterministic one in simulation too.

**3. Run the loop at a fixed rate.** The policy was trained at a fixed control frequency, typically 50 Hz, with a lower-level PD loop at around 1 kHz on the motor drivers. On the robot: read IMU and joint encoders, build the observation exactly as in training (same order, same scaling, same history length, same last action), run the network, convert the action to joint position targets, and send them to the drivers. Do this in a real-time loop with a fixed period, and log the actual loop time. A policy trained at 50 Hz and run at a jittery 40 to 60 Hz is effectively running on a different robot. If you use ROS 2, ros2_control is the usual structure; many teams instead write a single small C++ or Python process that talks to the motor bus directly, which is simpler to make deterministic.

**4. Put the safety layer in training too.** Joint position limits, torque limits, action clipping and rate limits belong in the simulated environment as well, for the reason CoFiT spells out. On top of that, add things that only exist on hardware: a software kill switch, a watchdog that sends zero torque if the loop misses deadlines, and limits that cut power when the base tilts past a threshold.

**5. First tests on a gantry.** Hang the robot from a gantry or harness so the feet just touch the ground. Order of tests: (a) hold the default standing pose with only the PD controllers, policy off; (b) policy on with zero velocity command, feet on the ground, harness taking some weight; (c) slowly lower the harness so the robot carries its own weight while standing; (d) small forward commands. At each step compare logged joint positions, velocities and torques with the same command in simulation. Large differences in torque or oscillation in a joint usually point to an actuator model or PD gain mismatch, which goes back into training as better actuator modeling or randomization.

**6. Close the loop.** Every hardware session should produce a log you can replay against simulation. The useful pattern is: observe a failure on the robot, reproduce it in simulation, fix it there (reward, randomization, actuator model), retrain, redeploy.

Further reading: [Humanoid-Gym](https://github.com/roboterax/humanoid-gym), which popularized the sim-to-sim check with MuJoCo; [rsl_rl](https://github.com/leggedrobotics/rsl_rl) and [Isaac Lab](https://github.com/isaac-sim/IsaacLab) for policy export; [ONNX Runtime](https://onnxruntime.ai/) for CPU inference; [ros2_control](https://control.ros.org/) if you go the ROS 2 route.

Next time: actuator modeling, meaning how to measure your motors and build an actuator network or a good analytic model, since it is usually the biggest single reason a policy that walks in simulation stumbles on hardware.

## Founder radar

### Google wants to be robotics' Android
The Information reported on 1 October that Google is positioning itself as the platform for robot makers rather than building its own full robot, in the style of Android for phones rather than Apple ([weekly summary](https://valueaddvc.com/pulse/weekly/2026-09-28)). This matches what DeepMind has said publicly about Gemini Robotics: one model meant to work across many bodies, with hardware partners such as Apptronik, Agility and Boston Dynamics. If this works, the foundation model layer becomes a commodity you license, and value moves to the parts Google will not do: specific hardware, deployment in particular industries, data collection, and the integration work between a general model and a specific robot.

**Idea seed.** The two papers today describe the same gap from different sides: training pipelines that look healthy while being wrong, and robots whose real-time stack differs from simulation in small ways. A tool that automatically diffs a sim rollout against a hardware log (observation by observation, same command, same timestamps) and flags mismatches in joint order, scaling, latency or actuator response would save every humanoid team weeks. Build it for the club first; if it helps there, it likely helps every lab running Isaac Lab policies on real robots.

## Outside your bubble

### [Thinning a forest restored all its carbon in 30 years, and grew bigger trees](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261001214120.htm)
The Silvicultural Systems Project in Victoria, Australia, set up mountain ash forest plots in 1987-89 and compared clearfelling, small patch clearfelling, heavy thinning (removing 50-70% of trees) and untouched controls. Thirty years later, trees in thinned stands averaged well over a meter in diameter, about 20% larger than in the controls, and the thinned forest stored as much carbon as the untouched one. The larger trees also resist fire better and form hollows used by the endangered Leadbeater's possum, which turned up in all thinned plots and none of the controls. Two things worth taking away: some questions can only be answered by experiments that run longer than most careers, and "use it or protect it" turned out to be a false choice here.
