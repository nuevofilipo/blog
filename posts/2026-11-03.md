# Ask your controller the same question twice

Ask a diffusion controller the same question with different noise, and if the answers disagree, you've probably found a motion your robot can't do. ## Robot learning lesson: diffusion as a controller, not just a planner

**The usual setup.** Your walking policy, once it exists, will be a small MLP. Observation in, joint targets out, one deterministic answer every 20 milliseconds. That's fast and robust, but it has two blind spots. You can't steer it without retraining (want it to duck? retrain with a ducking reward), and it can't tell you when it's out of its depth. It answers a motion it has never seen just as confidently as one it trained on a thousand times.

**What [DiffWBC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41354) changes.** Yifei Huang and colleagues replace the MLP with a diffusion model that generates a short future trajectory for the whole body, and then make it fast enough for real-time control.

- *One step, not fifty.* Classic diffusion denoises in many steps, which is far too slow for a 50 Hz loop. DiffWBC is trained to denoise in a single forward pass, so each control step costs about the same as an MLP. They train it either by distilling an RL teacher (the usual teacher-student pattern) or with model-based RL.
- *Steering at test time.* Since the model outputs a plan rather than a single action, you can push that plan with gradients of any differentiable cost while it's being generated: "keep the head below 1.25 m", "keep this hand level". Body-group masks decide which limbs get the push. The controller itself stays frozen. The authors show it walking under a 1.25 m ceiling better than if you edit the reference motion, and generalizing zero-shot to hurdles, reaching, a simulated actuator fault and balancing a tray.
- *Failure discovery without a simulator.* This is the clever bit. Run the generator several times on the same reference with different noise. If the plans agree, the motion is probably trackable. If they spread out, the model is unsure, and unsure usually means physically hard. Ranking clips by that spread flags tracking failures with 91% precision in the top 2%, and fine-tuning on the flagged clips fixes half of those failures.

**Numbers.** Lowest local tracking error among six general-purpose trackers on held-out benchmarks (20.1 mm on BONES), one forward pass per 50 Hz step, and deployment on a real Unitree G1. Videos are on the [project page](https://diffwbc.github.io/).

**Why the variance trick matters for you.** Every motion dataset you'll use contains clips your robot physically can't do: wrong proportions, a pose past a joint limit, a jump your motors can't power. Today you discover them by simulating everything and looking at what fails, or worse, on hardware. Using disagreement as an uncertainty signal is an old idea (ensembles, dropout at test time), and here it comes almost for free, because a generative model has noise built in.

**What to steal for your humanoid.**

- *Don't swap your walking policy for a diffusion model.* For a first walk, a PPO MLP is still the right tool. DiffWBC is a motion tracker distilled from an RL teacher, so it comes after you have a working teacher, not instead of one.
- *Steal the uncertainty idea now.* Train three or five walking policies with different seeds. Where their actions disagree a lot in simulation (certain speeds, turning rates, terrains), that's where hardware tests are most likely to fail. It's a cheap way to decide what to test first and what to keep off the command range.
- *Keep test-time steering in mind for the task phase.* When you move on to tasks, "frozen controller plus cost gradients" is a way to add constraints (avoid this shelf, keep this cup level) without a full retraining run.

**Caveats.** The abstract doesn't report how the controller behaves under large pushes or rough terrain, and test-time steering only works if your cost is differentiable. No code link yet. And single-step diffusion, distilled from a teacher, can't be better than the teacher at what the teacher already does. Its advantage is everything you can do on top.

## New papers worth reading

### [ROOF: getting up when there's a table on top of you](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.42097)
Fall recovery papers usually assume open space. Real falls happen next to furniture. Yidan Lu, Ping Luo, Peng Lu and colleagues train a proprioception-only policy for a fallen humanoid that has to make room under an overhead obstacle before standing up, because standing up too early just jams it harder. The trick is in the reward: instead of scripting an escape path, they reward the robot for gradually reducing its overlap with the obstacle, with a learned motion prior and penalties for stalled joints and bad support postures. In simulation, recovery under liftable plates goes from 18.9% without the geometric reward to 88.5% with it. On a real G1 it recovered in 95 of 150 attempts across seven scenes, including furniture it never saw in training. That's 63%, so read it as progress, not a solved problem. For your team: a getting-up policy will be your second policy after walking, and the lesson about shaping is general. Reward "less wrong than a moment ago" when you can't specify the right path.

### [Learning the dynamics of your own walking policy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41892)
Tianlin Zhang and colleagues treat a frozen RL locomotion policy as a black box and learn, online, how the robot's body actually moves when you give it velocity commands. The model handles changing conditions (a payload, a different floor) by treating them as hidden variables in a robust Gaussian model, then an MPC uses it to place an arm's end effector with about 1 cm error. The lesson for later: you don't have to retrain the walker to do precise loco-manipulation. Wrap it in a model of itself and plan on top.

### Also new
- [WorldBridge](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40748): a world action model with separate video and action experts trained jointly. On out-of-distribution RoboTwin 2.0 tasks, success goes from 25% to 71% as video and action supervision are added. More evidence that predicting video helps actions generalize.
- [Retargeting as an ODE on the robot's manifold](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41349): roughly twice as fast as OmniRetarget, slightly lower quality. Relevant the day you build a motion dataset for your own robot's proportions.
- [MolmoSpaces-CommonSense](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41377): a benchmark for whether generalist manipulation policies understand occlusion, containers and affordances. Success drops by more than half with more distractors, and policies grab the wrong object 30% of the time in clutter. A good antidote to demo videos.
- [Teleop to dexterity, 281 policies, about 1,700 real trials](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40828): a large real-robot evaluation of imitation policies on RJ45 grasping and insertion with a five-fingered hand. For the VLA, predicting absolute rather than relative actions improved insertion by up to 70 percentage points. Small design choices, huge swings.

## Founder radar

### [Tutor Intelligence: robots by the hour](https://thenextweb.com/news/tutor-intelligence-75m-series-b-a16z-robot-workers)
The MIT CSAIL spin-out from Watertown, Massachusetts announced a 75 million dollar Series B on Monday, led by Andreessen Horowitz, bringing it to 117 million in total. The interesting part isn't the robot, it's the business model. Customers don't buy anything. They pay per hour or per pick, with no upfront cost and no long-term contract. Tutor's robots (Sonny and Cassie) handle cereal boxes, cosmetics and toys in factories and warehouses, and when they get stuck, a remote human "tutor" helps. Every intervention becomes training data for the company's foundation model. They say they're in almost four times as many facilities as a year ago, and they're aiming for more than 1,000 robots under contract by the end of 2027. They didn't say how many they run today, and they didn't disclose revenue.

**The seed to plant:** "robot as a service plus remote humans plus data flywheel" turns the hardest part of robotics sales, a big capital purchase, into an operating expense a plant manager can approve alone. It also means the company pays for its training data while getting paid for labor. Compare yesterday's Maxinsights, which pays thousands of people to film chores. Same hunger for data, opposite direction of the cash flow. If you ever pitch a robotics company, the first question will be "who pays for your data?". Have an answer like Tutor's.

## Outside your bubble

### [Human DNA from cave dirt, and an Ice Age Europe that was better connected than we thought](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261031221153.htm)
A team led by Pere Gelabert and Ron Pinhasi at the University of Vienna (with David Reich among the co-authors) published new genomes from the Gravettian, the culture that spread across Europe roughly 33,000 to 21,000 years ago, in Science Advances. Three things stand out.

First, a woman from Isturitz Cave in southwestern France, about 30,000 years old, carried mostly western European ancestry but also a share from central and eastern Europe. Modeling dates that mixing to about 32,500 to 31,500 years ago. Earlier genetic work had split Gravettian Europe into a western and an eastern population that barely met. One genome suggests people moved and mixed across the continent more than that.

Second, at El Mirón Cave in Spain, they recovered human DNA from a layer of sediment about 22,000 years old, with no bones at all. People shed DNA into the floor they live on, and it can survive for millennia. That means sites with tools and hearths but no skeletons can still tell you who lived there.

Third, a fetus from Ostuni in southern Italy and his mother: the father was probably from the same broad population but not a close relative, which hints at mating patterns that avoided inbreeding in small groups.

The caveats are real: the connectivity claim rests heavily on one woman's genome, the sediment result is one site and one layer, and ancestry dates come from statistical models. But the method is the bigger story. "Read the population from what it left in the dirt" is the same move as environmental DNA in ecology, wastewater surveillance in epidemiology, and, if you squint, learning a robot's dynamics from the traces it leaves rather than from its spec sheet. Sometimes the best data isn't the object you're looking for, it's the residue around it.
