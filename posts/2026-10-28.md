# Your gait has a heartbeat, use it

*2026-10-28*

Wednesday. The first time a custom humanoid walks on real hardware, one of the most likely causes of failure is not the policy. It's a sensor that lies: a knee encoder zeroed 0.3 rad off, an IMU with a gyro bias, a foot contact switch stuck on. The policy can't tell. It just behaves strangely, and you spend an afternoon retuning rewards for a problem that lives in a calibration file. A TUM and Siemens paper from yesterday gives a neat way to catch this, and it rests on one simple fact: walking is periodic, so "normal" has a shape you can write down. Also today: a central pattern generator that gives a quadruped five gaits with one reward, a terrain adapter you add on top of a frozen humanoid tracker, Teradyne's numbers (headline beat, stock down), a Bosch internship on robots that keep learning after deployment, and T cells that can feel how stiff your tissue is.

## Robot learning lesson: phase-binned anomaly detection

**The setup.** [Ziegler, Özen, Mentes, Waschulzik and Knoll](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36852) take a frozen locomotion policy they cannot look inside (a multi-embodiment policy driving a simulated Unitree Go2 in MuJoCo), corrupt one of its sensors, and ask whether an outside monitor can figure out which sensor is lying and fix the reading before it reaches the policy.

**The trick: a transparent stand-in for the policy.** They fit a surrogate that predicts the policy's 12 joint targets as the sum of two parts:

- *A rhythm.* Four Fourier harmonics of the gait phase, conditioned on commanded speed. This is the "heartbeat" of the gait, what the legs do when nothing surprising happens.
- *A correction.* A sum of per-input terms, one for each of the 57 inputs (base motion, gravity vector, joint states, previous actions, contacts), each weighted by coefficients that depend on phase. Clipped to plus or minus 0.15.

Because the correction is additive, you can say exactly how much each sensor pushed each joint at any moment. That traceability is the whole point.

**Diagnosis.** Split the gait cycle into 20 phase bins. From fault-free runs, learn the healthy mean and spread of every joint's correction, per bin. At runtime, flag a joint whose correction leaves the 2-sigma band for its phase. Then score each of 34 sensor units by how much it pushes the flagged corrections further in the wrong direction. Top score is the suspect. Nothing flagged, no accusation.

**Repair.** Only the suspect gets touched. If its reading normally swings a lot over the gait cycle, replace it with the healthy value for the current phase. If it's mostly flat, estimate the bias with a slow running average and subtract it. Contact signals get replaced with nominal values.

**The numbers.** 37 injected faults (offsets, stuck readings, drift, dropout, spikes, contact errors), 640 faulty episodes:

- *No repair:* 259 of 640 keep walking.
- *Offline diagnosis plus repair:* 429. With the ground-truth faulty sensor given for free: 451. So diagnosis is getting most of the way to the ceiling.
- *Fully online version:* 382. It triggered in 454 of 640 faulty episodes and in 0 of 64 healthy ones. No false alarms is what makes a monitor usable.
- *The instructive failure:* a gradient-boosted tree surrogate predicted clean actions just as well (R squared 0.9764 vs 0.9766) and gave no real repair gain. An accurate model is not the same as an explainable one, and only the explainable one tells you where to look.

**Caveats.** One policy, simulation only, single faults only, and part of the fault suite was picked on development data. On Go1 and ANYmal C it still helped (197 to 305 and 162 to 265 walking episodes), but a gradient-based method did better there, so the ranking of methods doesn't transfer cleanly. No code.

**What to steal for your robot.**

- *Log by phase, not by time.* Whatever gait clock your walking policy uses (most humanoid rewards have one for foot timing), store it in every log. Then plot each joint position, velocity and torque as a band over phase from good runs. A new run that leaves the band in one joint is far easier to read than a 30-second time series.
- *Make a "healthy envelope" file part of your bring-up.* After the first decent walking session, save the per-phase mean and standard deviation of every sensor. Every later session gets automatically compared against it. An encoder that drifted after someone reassembled a knee shows up on the first lap.
- *You don't need the full surrogate on day one.* The phase-binned 2-sigma check on raw sensor values already catches offsets and stuck readings. Add the per-sensor attribution later, if you have faults that only show up through the policy's reaction.

It also pairs with yesterday's fall monitor: that one tells you the robot is in trouble, this one tells you which sensor got it there.

## New papers worth reading

### [A trainable CPG: five gaits, one reward](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36537)
Same idea from the other side: put the rhythm in by construction. Zhejiang University builds an eight-neuron central pattern generator (the oscillator circuits animal spines use to drive walking) for a Unitree Go2. A small network, pretrained offline from inverse kinematics and then frozen, turns oscillator state into joint targets. PPO then learns only six bounded "knobs" on top: body height, foot clearance, ground penetration, step length, turning, and trajectory orientation. The reward has no gait-style or foot-timing term at all, because the oscillator already handles timing. Result: walk, trot, pace, bound and pronk from one policy, with 99.4% success on gait and speed switches against 95.6% for the strongest baseline (Walk These Ways) and roughly half its velocity error. It ran on the real robot without fine-tuning. Flat ground only, and the baselines were reimplemented by the authors. For your humanoid: if the gait reward keeps producing shuffles or hops, a phase oscillator as an input, or as a prior on the action, is the cheapest structure you can add.

### [GRAFT: teach a frozen tracker about stairs](https://graft-humanoid.github.io/)
Humanoid "behavior foundation model" trackers (SONIC and friends) are trained mostly on flat ground. [GRAFT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36507) leaves the tracker frozen and adds a small adapter that only corrects lower-body targets based on terrain: foot placement, swing height, contact timing. It is trained with foot-geometry rewards, penalized for changing anything on flat ground, then distilled from privileged height maps to onboard depth. Tested on four different trackers in sim and on a G1 with SONIC crossing stairs, gaps and platforms. The design lesson: keep your general skill and your terrain skill as separate modules, and you never retrain the expensive one.

### For the reading list
- [REACH](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36546): RL hierarchy that lets a G1 switch between walking at variable height and crawling through low spaces.
- [Phase-conditioned PPO for loco-manipulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36270): one critic and reward per stage of a multi-step task, tested on G1 and Spot. Useful later when you move from walking to tasks.
- [Torch-Hand](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36789): a 21-DOF linkage-driven humanoid hand with a human-like joint layout.

## Founder radar

### The brain moves into the incumbent's arm
On 21 October, at NVIDIA GTC Berlin, [Teradyne Robotics and Sereact announced a partnership](https://www.therobotreport.com/teradyne-robotics-partners-sereact-scale-physical-ai-retail-logistics/): Sereact's Cortex vision-language-action model running on Universal Robots arms for returns handling and piece picking, with deployments already live. Sereact, from Stuttgart, raised a Series B of about 110 million dollars in May.

The pattern matters more than the deal. Universal Robots has the installed base, the distributors and the safety certifications. It does not have a model that can pick an unknown item out of a returns bin. Sereact has the model and no hardware business. Instead of fighting each other, the startup becomes the default brain for the incumbent's hardware, and the incumbent gets an AI story for its next earnings call.

Seeds to think about:

- *Who else has an installed base but no brain?* Mobile robot fleets, CNC tending cells, agricultural machinery, lab automation. Each is a possible "Sereact for X".
- *Distribution beats demos.* A 2-person team with a decent policy and one integrator partner can reach more customers than a 20-person team selling its own robot.
- *The risk:* you become a feature. If the arm maker eventually trains its own model, your partnership is the thing they learned from.

## Outside your bubble

### [Immune cells that can feel how stiff your tissue is](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261026225617.htm)
After an infection, some T cells don't leave. They settle into the tissue they defended (skin, lungs, gut) and stay there for years as tissue-resident memory cells, a local guard that reacts much faster than anything recruited from the blood. The textbook story is that chemical signals decide which T cells settle in.

A team led by Judith Mandl at McGill, publishing in Nature Immunology, adds a physical signal. They put human and mouse T cells into collagen gels tuned to different stiffnesses, mimicking soft and firm tissues. The stiffer the gel, the more the cells took on tissue-resident memory traits. In other words, a T cell partly decides whether to become a long-term resident by feeling how hard its surroundings are.

The caveats are real: this is gels, not living tissue; the molecular sensor that does the feeling hasn't been found; and the talk of applications for autoimmune disease, transplant rejection and cancer immunotherapy is for now a possibility. But the idea is striking: tumours and scarred tissue are often stiffer than healthy tissue, so mechanics might be quietly shaping which immune cells stay where.

The robotics echo is almost too neat. We spend a lot of effort giving robots tactile sensing because vision alone misses how things feel. It turns out the immune system has been running on touch as well as chemistry all along.
