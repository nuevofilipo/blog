# Teach it to walk before lunch

That question has your team's name on it, because the day will come when you want to fine-tune on the real robot and there's no motion-capture room in the club workshop. Also today: a cart-pushing G1 that feels force without force sensors, a whole-body controller that splits the job in two, a Chinese manipulation startup with a simulator, and why sticky ocean snow matters for the climate.

## Robot learning lesson: rewarding acceleration instead of velocity

**The problem nobody puts in the README.** Almost every locomotion reward you'll write starts with "track the commanded base velocity". In simulation that's free, since the simulator knows exactly how fast the torso is moving. On the real robot, base velocity is surprisingly hard to measure. You either need a motion-capture system or a state estimator that fuses IMU and leg kinematics and that you trust. That's fine for deployment, where the policy only needs to be roughly right. But it's a real obstacle the moment you want to *learn* on hardware, because a reward signal that drifts is worse than no signal at all.

**What [STRIDE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08857) does.** Nico Bohlinger and Jan Peters (TU Darmstadt, DFKI, hessian.AI) change what gets rewarded:

- *Command a speed, reward the acceleration that gets you there.* A velocity command is turned into a reference that ramps up with bounded acceleration. A small PI controller computes the acceleration the robot *should* have right now, and the reward scores how closely the low-pass-filtered IMU acceleration matches it. Accelerometers measure acceleration directly, with no integration and no mocap.
- *Plug the hole at cruising speed.* At constant speed the target acceleration is zero, which a standing robot can fake. So there's a second "hold" term that tracks the reference velocity using a velocity estimate integrated from the IMU. That estimate drifts, but only the hold term relies on it.
- *Make the learner sample-efficient.* Instead of PPO with thousands of parallel environments, they use XQC, an off-policy actor-critic with a carefully conditioned critic (batch norm, weight norm, a distributional cross-entropy loss). Off-policy means every transition gets reused many times, which is the only way learning on a real robot is affordable.
- *Two cheap tricks that do a lot of the work.* Stiffer joint PD gains (Kp 60, Kd 3, versus the G1's default of 45 and 1) make each action's effect more predictable early on. And left-right mirroring swaps the legs in half the replay-buffer samples, so the robot learns both sides of the gait from one side's data.

**The numbers.** In MuJoCo, both a Unitree G1 and a Booster T1 learn to walk forward after about 13 to 14 minutes of samples (250k transitions at 50 Hz is 83 minutes in total). Without the gains and mirroring tricks it takes more than an hour, and falls during training drop from over 2,000 to about 440. That 81% matters on hardware, because every fall is a repair.

**Honest caveats.** Everything is in simulation, and the simulated IMU has no noise or bias, which is the very thing that will bite on a real robot. The paper only covers forward walking, and there's no code link yet (it's built on the authors' RL-X library). Treat this as a well-argued design, not a proven hardware recipe.

**Why this matters for your team.**

1. *Write your reward around what you can measure.* Even if you train in Isaac Lab or MuJoCo, ask of each reward term whether you could compute it on the real robot. Terms you can measure on hardware are the ones you can keep using when you fine-tune or evaluate on hardware later.
2. *Steal the two tricks today.* Symmetry augmentation is a few lines in any PPO or SAC pipeline, as long as you write the mirror map for your robot's joint ordering carefully, which is where the bugs live. And sweeping PD gains is one of the cheapest experiments you can run before blaming the reward.
3. *Know the off-policy option exists.* Your first gait will almost certainly come from massively parallel PPO in sim. But if the club ever wants to adapt on the real robot, sample-efficient off-policy methods like this are the path. Bookmark it for phase two.

## New papers worth reading

### [ForPC: a G1 that feels the cart without force sensors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08664)
A two-stage RL controller learns to infer contact force and state as latent variables from ordinary onboard signals (joint states, IMU), then uses them to push a cart precisely. It's trained only in simulation and transfers zero-shot to a Unitree G1, which pushes up to 30 kg at 0.5 m/s. Why read it: your robot won't have wrist force-torque sensors either, and "estimate the force from proprioception" is the same trick that makes walking policies robust to pushes.

### [DKWBC: let a model do the tracking, let RL do the balancing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09605)
Instead of one network that tracks motions and stays upright, this splits the job. Model-based inverse dynamics plus PD feedback handle the tracking, and a learned residual policy only adds balance corrections. Results hold in sim and on hardware across ground contact, payloads and physical interaction. It's a good counterpoint to pure end-to-end RL, and worth knowing if your custom robot has an accurate URDF and mass model.

### Also new
- [SUGARv2, arXiv 2611.09266](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09266): generates loco-manipulation references from reusable task templates, trains teacher policies on them, and distills those into vision-based policies. 91% mean success in sim across five tasks, plus a real humanoid. A preview of phase two.
- [Lodos, arXiv 2611.08872](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08872): a GPU simulator from Georg Martius's group built for contact between detailed meshes, reporting about 10x the throughput of established simulators in that setting.
- [VLA Handover, arXiv 2611.08987](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08987): a small VLA on the robot runs by default and calls a big server-side VLA only when it predicts it's about to fail. 11 to 22 points better on LIBERO with half the server calls. A sensible architecture for any robot with a weak onboard computer.
- [Meituan-Robotics-0, arXiv 2611.08577](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08577): a food-delivery giant's VLA foundation model, with 93.6% on RoboTwin 2.0. The interesting part is "Rollback", which uses human corrections as both positive and negative supervision.

There's also a Tencent Cloud partnership for embodied-AI services.

The pattern is worth noticing. Robotics foundation-model companies keep ending up owning their simulator, because the model is only as good as the place it trains and gets tested. Context: robotics startups raised 9.9 billion dollars in Q3 2026, more than double a year earlier ([Runtime Wire](https://runtimewire.com/article/robotics-startup-funding-q3-2026-physical-ai)), so capital isn't what's scarce. Good evaluation is.

Questions worth an evening:
- Is "evaluation as a service" for robot policies a business? Labs spend weeks building benchmarks nobody trusts. A neutral, reproducible sim and real test harness could be the scale-up of robotics.
- STRIDE above is a hint of a hardware niche: if robots learn on-device, they need rigs that let them fall safely and reset themselves. Who builds the treadmill-plus-harness for humanoids?
- What would your club's robot need to become someone else's eval platform?

## Outside your bubble

### [Sticky snow that sinks carbon](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261105224558.htm)
The ocean is always snowing. "Marine snow" is the steady fall of dead plankton, poop and mucus from the surface into the deep, and it moves more than 10 gigatons of carbon out of surface waters every year. The catch: only about 2 gigatons sink deep enough to stay locked away for centuries. The rest gets eaten by bacteria on the way down and released again.

A Scripps and University of Lincoln team led by Bryce Inman (published in *Science*, with the imaging method in *Nature Communications*) finally watched this happen at the scale of single bacteria. Using 3D confocal microscopy and custom fluorescent molecules, they tracked microbes moving through and colonizing individual snow particles. The finding: particle *viscosity* matters a lot. Stickier particles resist breakdown and fragmentation, and in their experiments and simulations deliver more than six times as much carbon to the deep ocean as runnier ones. It took four years just to get the imaging to work.

Caveat: the six-times figure comes from lab work and modelling, not field measurements of carbon flux. Still, it's a nice reminder that big planetary numbers can depend on a material property you'd never have thought to measure. It's the same lesson as STRIDE, really: before you trust a signal, ask how you're measuring it.
