# Keep your sigma alive

*2026-10-29*

Thursday. Here is a training failure that's easy to miss on the dashboard. Your walking policy learned to stand, then to shuffle, and now the reward curve has gone flat. You change the reward to ask for longer steps and nothing happens. The policy isn't stubborn. It has lost the ability to learn, and a paper from Turin and IIT Genoa points at one number you are probably already logging but not watching: the standard deviation of the policy's action noise. Also today: humanoid parkour that predicts its next footstep, a memory that remembers the map, Agility's GXO expansion (and a customer saying out loud that it has no ROI yet), Alphabet down 10% while its cloud doubles, a RAI Institute internship in Zurich, and why aging muscles quietly swap strength for survival.

## Robot learning lesson: loss of plasticity lives in sigma

**The problem.** "Loss of plasticity" is the name for a network that stops adapting to new data. In RL it shows up when the task shifts: new friction, a locked joint, a new reward term. The policy that learned the old task fine barely moves on the new one. Lots of metrics correlate with it. Fewer people agree on why it happens.

**The claim.** [Grieco, D'Elia, Caldarelli and Camoriano](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36309) look at PPO with Gaussian policies, the standard setup in IsaacLab and RSL-RL, the stack your club almost certainly uses. PPO's policy outputs a mean action and adds noise with a standard deviation sigma. During training, sigma usually shrinks as the policy gets confident. The paper argues that this shrinking is a big part of why plasticity dies.

**Why, in plain terms.** The PPO gradient with respect to the mean network carries a factor of 1/sigma. The curvature (how sharp the loss landscape is) carries 1/sigma squared. So as sigma heads toward zero:

- *Gradients blow up,* so each update gets noisier and bigger.
- *The landscape gets sharp,* so a learning rate that was stable last week now overshoots.
- *The useful directions collapse,* because the sharp part of the curvature only spans a few directions. The network effectively has fewer knobs it can turn.

They fit these scalings on a Franka reach task with 4096 parallel envs and 30 seeds, resetting sigma every 500 iterations. The measured exponents come out close to 1 and 2, as the theory predicts, with moderate fits (R squared 0.64 and 0.87).

**The interesting part: many known fixes are secretly sigma fixes.**

- *L2 weight regularization* keeps sigma higher on Slippery Ant (friction randomized every 2M steps), and that is where its benefit comes from. Plain PPO with sigma forced to follow the same schedule as the L2 run does about as well.
- *Continual Backprop*, a method built to fight plasticity loss, looks good mostly because of its effect on sigma, and does about 20% worse when you evaluate the final policy deterministically.
- *Adam settings* that shrink the effective step size slow sigma's collapse, which delays the failure.
- *A sigma-scaled learning rate* alone avoids collapse but stalls around 2000 reward against about 4000 for the others. Taming the symptom isn't the same as keeping exploration alive.

**The robot test.** Train a Franka to reach, lock its base joint in sim, keep training for 500 iterations, then deploy on the real arm at 50 targets. Resetting sigma beat raising the entropy bonus, and both beat doing nothing (mean reward about 0.045 vs roughly minus 0.009 to minus 0.013, highly significant).

**Caveats.** Theory covers isotropic Gaussian policies (one sigma for all joints). Most locomotion setups use a separate sigma per joint, and the Ant experiments, which do, are a looser match. The causal case rests mostly on the sigma-reset experiments. No legged robot, no code.

**What to steal for your humanoid.**

- *Plot sigma per joint, every run.* RSL-RL logs a "mean noise std". Look at it per joint as well. A knee whose sigma has collapsed to near zero while the ankle is still exploring tells you which part of the gait can still change.
- *Curriculum changes are task shifts.* When you raise the speed command range, add rougher terrain or switch on a new reward, you are asking a converged policy to adapt. If nothing improves after the switch, check sigma before you blame the new reward.
- *Put a floor under sigma, or reset it.* A minimum std (clamping the log-std parameter) is one line of config. Bumping sigma back up when you change the curriculum stage is another. Both are cheaper than a fresh run from scratch.
- *Watch the deterministic eval.* You deploy the mean action, not the noisy one. Evaluate both during training. A gap between them is information.

This pairs with the 15 October post on reading PPO curves: a flat reward plus a falling sigma plus a growing gradient norm is the plasticity-loss signature.

## New papers worth reading

### [HC2Robot: humanoid parkour that guesses its next footstep](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36825)
The usual recipe for perceptive locomotion is asymmetric actor-critic: the critic sees privileged simulator state, the actor sees only onboard sensors. The authors argue the actor shouldn't try to recover the privileged state, only a usable approximation of it. Their "predicted foresight" module guesses, from onboard observations, when each foot will next touch down, where, and how good that support will be. A transformer with learnable multi-stream routing does the temporal reasoning, and a short-horizon motion prediction head acts as a regularizer. 97.6% mean traversal success in simulation over stairs, gaps, high platforms and slopes. Simulation only, no code. The idea worth keeping: make the policy predict the thing that actually matters for the next half-second (the next foothold) rather than the full world state.

### [Remember where you walk](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37203)
With Jan Peters among the authors. An LSTM memory has to squeeze everything a legged robot has seen into one vector, and long-horizon details get lost. Local Spatial Matrix Memory stores what matters in cells indexed by position, so "what was over there" lives at "over there". In maze exploration it matches strong recurrent baselines on coverage, is the only architecture that actually remembers the layout, and cuts map reconstruction error by a factor of three. Not your first problem with a robot that can't walk yet, but it's the right shape of idea for when the tasks start: memory organized by space, not by time.

### Also new
- [Whole-body humanoid control via stochastic motion tokenization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36452): discrete motion tokens as the command interface for a G1 whole-body tracker.

## Founder radar

### "We have not achieved ROI on humanoids yet"
On 26 October [Agility Robotics and GXO announced](https://www.therobotreport.com/agility-gxo-logistics-plan-expanded-deployment-digit-humanoid/) that Digit is going to more GXO warehouses for e-commerce fulfillment, with Digit 4 deployments starting early 2027 and a move to Digit 5 when it ships. Digit has moved more than 100,000 totes at GXO's Georgia site since late 2023, Agility counts 65,000-plus hours of real operation, and its SEC-approved SPAC merger with Churchill Capital Corp. XI is set to take it public.

The best line came from GXO's own CEO back in August: they had run 45 humanoid pilots, had not reached ROI, and expected to be about two years away. And yet they are expanding.

Seeds to think about:

- *Customers buy the learning curve, not the robot.* GXO is paying to be first in line when the economics work. That's an option, not a purchase, and it tells you how early-stage robotics actually gets sold.
- *Moving totes is the "hello world" of humanoid work.* Three years, one task, six figures of totes. Whoever makes the second and third task cheap to add owns the next phase. That's a software and robot-learning problem, which is to say your kind of problem.
- *Count the boring numbers.* Hours of operation and units moved are the metrics that survive due diligence. If you ever build a robot company, start logging them on day one.

## Outside your bubble

### [Aging muscle trades strength for survival](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261027063704.htm)
As we age, muscles lose fast-twitch fibers (the powerful, sprint-type ones) and drift toward slow-twitch fibers. The usual reading is decline: things wear out. A team led by the University of Copenhagen, publishing in Nature Aging, suggests part of it is a deliberate defense.

The trail starts with cardiolipin, a fat molecule in the inner membrane of mitochondria. It drops with age and disease in both mice and humans. With less of it, mitochondria leak more reactive oxygen species. Those signals, through a protein called ERR-gamma, push the muscle toward slow-twitch fibers and make it divert glucose into making its own antioxidants. Lowering cardiolipin in young mice reproduced the aging pattern and the wasting. Restoring it to about two-thirds of normal reversed the wasting and prevented early deaths.

The twist is the antioxidant result. In cells, antioxidants reduced the fiber shift. In mice, they made the muscles worse. Blocking the "symptom" removed a protective response. The muscle was giving up strength to survive oxidative stress, and taking away the stress signal took away the protection.

Caveats: the mechanism and the reversal are in mice; human samples only show that cardiolipin declines. A cardiolipin-stabilizing drug, elamipretide, recently got accelerated FDA approval for a rare disorder, Barth syndrome, but nobody knows yet whether it helps ordinary age-related muscle loss.

It rhymes with today's lesson more than I expected. A policy whose sigma shrinks is also trading adaptability for stability, and the fixes that work are the ones that address the cause rather than the visible symptom. Bodies and networks both get conservative with age.
