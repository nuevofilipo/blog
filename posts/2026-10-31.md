# One GPU and a 40 millisecond cliff

*2026-10-31*

Saturday, and Halloween, so here is a properly scary number: 56%. That's how often a freshly trained humanoid motion tracker still succeeds once you add 40 milliseconds of action delay. At 20 ms it was 98.5%. Today's lesson is a small, honest paper that shows what happens when you take one of the big humanoid recipes (NVIDIA's SONIC) and move it to a small robot on one shared GPU, which is roughly the situation a student club is in. Also today: a Stanford paper on making one policy work across different controllers, a 600 million dollar round for self-driving excavators, a startup that wants to own the factory instead of selling robots to one, Palantir and AMD reporting on Monday, a new Robot Learning Paper Club in Zurich, and a snakebite problem far bigger than the official numbers say.

## Robot learning lesson: porting a big recipe to a small robot

**The setup.** [SONIC-K1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39327), by Kyrylo Kolesnichenko and Jong-Hoon Kim, takes SONIC, the universal whole-body motion tracker NVIDIA trained on 128 GPUs for about three days, and retrains it from scratch for the Booster K1. The K1 is a small humanoid with 22 actuated joints: 6 per leg, 4 per arm, 2 in the head, no waist and no wrists. The authors release code, weights and evaluation tools on a [project page](https://kyry.me/sonic-k1), and call it the first open-weights whole-body tracker for this robot.

**The recipe, in numbers.**

- *Compute:* one H100 NVL shared with other jobs, 16,384 parallel Isaac Lab environments, PPO, 50,000 iterations, 135 hours of wall clock (about 103 hours on an unshared GPU). They estimate that's 1 to 1.5% of SONIC's compute.
- *Control:* policy at 50 Hz, physics at 200 Hz, PD control at the joints. The actor has 25.1 million parameters and encodes the target motion into a 64-dimensional quantized token, the same "motion token" idea from the 29 October post.
- *Data:* 142,220 clips from the Bones-SEED dataset, retargeted to the K1, then filtered for joint-limit violations, feet going through the floor, velocity spikes and NaNs. Only 62,427 clips survived. They trained on 9,991 and held out 1,985.
- *Changes from SONIC:* almost none. They added a head-tracking reward, scaled push and center-of-mass randomization down to 0.7, and raised the initial action standard deviation from 0.05 to 0.5. That last one is the interesting fix.

**Results.** In MuJoCo, on held-out clips: 97.4% success with 15.4 mm joint position error. 93.6% on PHUMA, 99.0% on 100STYLE. LAFAN1 is the weak spot: 70.6% on 10-second windows and 18.5% on full clips, with falling-and-getting-up working only 4.2% of the time. A model trained on just 10 clips reached 63.6% success against 93.1% for the 9,991-clip model, though that comparison also changed iteration count and environment count, so it's not a clean data ablation.

On the real K1, run as ONNX at 50 Hz on the onboard computer with a safety belt: upper-body and slow whole-body motions like squats ran smoothly. Steps and turns were less reliable, with visible leg jitter. No mocap was available, so there's no real-robot error number.

**Why the interesting parts matter.**

- *The sigma fix is the 29 October lesson in the wild.* SONIC's initial action std of 0.05 combined with a KL-adaptive learning-rate schedule throttled learning. The KL between two Gaussian policies grows with the squared shift of the mean divided by sigma squared, so with a tiny sigma even small updates look like huge policy changes, and the scheduler keeps cutting the learning rate. Raising sigma to 0.5 unblocked training. If you copy a recipe from a paper trained on another robot, sigma and the learning-rate schedule are among the first things to check.
- *Latency is the cliff, and they didn't train for it.* The authors did not model actuation delay. In simulation: 98.5% success at 20 ms of delay, 56.0% at 40 ms. Meanwhile, it was robust to everything else they tried: PD gains from 0.6 to 1.4 times nominal, 70% motor strength, a 2 kg payload, 3 times the observation noise, real torque caps, pushes up to 3 times the training range. The leg jitter on hardware is very plausibly that latency gap. This is the 26 October lesson ("train with the lag you will have") with a clean number attached.
- *Retargeting throws away more than half your data.* 142k clips went in, 62k came out physically feasible for a robot with no waist and no wrists. Human motion data doesn't fit a small robot for free, and the filter is part of the pipeline, not an afterthought.

**What to steal for your humanoid.**

- *Measure your real loop delay before training anything.* Timestamp the moment an observation is read and the moment the motor driver receives the command, on your actual robot, with your actual comms bus. If it's 15 ms, randomize between about 5 and 25 ms in simulation. If it's 40 ms, the SONIC-K1 numbers say you should treat that as an emergency.
- *Copy a recipe, then check three things.* Initial action std, the learning-rate schedule, and the domain randomization ranges were all tuned for someone else's robot. SONIC-K1 had to change two of the three.
- *Use the 1% compute number to plan.* A full whole-body tracker trained on one H100 in about four days is within reach of a club with cluster access. For the first walk you need far less: a velocity-tracking policy with good rewards trains in hours. But once you move to tasks, a tracker like this is the low-level layer you'd want, and now you know what it costs.
- *Read the code before you write your own.* An open, complete Isaac Lab training setup for a small humanoid, with rewards, randomization and an evaluation harness, is worth an afternoon even if your robot looks nothing like a K1.

**Caveats.** One training seed, no ablations, no hardware tracking metrics, and the SMPL and video input encoders from SONIC were never trained, so you can't drive it straight from human video. The authors' own next steps are exactly the lessons above: train with real torque caps, actuation delay and gain randomization, then measure on hardware properly.

## New papers worth reading

### [Cross-controller transfer via in-context behavior adaptation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39280)
Jonathan Yang, Chelsea Finn and Dorsa Sadigh at Stanford tackle yesterday's annoying question from the other side. Yesterday's paper showed that "position actions" mean different things depending on gains, filters and scaling. This one says: fine, then let the policy figure out which controller it's on. A transformer context encoder reads a short history of commanded actions and the robot motions that followed, and summarizes "how this controller behaves". One policy, trained across six controller types and varying gains and compliance, improves average real-world success by 33 percentage points over just pooling the data. It reaches 50% on unseen controller modes and 52% on unseen gains, and on contact-rich tasks like USB insertion and surface wiping it changes its strategy, not just its actions. Same idea as a walking policy that infers ground friction from its own recent history, applied to the robot's own low-level stack. For your team: if you ever pool data from two robots or two firmware versions, this is the paper to read first.

### [Humanoid Dodgeball](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40003)
Zhang, Wang, Chen, Tang, Xiang, Shi and Ge train a humanoid to dodge incoming projectiles with whole-body moves. Two ingredients: "threat-aware" reward shaping that pays the robot for getting out of the way, and several direction-specific adversarial motion priors (AMP) that keep the dodges stable and human-like. Validated in simulation and on hardware across obstacle speeds and launch distances. The abstract gives no numbers, so this is a "read the PDF" paper. Worth it as a clean example of using multiple style priors instead of one big one.

### Also new
- [UMI-Live](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39385): an in-the-wild bimanual data collection system; a fine-tuned pi-0.5 went 10 out of 10 on a bimanual pick-and-place.
- [Utility-WAM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39367): fixes action shifts in world action models caused by predicted futures; 93.3% on RoboTwin 2.0, 98.9% on LIBERO, 53 of 60 real bimanual trials.
- [Event2Reward](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39212): learns shaping rewards from task progress relations; 60% and 90% real-robot success after 30 minutes of training.

## Founder radar

### Bedrock Robotics: retrofit the machines that already exist
Bedrock raised a 600 million dollar Series C at a 4.1 billion dollar valuation on 26 October, co-led by CapitalG (Alphabet's growth fund) and Emergence, with Nvidia among existing backers ([TNW](https://thenextweb.com/news/bedrock-robotics-600m-series-c-4-1bn-valuation)). The valuation more than doubled since February. Founded in 2024 by ex-Waymo engineers, CEO Boris Sofman previously led Waymo's autonomous trucking. The product is a kit that turns existing excavators into autonomous ones, and since mid-August machines with nobody at the controls have done paid work on live job sites for customers in Arizona, Minnesota, Florida and elsewhere.

### Foundry: don't sell robots to a factory, own the factory
Foundry Robotics raised a 100 million dollar Series A led by Marlinspike Partners, with Khosla and others, reported 28 October ([Runtime Wire](https://runtimewire.com/article/foundry-robotics-100m-series-a-autonomous-factory)). The money builds out a 130,000 square foot factory in Emeryville for "flexible hardware assembly", run by its own software, FoundryOS, which coordinates robotic assembly cells. Its pitch is to use venture money to own manufacturing capacity, not just sell software to manufacturers.

**Seeds to plant.**
- *The fastest path to deployment can skip the new body.* Bedrock didn't design an excavator. It put autonomy on machines customers already own, already trust and already know how to service. For humanoids the analogue is to ask which part of a task is truly humanoid-shaped and which part an existing machine could do with better software.
- *Selling the outcome, not the robot.* Foundry's bet is that a robot-run factory sells assembled hardware, and keeps all the margin on the automation, rather than pricing a robot cell that a customer has to integrate. It needs far more capital, but it puts you in charge of the whole loop, including the data.
- *Ex-Waymo founders keep showing up.* Autonomous driving trained a generation of engineers in safety cases, fleet operations and long-tail testing. Those skills transfer to any robot that has to work unsupervised around people, and investors pay for that track record.

## Outside your bubble

### [Snakebites kill far more people than anyone has been counting](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261029072117.htm)
A team at the Liverpool School of Tropical Medicine updated its 2008 global estimate of snakebite harm, reviewing studies from 79 countries and published in PLOS Medicine. The new numbers: at least 2.1 million envenomings a year, possibly up to 7 million, and between 274,000 and 513,000 deaths. Another 800,000 survivors a year are left with long-term disabilities, including amputations. Sub-Saharan Africa accounts for about 45% of envenomings and deaths, at almost three times the rate of south Asia, with Nigeria, the Democratic Republic of Congo and Ethiopia hardest hit.

Why nobody saw it: more than half the countries with venomous snakes don't record bites at all. Victims are often farm workers in rural areas, far from a clinic, who never show up in health statistics. So the team estimated rates for the missing places with a geostatistical model that combines the data they have with climate, the number of snake species present, and socioeconomic indicators. The caveat is right there in the range: the upper estimate is nearly twice the lower one, because a lot of it is modeled, not counted.

The reason it's here: this is a sim-to-real problem in public health. When the real data is missing, you build a model from things you can measure, accept wide uncertainty bars, and use it to decide where to act: stockpile antivenom, train rural health workers, fix transport. The WHO wants to halve snakebite deaths by 2030, and you can't halve a number you never measured. It's also a reminder that some of the world's most solvable problems are invisible simply because nobody built the sensor.
