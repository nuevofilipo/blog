# The glue code is the model

*2026-11-28*

Good Saturday morning. Here's a sentence that should annoy anyone who has spent a week fine-tuning a policy: with the same models and the same weights, just changing the wiring around them can move the success rate by tens of points. Today's main paper ran 470,000 rollouts to find out which wiring works. The answer is a recipe that adds up to 42.8 points without touching a single weight.

After that: a lesson in why the number of denoising steps in your flow-matching policy isn't the only knob, an open Chinese VLA claiming to beat π0.5 by a wide margin, a $1.6 billion wheeled humanoid, a flow-matching internship in Zurich, and a narwhal tusk that's built like a rope.

## The big one: 470,000 rollouts on how to wire a robot brain

**[Toward Agentic Robotics: A Large-Scale Study of Harness Design for Robot-Use Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34778)** (Yuxiang Lai, Jike Zhong, Yu-Jhe Li, ... Ruixi Fan, 12 authors), submitted 25 November. No code link yet.

The setup is one you'll recognize. There are two kinds of models fighting for the robot's brain:

- **VLMs** (big vision-language models) are great at understanding a scene, remembering what happened, and reasoning about what to do. They are bad at the actual motion: even with long chains of reasoning, they predict 3D gripper poses poorly.
- **VLAs and world-action models (WAMs)** are great at fast, smooth control on tasks they were trained on, and they fall apart when the scene shifts or the task needs memory and multi-step reasoning.

The obvious move is to combine them: the VLM thinks, writes some policy code or calls tools, and hands sub-tasks to the VLA. The authors call the glue between them the **policy harness**. Does the VLM write code or call the VLA directly? How often does it check in? Does the VLA get help from tools such as a grasp detector? When at test time does the VLM step in?

That's thousands of possible configurations, and every one needs thousands of rollouts to evaluate fairly. So most papers pick one design from a handful and move on. These authors did the boring, expensive thing instead: **more than 470,000 rollouts** across **eight simulation benchmarks**, **six VLMs** and **15 VLA/WAM checkpoints**, organized as seven controlled studies.

The headline finding is less a single trick than a principle: **a good harness has to fit both sides**. What helps a strong VLM paired with a weak VLA can hurt the reverse. The studies pin down what structure the VLM's policy code needs, where the VLA actually benefits from tools and code (and where it doesn't), and when it's worth calling the VLM at test time at all. They bundle the lessons into a reference recipe, **X-Harness**, which raises base policies by **up to 42.8 points** on memory, reasoning and domain-shift tasks. No retraining.

**Why this matters to you.** Your club is about to spend months on walking, and walking is a pure low-level policy problem: no harness needed. But the second phase, "teach it tasks", is exactly where this paper lives. The practical takeaways:

1. **Budget for the glue.** If a harness change can swing results by tens of points, then "we fine-tuned for a week and gained 5%" may be the expensive way to get the smaller gain.
2. **Evaluate in combinations, not in isolation.** A VLA that looks worse alone can be the better partner for a given VLM. Pick components by how they play together.
3. **Simulation is what makes this possible.** 470,000 real rollouts would be absurd. A cheap, honest sim evaluation loop is what lets you tune a harness at all, and that's an argument for building one early.

Caveats: everything is in simulation, "up to 42.8" is the best case and not the typical one, and the abstract doesn't list a real-robot check. Read the seven studies themselves rather than just the recipe.

## Robot learning lesson: the steps matter, not just how many

**[Ground Your Steps: Optimizing Sampling Schedules in Flow Matching Robot Policies](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34148)** (Licheng Yang, Yonghao He, ... Hu Su, 6 authors), submitted 25 November. [Code](https://github.com/D-Robotics-AI-Lab/GroundYourSteps).

Quick refresher, because flow matching is now in most of the policies you'll touch (π0.5 and GR00T among them). A flow-matching policy doesn't output an action directly. It starts from random noise and integrates a learned **velocity field** that pushes that noise toward a plausible action chunk. You can't integrate continuously on a robot, so you take a fixed number of steps; each step costs one forward pass, a **neural function evaluation (NFE)**.

Most people treat NFE as the only knob: more steps, better actions, more latency. This paper points at the other knob. With, say, five steps, **where** in the noise-to-action path you place them changes the result. That placement is the **sampling schedule**, and usually it's just uniform spacing.

GYS picks the schedule per task and per step budget, without any training. The trick is neat:

- Take each joint's action sequence and apply **fractional differencing** (a smoothed form of "subtract the previous value"), so you compare motion patterns, not raw positions.
- Compare two sets of differences: generated chunk versus demonstration, and generated chunk versus another independent generated chunk. If the policy matches the demonstrations, those two distributions match.
- Minimize the **energy distance** between them over the schedule.

The result: **+2.8 percentage points** for both π0.5 and GR00T N1.7 on RoboTwin 2.0, at nearly every step budget, and up to **+3.3 points** on three real tasks. The schedule change is the only change.

Small numbers, but think about what it costs: zero training, zero extra latency. On an embedded humanoid computer where every forward pass hurts, "same number of steps, placed better" is a free lunch. And it echoes the main paper: there's performance hiding in everything **around** the weights.

## Seed for your brain: sell the harness, not the model

Put the two papers above next to each other and a startup shape falls out. Everyone is racing to train the best robot foundation model, which takes hundreds of millions of dollars. Almost nobody is selling the layer that makes **any** foundation model work better on a specific customer's robot: the harness, the sampling schedule, the evaluation loop that tunes them.

Picture a company that takes a customer's robot, their chosen VLA and their tasks, runs thousands of sim rollouts over harness configurations, and ships the best wiring plus a regression suite. It's model-agnostic, so it benefits every time someone releases a better model instead of being crushed by it. The hard part is the moat: if the recipe is a paper, anyone can copy it. The defensible piece is probably the evaluation infrastructure and the data from many customers on which harnesses work for which robots. Worth chewing on.

## Quick hits from the arXiv batch

- **[Cairn](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34885)** steers a **frozen** π0.5 with learned 3D object trajectories, improving success when objects are moved in LIBERO scenes. Same theme as today: help the model from outside.
- **[Co-World Model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34302)** learns skills from a single human video by aligning human and robot actions through their **effect on the object**, not through body kinematics. That neatly dodges the "humans don't have grippers" problem. Tested on two physical robots.
- **[DataForgeVLA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34287)** builds a 340-episode manipulation dataset that's fully synthetic: a video model imagines the demo, then a trajectory is recovered from it.
- **[RePace](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34864)** uses a language model to pick how much to speed up each segment of a demonstration before VLA fine-tuning. Teleoperated demos are slow, so policies that copy them are slow too.
- **[Graph Anchors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34509)** makes planning in a frozen latent world model work for distant goals by planning through intermediate "anchor" states. Training-free.

## Outside the bubble: the narwhal's tusk is a rope

A narwhal's tusk is a tooth (usually the upper left canine of males) that grows up to about **2 metres** long, roughly half the animal's body length, and it spirals. A study in *Nature Communications* ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261126063316.htm)), led by Aarhus University with the Paul Scherrer Institute in Villigen, found out what that spiral is hiding.

The team used **X-ray tensor tomography** at synchrotrons in Switzerland, Sweden and France. They rotated the tooth in the beam, took millions of measurements, and rebuilt a 3D map of how the nanoscale collagen fibres are oriented. The spiral didn't show up at first; it only appeared when they compared neighbouring points.

What they found: **two helices twisting in opposite directions**. The thin outer layer, the cementum, forms a left-handed helix. The dentin underneath spirals the other way. That's exactly how rope is made: strands twisted one way, plied together the other way, so the twisting forces cancel and the whole thing resists torsion. Add mineral crystals for hardness and collagen fibres for reinforcement, and you get something the researchers compare to reinforced concrete.

Caveats: the article doesn't say how many tusks were scanned or how much torsion resistance the design adds, and nobody fully agrees on what the tusk is for (mate selection and social "duels" are the leading guesses).

Why it's worth your two minutes: evolution found counter-rotating layers long before rope-makers did. Whenever you design a robot limb or a gripper finger, the best structural ideas are often already sitting in a museum drawer. And it's a nice ETH-adjacent detail that part of the imaging ran at PSI, an hour from your lab.
