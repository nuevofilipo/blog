# The first day the policy touches metal

*2026-10-12*

Yesterday's post ended with a table full of numbers and a checkpoint that passed the test suite. Today is about what happens next: the first time that policy drives real motors. It is the most nerve-racking afternoon in a humanoid project, and also the one where a written checklist does the most good. Also today: a humanoid that learns to catch boxes thrown at its face, a wearable that teaches robots to use their elbows and hips, a recipe for turning a table-tennis video into a humanoid skill, a pre-seed humanoid company that walked in five months, and Neanderthal glue that turns out to fight bacteria.

## Robot learning lesson: a hardware bring-up protocol

**The goal of day one is not walking.** It is finding out, cheaply, every way in which the real robot differs from the one in simulation, before any of those differences can throw 40 kg of custom hardware at the floor. Treat the first session as a series of gates. Each gate has a pass condition, and you only move to the next one when it passes. If a gate fails, you go back to the desk, not forward with crossed fingers.

**Gate 0: safety before software.** A hardware emergency stop that cuts motor power, not a key in a terminal, within reach of a person whose only job is to press it. The robot hangs from a gantry or crane with the slack set so the feet just touch the ground and a fall stops a few centimeters down. Joint position and torque limits are enforced in the motor drivers or the lowest software layer, set tighter than the hardware limits, so a bad action can never ask for more than you allowed. A watchdog that switches the motors to damping mode (zero position gain, small velocity gain) if the policy stops sending commands for more than a few control cycles. Damping mode, not "off": a limp robot falls fast, a damped one sags slowly.

**Gate 1: is the robot the one in the URDF?** With the policy not running, move each joint by hand in damping mode and check that the sign and zero of every reading match the simulator. Then command a slow sine on one joint at a time with the deployed PD gains. Three bugs show up here in almost every project: a joint with flipped direction, an encoder offset of a few degrees, and left and right legs swapped in the joint order. The robot-description audit on 10 October covered the description; this is the same audit against the real thing. Also tilt the robot by hand and check that the IMU reports gravity in the frame and with the signs the policy expects.

**Gate 2: open-loop replay.** Take a short trajectory from simulation, for example the default standing pose and a slow squat, and play it on the hanging robot as position targets. Log commanded and measured joint positions at the full control rate. Compare with the same replay in simulation. Large tracking lag means the actuator model or the PD gains are off (6 October's actuator-modeling post); oscillation means the gains are too high for the real motor and transmission. Fix it here, because no policy will.

**Gate 3: the policy in the loop, still hanging, zero command.** Run the policy with a zero velocity command and the feet just loaded. A good standing policy holds still with small corrections. Watch for three things: chatter (fast small oscillations, usually latency or too little action smoothing), drift toward a joint limit, and the policy stepping in place, which often means its observations are wrong. Before blaming the policy, record the observation vector on hardware and compare it with what the policy saw in simulation for the same pose. A wrong scale on joint velocities or a mis-ordered observation is far more common than a bad policy.

**Gate 4: lower the gantry in steps.** Add load in small increments until the robot carries its full weight, still with slack that catches a fall. Only then give small forward commands, around 0.1 to 0.2 m/s, and raise them over several runs. Keep a person at the stop and stop at the first sign of a stumble.

**Log everything, every time.** Commands, observations, actions, joint positions, velocities, motor currents and temperatures, at the control rate, with the git hash of the policy and the firmware. Name the log after the run in the evaluation table. When something goes wrong, the log lets you replay the exact observation sequence through the policy on your laptop and see what it was thinking. Without logs, a fall is a story; with them, it is a bug report.

**A rule that saves hardware:** never change two things between runs. New gains and a new checkpoint in the same run means that when it fails, you have learned nothing.

## New papers worth reading

### [Reflex: Visually Grounded Reactive Humanoid Control](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12772)
Submitted this week. A Unitree G1 catches boxes thrown by people, using only its own head-mounted RGB-D camera. The interesting part is the three-stage structure. First, an RL policy learns whole-body catching with perfect knowledge of the box state. Second, it learns to infer a compact description of the box's motion from delayed and incomplete observations. Third, a vision encoder learns to produce that same description from images, while the controller stays frozen. In simulation, vision-based catching reaches 85.7 percent success against 87.9 percent with privileged box state, so the vision stage loses almost nothing. Removing the image history drops success by about 36 to 40 points: a single frame does not tell you where a box is going. On the real robot it catches 65 percent of boxes from 2.5 m and 40 percent from 5 m, which the authors put at 76 and 59 percent of human catchers. The pattern of training with privileged information and then distilling into a perception module is the same one many walking policies use, applied to a much faster task.

### [PanTact: Teaching Whole-Body Dexterity with a Wearable Contact Interface](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12558)
From a team including Shuran Song and Guanya Shi. Humans carry a box against their chest, push a door with a hip, and lean on a table. Most humanoid manipulation research still uses only the hands. PanTact is a wearable rig, without a robot, that combines a head camera, two handheld UMI-style grippers, and contact patches on the body to record where and when the human touches things. A policy predicts contact goals (which surface should touch what, and when), and a contact-aware whole-body controller decides how the robot's own body achieves them. Deployed on a G1 for pushing, sweeping, body support and carrying loads together with the arms. The idea worth keeping: contacts, not joint angles, are the part of a demonstration that transfers across very different bodies. No numbers in the abstract, so check the [project page](https://pantact.github.io) before getting too excited.

### [VICAR: Video Imitation with Contact-Aware Retargeting for Humanoid Interactive Skills](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13079)
From Dvij Kalaria, Bike Zhang and S. Shankar Sastry. Retargeting human video to a humanoid usually keeps the pose and loses the contacts: the hand ends up a few centimeters from the ladder rung, which is the whole point. VICAR reconstructs the human motion and the relevant obstacles from a single video, then retargets with contact poses and collision clearance as high-priority constraints, so the robot's hand lands where the human's did. A generator conditioned on contact points then adapts the skill when the paddle or the rung is somewhere else. Shown on six table-tennis serves, three pickups and ladder climbing. Relevant for the "teach it tasks" phase after walking: if the club ever wants skills from YouTube instead of a mocap suit, contact preservation is the step that most pipelines skip.

## Founder radar

### [Minerva Humanoids: idea to walking humanoid in five months, 10 million dollars pre-seed](https://thenextweb.com/news/minerva-humanoids-10m-pre-seed-general-catalyst)
Announced 6 October. Minerva builds Roger, a humanoid for jobs nobody should do in person: bomb disposal, offshore rigs, high-voltage plants, hazardous materials. The team: CEO Sandor Felber (ex-Tesla Optimus, MIT CSAIL humanoids), CTO Maurice Rahme (six years at Boston Dynamics, technical lead on Stretch), and a British special forces veteran as head of hazardous operations. General Catalyst led the round, with Long Journey and Credo co-leading and Hugging Face among the backers.

What makes it interesting is the choice not to wait for autonomy. Roger is teleoperated through a VR headset for the hands and the task; the AI handles balance, fall recovery and navigation. That is exactly the split where robot learning is mature today, locomotion, and the split where humans are still far better, judgment on a bomb. In bomb disposal, a robot that is remote-controlled is already the standard, so the customer does not need to trust a policy with the decision, only with staying upright. Paid pilots start this autumn, prototypes are built in Germany, and the company says it avoids single-source Chinese components, a selling point for defense buyers.

**Idea seed.** Find the job where teleoperation is already accepted and the current robot is bad at moving: tracked EOD robots that cannot climb stairs, inspection crawlers that cannot open doors. A learned locomotion layer under a human operator is a product you can sell now, and every hour of teleoperation is training data for the autonomous version later.

## Outside your bubble

### [Neanderthal glue was probably also medicine](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261008005256.htm)
Reported 10 October, published in PLOS One. Neanderthals heated birch bark to make tar, the oldest known synthetic material, and used it to glue stone points onto wooden handles. A team led by Tjaark Siemssen (Cologne and Oxford) asked whether it might also have been good for wounds. They made tar with two methods Neanderthals could plausibly have used, dry distillation in a sealed pit and condensing smoke on a cool stone, and tested it against *Staphylococcus aureus*, a common cause of wound infections. Every sample inhibited the bacteria, whichever method made it. The study cannot prove that anyone actually smeared it on a cut, and a lab result is not a treatment.

Two things to take away. First, a tool built for one job turning out to solve a second is an old story, and it is how many startups find their real market. Second, notice the method: when you cannot observe the past, you rebuild the process with the means available then and test what comes out. That is replication by reconstruction, and it is not far from what a sim-to-real team does when it rebuilds the real robot in a simulator to understand why something went wrong.
