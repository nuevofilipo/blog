# Teaching a robot what a step is: reward design for bipedal gaits

*2026-10-16*

Here is an uncomfortable truth about RL locomotion: the robot never learns to walk. It learns to maximize a number you wrote, and walking is just the cheapest way to get that number if you wrote it well. Write it badly and you get a robot that shuffles, skates, vibrates or hops on one foot with complete confidence. Yesterday was about reading the training curves. Today is about the thing those curves measure, the reward, and in particular the trick that turns "move forward somehow" into an actual gait. Also today: MIT making motion tracking squishy, video generators drawing references directly in a robot's joint space, two humanoids playing table tennis doubles, a memory that ticks in seconds instead of frames, a Stockholm startup putting humanoid factories into shipping containers, and patients under anesthesia whose brains keep predicting the next word of a story.

## Robot learning lesson: rewarding a gait, not just motion

Most humanoid walking rewards fall into three buckets. Once you see them that way, a reward file stops looking like a random list of forty terms.

**1. The task terms (what you want).**
- *Linear velocity tracking* in x and y, and *yaw rate tracking*. Almost always an exponential kernel, `exp(-error^2 / sigma)`, not a raw negative error. The kernel is bounded between 0 and 1, gives a strong gradient near the target and does not explode when the robot falls. Sigma around 0.25 is a common starting point. Make it too small and the reward is zero almost everywhere early in training, so the policy never finds the signal.
- These terms should carry the largest weights. If your penalties added together can outweigh velocity tracking, you get the standing-still failure from yesterday.

**2. The regularizers (what you tolerate).**
- Torques, joint accelerations, *action rate* (the change between consecutive actions), joint limits, base height, base orientation, angular velocity in roll and pitch.
- Each one is a small push toward hardware-friendly behaviour. Action rate is the most important for sim-to-real: it is the difference between a policy that drives motors and one that rattles them.
- Start them small, or ramp them in over the first few thousand iterations. Regularizers that are strong from step zero teach the robot that doing nothing is optimal.

**3. The gait terms (what makes it walking).** This is the part that separates humanoids from quadrupeds, and it is where most of the design work goes.
- *Feet air time.* The classic legged_gym term pays the robot when a foot lands, in proportion to how long it was in the air (minus an offset of around 0.5 s for quadrupeds, often 0.3 to 0.4 s for humanoids). It is only active when the command is non-zero. Without it, policies love to shuffle with feet that barely leave the ground, because that is the lowest-risk way to move.
- *Foot clearance.* Rewards swing-foot height near a target such as 8 to 10 cm, often weighted by foot velocity so that it only matters while the foot is actually moving. This prevents toe-stubbing on real floors, which are never as flat as simulation.
- *Foot slip.* Penalizes horizontal foot velocity while the foot is in contact. A skating gait in simulation becomes a falling gait on real flooring with different friction.
- *Feet distance and knee distance.* Keep the feet apart. Humanoids in simulation love to cross their legs, because nothing tells them it will end in tears.
- *Contact forces.* Penalize impacts above a threshold. Your gearboxes will thank you.

**The big idea: give the policy a clock.** A periodic gait has a rhythm, and you can hand that rhythm to the robot explicitly. Add a phase variable that cycles from 0 to 1 over a gait period (about 0.6 to 0.8 s for a small humanoid), feed `sin(2 pi phase)` and `cos(2 pi phase)` into the observation, and define which foot should be in stance and which in swing at each point of the cycle. Left foot in stance for the first half and right foot for the second, with a small double-support window in between. Then reward contact when the schedule says stance and penalize it when the schedule says swing.

This is the idea behind the periodic reward composition from Oregon State's Cassie work (Siekmann and colleagues, 2021), and it is how Humanoid-Gym, Unitree's rl_gym examples and many club-built bipeds get their first clean gait. It works because it removes the hardest exploration problem, discovering alternation, and replaces it with a much easier one, following a beat.

Practical notes for your first runs:
- *Zero command, zero clock.* When the velocity command is zero, either stop the clock and reward both feet in contact, or the robot will march in place forever. Marching in place is not a failure, but it drains batteries and annoys everyone watching the demo.
- *Pick the period from the hardware, not from humans.* Shorter legs mean shorter periods. If your swing phase is too long for your motors' speed limits, the policy will learn to cheat the schedule.
- *The clock is a crutch you can remove later.* Once a policy walks, you can anneal the gait terms toward zero, or move to motion-tracking rewards (the 14 October lesson) and let human data provide the rhythm. Many teams keep the clock for good, and that is fine.
- *Check per-term contributions.* After a few thousand iterations, log what fraction of total reward each term provides. If feet air time or contact scheduling provides more than velocity tracking, the robot will optimize for beautiful steps in place. Rebalance.

**A sane order of operations.** Get the robot standing (alive and orientation terms only). Add velocity tracking and the clock. Add feet terms. Add regularizers last, ramped in. Only then turn on harder terrain and the full domain randomization. Change one thing per run, and name every run after what changed. Future you, at 1 a.m., will be grateful.

Further reading: [Siekmann et al., Sim-to-Real Learning of All Common Bipedal Gaits via Periodic Reward Composition](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.01387), [Humanoid-Gym](https://github.com/roboterax/humanoid-gym) (read its reward config, it is the best annotated example), and [legged_gym](https://github.com/leggedrobotics/legged_gym) for the original feet air time term.

## New papers worth reading

### [Compliant GMT: Making General Motion Tracking Compliant via Residual Reference Adaptation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22119)
Pulkit Agrawal's group at MIT tackles a real problem with general motion trackers: they are stiff. They reproduce the reference motion faithfully, even when a human is pushing back, which is great for dance videos and bad for anything that touches people. Instead of retraining, C-GMT keeps a pretrained tracker frozen and adds a small residual adapter. The adapter reads a history of proprioception and bends the reference so that the robot yields to contact. The result is close to state-of-the-art trackers on accuracy and close to single-motion compliant policies on compliance, shown on a Unitree G1. Why it matters for you: once your humanoid walks, the next thing people will do is touch it. "Freeze the big model, train a small residual on top" is also a pattern you can afford with a student compute budget. [Project page](https://venki1899.github.io/c-gmt).

### [KinoGen: Customizable Humanoid Loco-Manipulation References via Kinematically Grounded Video Generation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20552)
Generating motion references from video usually means: generate a human video, estimate the human pose, retarget to the robot, then repair the floating feet and the hands going through the table. KinoGen skips the human. It renders the actual robot and object in simulation, conditions a video generator on those renders, and then fits the robot model directly to the generated two-view video. The reference lives in the robot's own joint space from the start. Across eight loco-manipulation tasks it shows less penetration and foot skating than video baselines and keeps over 90% of annotated contacts. You can also edit a keyframe (say, move the box to a new place), regenerate and get a fresh reference. Tracking policies trained this way ran on a G1 with dexterous hands. If your club ever needs reference motions for a custom robot no one has motion capture for, this is the direction to watch. [Project page](https://kinogen-humanoid.github.io).

### [PingpongDuo: Agile Humanoid Table Tennis Doubles with Safe Team Coordination](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20579)
Singles table tennis is hard. Doubles is harder, because you have to get out of your partner's way within a second. The architecture is a good lesson in decomposition: a team planner picks positions and filters them through learned risk estimates, separate footwork and striking skills are trained from each other's end states so that the transitions are smooth, and a residual on the racket arm corrects shots using a simulator calibrated against real ball flight. One robot clears 2.15 m and recovers in 2.4 s at up to 2.23 m/s. Two robots manage over ten strikes in strict alternation. Note the trick of training skill B from the states that skill A ends in: that is exactly the problem you will hit when chaining "walk" into "pick up". [Project page](https://www.archon.tech/research/PingpongDuo).

### [Physical Time as a Missing Dimension of Robot Policy Memory Dynamics](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20524)
Most policy memories count steps, not seconds. If your camera drops frames or your control loop jitters, a step-based memory quietly gets time wrong. Temporal Flow Policy makes memory evolve with measured elapsed time between observations, then conditions diffusion and flow-matching policies (Diffusion Policy and pi0.5) on it. It beats recurrent and state-space memories at the same intervals. Fun detail: feeding in fake intervals predictably delays or advances what the robot does, so the memory really is tracking time. A small idea with a long reach. Every real robot has an irregular clock.

## Founder radar

### [120ft puts humanoid factories in shipping containers](https://www.unite.ai/120ft-funding-humanoid-robot-microfactories/)
Stockholm-based 120ft came out of stealth on 15 October with a 576,000 euro angel round from 22 angels, among them people from EQT, Cherry Ventures and Planet A. Founder Martin Willers previously co-founded the audio brand Transparent. The product is three 40-foot containers that form an autonomous microfactory staffed by humanoids. Brands send parts and specs and pay per finished unit. Software turns a bill of materials and quality requirements into instructions, simulates the line to find bottlenecks and recovers from failed tasks. The company claims teaching time for a new skill fell from seven weeks to seven hours, with seven minutes as the 2027 goal. Those numbers are unaudited.

Two seeds to plant. First, the business model: they do not sell robots, they sell units built. That sidesteps the hardest question in humanoids, which is who buys the first robot. Second, the bottleneck they are attacking is skill onboarding time, not hardware. Whoever makes "new task to deployed policy" fast and boring wins a lot of markets at once. A student team that has trained a biped from scratch knows more about that pipeline than most founders do.

## Outside your bubble

### [Under anesthesia, the brain is still predicting the next word](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261015003440.htm)
A Baylor College of Medicine team led by neurosurgeon Sameer Sheth recorded hundreds of individual hippocampal neurons with Neuropixels probes in epilepsy surgery patients under general anesthesia, while playing them tones and stories. The study, published in Nature, found that the "unconscious" hippocampus still told surprising tones apart and got better at it during the session. It also encoded word categories such as nouns, verbs and adjectives, and its activity could predict upcoming words in a sentence. The caveats are real: one anesthetic, one brain region, and no evidence that the patients understood anything. The comparison to language models is an analogy, not a mechanism.

Why it is here: you spend your days building systems that are prediction machines with nobody home. This study suggests the boundary between "processing" and "experiencing" runs right through our own heads, and that a large amount of predictive machinery keeps running when the lights are off. It is a useful humility check the next time someone, maybe you, says a model "understands" a task. It also raises a question with no good answer yet: what exactly is the extra thing consciousness adds on top of prediction?
