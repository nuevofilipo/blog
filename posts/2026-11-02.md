## Robot learning lesson: first-order gradients, or why PPO plays darts blindfolded

**The two ways to get a gradient.** Every RL method for your humanoid has to answer one question: if I nudge the policy's output, does the return go up? There are two ways to find out.

- *Zeroth order (PPO and friends).* Treat the simulator as a black box. Sample actions with some noise, see which ones led to higher returns, and push the policy towards them. This is the likelihood-ratio trick. It only needs rewards, never derivatives, so contacts, friction cones and joint limits don't bother it at all. The price is variance: you're estimating a gradient from random trials, so you need millions of them. That's why you run 4,096 parallel environments.
- *First order.* The physics step is a function, state and action in, next state out. If you can differentiate it, you can chain-rule the reward back through every step to the action that caused it. That gives you the actual direction of improvement, not a noisy estimate of it. In theory it's far more sample-efficient.

**Why first order rarely works for legged robots.** Two reasons, and both come from your robot's feet.

- *Contacts aren't smooth.* The moment a foot touches the ground, the dynamics change abruptly. Near those events, the derivative can be huge or undefined. Most gradient samples are fine, but a few are enormous, and one enormous sample in a batch wrecks the update.
- *Long horizons multiply.* Backpropagating through 100 steps means multiplying 100 Jacobians. If they're slightly larger than one, the product explodes; if slightly smaller, it vanishes. Walking is a chaotic system, so this happens constantly. The standard fix, SHAC (short-horizon actor-critic), cuts the horizon into short windows and lets a learned critic estimate the rest. On whole-body humanoid tracking, the authors report that SHAC still stops on exploding gradients within minutes.

**What [nabla-Mimic](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41654) does differently.** Weyl Lu and Yubei Chen make the case that exact simulator gradients beat the surrogate gradients a recent world-model method got from a learned model, once you remove two problems.

- *Clip each sample relative to the median, at every backward step.* Instead of one global gradient-norm clip at the end, they bound each sample's gradient relative to the median of the batch, at every step of the backward pass. The rare contact-induced blowups get cut down before they spread backwards through time. The idea is simple: when your noise is heavy-tailed, the median tells you what "normal" looks like and the mean doesn't.
- *Turn gradients into targets, then regress.* Simulating a window and computing its adjoint (the backward pass through the contact solver) is expensive. So rather than taking one gradient step per window, they use the gradient to compute improved action targets, then train the policy on those targets with plain supervised regression, several times. Each target is bounded no matter how large the raw gradient was, and one expensive simulation gets reused for several updates. It's the same move PPO makes when it runs several epochs on one rollout, made in gradient space.
- *Make the simulator fast enough to differentiate.* NablaEngine, their open-source GPU engine for the Unitree G1, computes MuJoCo's full contact dynamics and runs the adjoint of a whole control step as a single fused GPU kernel. Its analytic gradients match float64 finite differences, and it runs 11.5 times faster than the existing MuJoCo Warp adjoint.

**Results.** One policy tracking all 40 LAFAN1 motion clips, on one GPU. It reaches the 0.82 score of the best world-model method in 20 minutes instead of 1.2 hours, and gets to 0.874 after 1.8 hours.

**Why this is worth your morning.** The 31 October post showed what PPO costs: about 100 GPU hours for a full whole-body tracker on a small robot. If first-order methods really get you there an order of magnitude faster, the bottleneck shifts from compute to how quickly you can fix your reward, and that changes how a student team works. You'd change a reward term and see the answer before lunch, not on Thursday.

**What to steal for your humanoid.**

- *Don't switch your walking pipeline yet.* nabla-Mimic is motion tracking in simulation, on a G1, in its own engine. The abstract reports no hardware results. For a first walk on a custom robot, PPO in Isaac Lab or MuJoCo Playground is still the boring, correct choice: robust to contact mess, well documented, and tolerant of a robot model you haven't perfectly measured yet.
- *Steal the median-relative clipping now.* It works with any gradient that has rare huge outliers. If your PPO training spikes occasionally, look at per-sample advantage or gradient magnitudes before you blame the learning rate.
- *Steal the "targets, not steps" pattern.* Computing a bounded target and regressing onto it is a robust way to use any noisy improvement signal: a planner, a teacher policy, a differentiable model. It's essentially how teacher-student distillation works, which you'll likely use once you add perception.
- *Watch the engine.* Supporting a new robot in a differentiable engine means getting its URDF, inertias and actuator model right, which you need for sim-to-real anyway. Once your robot walks with PPO, a first-order tracker for teaching it motions is a plausible club project.

**Caveats.** One robot, one benchmark, only tracking (no velocity-command walking), and an engine that currently supports only the G1. Whether first-order policies are as robust to domain randomization and real-world latency as PPO policies is the open question, and nothing in the abstract answers it.

## New papers worth reading

### [Joint-health-aware RL: when one motor gets weaker](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41348)
Haifa Zhang, Yijing Wang and Zhiqiang Zuo model something every custom robot will experience: individual actuators losing authority. Think of a knee motor that derates as it heats up during a test session, or a gearbox that has started to slip. They describe each joint's condition as a continuous health value between healthy and degraded, apply it as a multiplier on the executed action, and train a small HealthNet that estimates per-joint health from recent observations and actions. The policy uses that estimate to shift effort onto healthy joints. Tested in simulation on Walker, Go2, Cassie and Panda manipulation tasks, locomotion returns improve by up to 250% over a nominal policy. On the heavily degraded Cassie tasks, return goes from 3.94 to 40.5. The important comparison is against a policy trained with degradation but without the health estimate, and that one loses too. So it's the explicit per-joint estimate that helps, not just seeing degradation during training. For your team: SONIC-K1 randomized motor strength globally to 70%. Randomizing it per joint, independently, costs one line in your config and is closer to how real hardware fails. Simulation only, so treat the numbers as an upper bound.

### [VLA policies don't know when not to act](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41609)
A safety paper for when your robot moves on to tasks. Minsu Jang and colleagues give six VLA checkpoints, including two GR00T models, empty, nonsense, impossible and wrong instructions in LIBERO. With an empty instruction, the robot started moving in every episode with every checkpoint, and five of the six often finished some task nobody had asked for. Adding "if no task is given, do not move" to the prompt stopped none of them. What did work in five of six: a gate that compares the first action chunk for the real instruction with the chunk for an empty instruction, and holds the robot if they're nearly the same. Fine-tuning to stand still stopped unwanted task completions, but not the robot starting to move. The lesson: language conditioning in these models is weaker than the demos suggest, so build a "should I be moving at all" check outside the policy.

### Also new
- [Movens-Zero](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41877): a humanoid motion model trained on Movens-5M, which is 5 million physically verified humanoid motion sequences (over 10,000 hours) mined from internet video and aligned with text, speech, music and video. They show steady gains from scaling both data (1% to 100%) and model size (64M to 911M parameters). Compare SONIC-K1, where retargeting threw away more than half the clips: here the filtering pipeline is the product.
- [Learning from fleet experience across 96 robots](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25736): instead of more expert demos, the robots retrieve successful segments that match their own failures and fine-tune on those, with operators labeling only success or failure. On multi-stage phone packaging, 96 robots raise success from 79.2% to 98.3%. A clean example of a data flywheel that doesn't need more teleoperation.
- [REA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.42053): a runtime that pairs a slow reasoning model with fast VLA and whole-body policies under one rule: a running policy never waits for the slow model. It reaches 95.9% on LIBERO-Pro and runs on a real G1. Worth reading for the systems design once you have more than one skill to switch between.

## Founder radar

### [Bleu Robotics: a humanoid deployed in under an hour](https://sifted.eu/articles/french-robotics-startup-industrial-humanoids-20-minutes-of-training/)
Paris-based Bleu Robotics raised a 5 million euro pre-seed, led by Contrarian Ventures and OVNI Capital and announced on 29 October. The pitch: humanoid hardware exists, but every factory deployment is still a custom integration project. Bleu sells the software layer. An operator demonstrates a task at the workstation with Bleu's handheld grippers, about ten demonstrations, no code, and the robot learns it. The goal is a full deployment in under an hour. They say they're already deploying with automotive manufacturers and the paper maker WEPA, which is also an investor. Note the shape of the bet: they don't build the robot, they sell the last mile to whoever does. The hour-long deployment is still a claim, and it's exactly what to check when they announce a customer.

**The seed to plant:** the handheld-gripper approach (the UMI idea from the 31 October post) has gone from research tool to startup product in about two years. Ask yourself which other research data-collection rig could follow the same path.

### The data shovel sellers
The October [Robotics Roundup](https://roboticsroundup.substack.com/p/robotics-roundup-34-october-2026) by F-Prime Capital counts 28 robotics rounds last month, and singles out training-data companies as a theme. The clearest example is [Maxinsights](https://thenextweb.com/news/maxinsights-30m-seed-robot-training-data), a 30 million dollar seed led by Peak XV: it pays more than 5,000 contractors in six countries to film themselves doing chores with head, wrist and chest cameras. It says it supplied all 200,000 hours of first-person video behind Genesis AI's GENE-26.5, and next it wants a million hours of force data from a sensor glove in 2027. Those are company claims. Put it next to Movens-Zero above and the pattern is clear: whoever owns clean, verified, physically executable data has something every model lab needs.

## Outside your bubble

### [Trees know their own shape, and straighten themselves with it](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261030003006.htm)
A team at INRAE in France, led by Bruno Moulia, wanted to know how a bent tree trunk gets straight again. Gravity and light obviously play a part: lay a young poplar on its side and the stem curves upward. But then it doesn't overshoot. It straightens out. Is that just gravity, or does the tree sense its own curvature?

To separate the two, they grew poplars horizontally until the stems had a clear upward curve, about 10 days, then put them on a slowly rotating platform inside an evenly lit sphere. Rotation averages out gravity's direction, and the sphere removes any directional light. With no outside cue left, the stems still straightened over the following weeks. The mechanism is tension wood, a special wood that contracts as it forms. It stopped forming on the upper side that had pulled the stem up and started forming on the opposite side, acting like an opposing muscle. The paper, in New Phytologist, concludes that the tree's sense of its own shape, its proprioception, decides where that wood grows. The press release gives no sample sizes or angles, so treat the details with care until you've read the paper.

The reason it's here: it's a control loop built from growth instead of motors, running over weeks instead of milliseconds, with proprioception as the feedback signal and two antagonistic actuators. Botanists have suspected plant proprioception since a 2012 PNAS paper, and practical uses (sturdier forests, crops that don't fall over in storms, better timber) are still possibilities rather than results. It's also a nice reminder that "knowing your own state" is the first thing any body needs, whether it's made of wood or carbon fiber.
