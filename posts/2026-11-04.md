# Put the box in the simulator

*2026-11-04*

Wednesday. AMD reported last night and, unlike Palantir on Monday, the market actually said thank you. Today's lesson comes from the RAI Institute: a humanoid that carries boxes without looking like a forklift on stilts. The trick is almost embarrassingly simple. If your low-level controller will spend its life carrying things, let it carry things during training, even if it never sees them. Also today: a model-based controller that shrugs off a 245 newton shove, a VLA trained on 16,000 hours of people holding grippers and no robot at all, Cognex tonight, a quiet Luma week, and Voyager 1 about to become one light-day away from home.

## Robot learning lesson: object-aware motion priors

**The standard recipe, and its blind spot.** Most humanoid loco-manipulation stacks have two layers. At the bottom, a whole-body controller (WBC) trained with RL that takes commands like "base at this height, hands here, walk at this velocity". On top, a task policy that decides what those commands should be. The bottom layer is usually trained with hand-tuned rewards and no object in the scene. That has two costs. The motion looks stiff and robotic, because nobody writes a reward for "move like a person". And the controller has never felt a payload, so when a 5 kg box suddenly hangs off both wrists, it's seeing forces it has never trained on.

**What [HLO-AMP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05113) does.** Jean-Pierre Sleiman, Farbod Farshidian, Jessica Hodgins and colleagues at the RAI Institute keep the two-layer structure but change how each layer is trained.

- *Low level: imitation, with the box physically there.* They capture a human carrying boxes, retarget it to a Unitree G1, and train one tracking policy across four kinds of motion: walking empty-handed, walking with a box, picking up, and putting down. The key detail: the box exists in the simulation and pushes back on the hands, but the policy never observes it. It only sees proprioception plus a 12-number command (base height, roll and pitch, planar velocity, two hand positions). So the controller learns to absorb whatever load shows up, without needing to know where the load is.
- *High level: free, but fenced in.* The task policy is trained with RL on top of the frozen controller, with no reference motions. It sees the box's faces and a goal. Its outputs pass through a clip whose bounds come from the range of commands the low level actually saw in its training data. In plain words: the planner may only ask for things the controller has practiced.
- *Training details.* IsaacLab, 8,192 parallel environments, PPO with a privileged critic, 50 Hz, boxes from 0.5 to 8 kg, and the usual domain randomization of friction, mass and actuator gains.

**Numbers.** In 10,000 simulated rollouts, 80.7% task success at 20 seconds (87.9% at 40). A single end-to-end policy reaches 3.6%. A standard WBC trained without the object reaches 50.3% and drops the box 43% of the time. The ablations are the real lesson: remove the object from low-level training and success falls to 2.9%. Remove the command clipping, 15.5%. Remove the noise on the commands during training, 3.0%. Every piece matters, but "train with the payload" matters most. On hardware the same single policy transfers zero-shot, handles 0.5 to 7 kg, recovers when they knock the box out of its hands, and completes multi-box sequences. At 7 kg the joints hit their effort limits and the robot eventually overheats, which is a refreshingly honest result.

**Caveats.** The box pose comes from an external motion-capture system, not cameras on the robot. The motion library has only four types of motion, so anything new means new mocap.

**Why this matters for your walker.** Two takeaways you can use before you ever pick up a box.

1. *Train with the disturbances you'll actually meet, even unobserved.* Your robot will carry a battery that shifts slightly, cables that pull, a head that's heavier than the CAD model says. Randomizing these as physical loads in simulation, not just as noise on observations, is cheap and is the same idea as HLO-AMP's invisible box.
2. *Clip your interface to what you trained on.* When you later put a navigation or task layer on top of your walking policy, write down the command ranges the walker saw in training and clamp to them. "Walk at 1.8 m/s" sent to a policy trained up to 1.2 m/s is how robots fall in demos.

Farshidian spent years at ETH's Robotic Systems Lab before RAI, so this paper is close to home. RAI also has a Zurich office with an open research internship (see below).

## New papers worth reading

### [The old school answers back: online iLQR-MPC on a G1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04204)
A useful counterweight to everything above. Xiang Li and colleagues plan footsteps and contact forces together in one model predictive control problem, using a single rigid body model of the robot, then turn that plan into joint torques at 500 Hz with a whole-body controller. No policy training at all. It replans at 100 Hz with a mean solve time of 8.9 ms, and on a real Unitree G1 it recovers from pushes of 245 N forward and 175 N sideways. Why read it: RL policies are robust but opaque. When this controller takes a step to the side, you can point at the cost term that made it do so. Many good teams use a controller like this as a baseline, a teacher, or a safety fallback for their learned walker.

### [AIRoA-0: a VLA that never saw a robot during training](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03815)
A 20-author team trained a two-armed manipulation policy on 16,151 hours of people using YUBI, an open-source handheld gripper with a camera on it. No teleoperation, no RL, no corrections on the robot. The robot only shows up at test time. With just one wrist camera per arm, it sorts small parts, swaps a robot vacuum's mop pads, unzips a pencil case and folds towels at a site it never trained in. 78% average success across 10 tasks, against 27% for the best of four VLA baselines post-trained on the same data. Post-training takes under four hours on one 8-GPU H200 node. Dataset, weights and recipe are being released. [Project page](https://a-0.airoa.io).

### Also new
- [CRONOS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05080): a benchmark for RL fine-tuning of VLAs without scene resets. On a real robot nobody resets the scene for you after every attempt, and naive fine-tuning falls apart in that setting. [Project page](https://embodiedai-ntu.github.io/cronos/).
- [arXiv 2611.04839](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04839): scores candidate command sequences for humanoid loco-manipulation by imagining their outcome in a latent space before executing any of them.
- [MABEL, arXiv 2611.04083](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04083): an open-source humanoid-style mobile bimanual robot with whole-body teleoperation and an imitation learning pipeline.

## Founder radar

### Seed: the data is the product
Put AIRoA-0 next to HLO-AMP and a pattern appears. Both get their capability from human data collected without a robot: one from handheld grippers, the other from motion capture of a person carrying boxes. The bottleneck for robot learning is turning into "who has the right human demonstrations, cleaned, retargeted and labeled". AIRoA's 16,000 hours is a giant dataset. HLO-AMP's ablation says the motion data is only useful if it includes the object.

Three questions worth an evening:
- Who sells object-aware motion capture, meaning human motion plus the 3D pose and mass of whatever they're holding, ready to retarget onto a G1, an H1 or a custom robot? Today every lab does this itself.
- Could a cheap rig, a handheld gripper plus one tracked box plus a phone, record loco-manipulation data at home, the way UMI did for tabletop manipulation?
- Retargeting is still where most of the pain lives (wrong proportions, joint limits, feet sliding). A tool that takes any mocap clip and reports "this is trackable on your robot, this isn't, and here's why" is useful to every humanoid team on the planet, including yours.

## Outside your bubble

### [Voyager 1 is about to be one light-day from Earth](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261103003152.htm)
Around 18 November, Voyager 1 will be about 25.9 billion kilometers away, far enough that its radio signal takes a full 24 hours to reach us. Ask it a question on Monday morning, get the answer Wednesday morning. It's moving at roughly 61,000 km/h through interstellar space, on hardware launched in 1977.

Two recent episodes show what operating at that distance is like. In early September, JPL engineers swapped three onboard devices for lower-power alternatives (nicknamed the "Big Bang") because the nuclear generators lose about 4 watts every year. Without it, another science instrument might have been switched off as early as summer 2027. Then on 18 and 19 October the Deep Space Network couldn't find the signal at all. It came back on 20 October, about 48 hours later. A set of thrusters had stopped working and fault protection had automatically switched to the backups. Nobody told it to. Nobody could have, in time.

That's the part worth thinking about from a robotics seat. With a 48-hour round trip, teleoperation is impossible and every recovery behavior has to live on board, written decades ago for failures nobody could predict exactly. Voyager is the most extreme case of a problem your humanoid has in milliseconds: when the loop to a human (or to a planner) is too slow, the low-level system has to be able to save itself. Good fault protection is a quiet, unglamorous kind of autonomy, and it has kept this machine running for 49 years.
