# Play tag, learn to run

*2026-11-26*

Good Thursday morning. The last two days were about bolting new skills onto a walker you already trust. Today's main paper goes the other way: what if you skip the carefully hand-tuned reward entirely and just let two humanoids chase each other around a simulator? Turns out they invent sprinting, dodging and tackling on their own. It's the oldest trick in game AI (self-play), now applied to legs.

After that: why your policy's inference time matters more than you think, a warning about "sample more and pick the best" for VLAs, a world model that finally learns what a failed grasp looks like, a big bank betting on the first humanoid stock, a MERL internship that isn't PhD-only, and the ice age we accidentally cancelled.

## The big one: humanoids that learn agility by playing tag

**[An Adversarial Multi-Agent Pursuit Game for Locomotor Agility Pretraining](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33407)** (Nathan A.C. Hogg, Matt Hyatt, Neil Klingensmith, George K. Thiruvathukal), submitted 24 November.

Here's the problem the authors start from. There are two common ways to train a humanoid to move:

1. **Dense, hand-shaped rewards.** Reward forward velocity, penalise torque, reward foot clearance, penalise body tilt, and so on. Works, but the policy tends to find *one* behaviour that maxes out the reward and then stops getting better.
2. **Reference tracking.** Collect motion capture and reward the robot for copying it. Looks natural, but the robot can only move in ways a human already showed it. Motion generation and motor control live in separate boxes.

Their alternative is borrowed from Go and StarCraft: **self-play**. They built **MJTag**, a suite of tag games in a physics simulator. One humanoid is the pursuer, one is the evader. The reward is sparse: basically "did you tag them / did you escape". The policy is **role-conditioned** (it's told whether it's chasing or fleeing) and is a low-level locomotion controller that can optionally take planar velocity commands, so you can still steer it like a normal walker afterwards.

They train with **league play**, meaning a policy plays against a pool of past and current opponents instead of just the latest one. That's the standard fix for self-play going in circles (A beats B, B beats C, C beats A, forever). Out of this, without anyone writing a "dodge" reward, the controller develops **dodging, sprinting and tackling**.

Why it's interesting: in a competitive game, your opponent keeps getting better, so the task keeps getting harder. It's a curriculum that writes itself. A dense velocity reward is a fixed target; an opponent is a moving one.

Caveats, and they're real: the abstract doesn't name a robot, doesn't mention hardware experiments, and there's no code link. This is a simulation result about *emergent behaviour*, not a recipe for a robust walker yet. Tackling also isn't something you want your club's robot learning anytime soon.

**What to take for your club.** Don't throw out your reward function for first steps. A stable walk on flat ground is still best learned with a standard, shaped reward (velocity tracking plus regularisers). But keep this in your back pocket for phase two:

- Once you have a base walker, an **adversary** is a cheap way to make it robust. The simplest version isn't a second humanoid, it's a learned "pusher" that applies forces to your robot and gets rewarded when it falls. That's classic robust adversarial RL, and it's much easier than multi-agent tag.
- The **role-conditioning plus velocity command** design is worth copying: one network, a small conditioning input that switches behaviour, and an outer interface that stays a normal velocity command.

## Robot learning lesson: your control loop has a deadline

**[iMFPO: Improving Mean Flow Policy Optimization with Drift-Aware Velocity Alignment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33922)** (Lisiyu Pan, Ting Huang, Zeyu Zhang, Hao Tang), submitted 25 November. Code: [GitHub](https://github.com/AIGeeksGroup/iMFPO).

Background you should have in your head. **Flow** and **diffusion** policies generate an action by starting from noise and refining it over many small steps. They're very expressive (good at representing "there are several valid ways to do this"), which is why they power most modern manipulation VLAs. The catch: many steps means slow.

The headline number in this paper: on a Unitree Go2, action generation drops from **15.57 ms** with a 64-step flow policy to **0.41 ms** with their one-step version. They also test on G1, H1 and Spot.

Why that number matters: a locomotion policy typically runs at **50 Hz**, which means **20 ms per step** for *everything*: reading sensors, state estimation, running the network, sending commands. Spend 15 ms generating the action and you've used most of your budget. And latency isn't just a speed issue; it's a sim-to-real issue, because your simulator usually assumes the action arrives instantly.

The trick, simplified: instead of learning the *instantaneous* velocity at each point along the noise-to-action path (which you then have to integrate step by step), learn the **average velocity over an interval**. If you know the average velocity from noise to action, one big jump gets you there. That's the "mean flow" idea. iMFPO's contribution is keeping that consistent during online RL, where the action distribution keeps shifting, using a correction term computed with a Jacobian-vector product, plus a **frozen PPO teacher** that gives action-level guidance during training.

Practical takeaway for you: a plain PPO policy with a Gaussian output is still the sensible default for learning to walk. It's fast, simple, and everyone's sim-to-real tricks are built around it. Read this paper as a map for later, when you want an expressive policy for manipulation tasks and need it to fit in your control loop. And **measure your inference latency on the actual onboard computer now**, then simulate that delay in training. It's a classic cause of "worked in sim, shakes on the robot".

## Seed for your brain: more samples can make things worse

**[Best-of-N Test-Time Steering Can Lead to Reward Hacking in VLA Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33398)** (Vanshaj Khattar, Jing Liu, Toshiaki Koike-Akino, Ming Jin, Ye Wang), submitted 24 November.

A popular trick: sample N candidate actions from a VLA, score them with a verifier, run the best one. More candidates, better pick, right? The authors test this with π0.5 on 60 LIBERO-Safety cases (10 repeats each) and find that **"increasing N does not always improve performance when the verifier is inaccurate."** With enough candidates, you start selecting the ones that fool the verifier rather than the ones that do the task. Calibrating the verifier and picking with a softmax instead of a hard max helps in some settings.

This is Goodhart's law in a robot: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. The larger N gets, the harder you're optimising against your verifier's blind spots.

Pair it with **[Beyond the Happy Path: Generalizing Robot World Models from Simulated Failures](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33925)** (Zhiyue Zhao, ... Chunhua Shen), submitted 25 November. Their observation is brutal: across five pretrained action-conditioned world models, predicting object motion is at most **10.4% better than just assuming nothing moves**, and two models do *worse* than that. The reason: they were trained on successful demos, so they've never seen an object slip or tip over. Their fix is **62,000 simulated trajectories of failed and successful grasps**, which cut object trajectory error by 28.2% on Cosmos3 Nano (35.0% on unseen objects), with improvements carrying over to real failure videos.

The seed: **failure data is underpriced.** Everyone collects and shares successful demos. Hardly anyone collects, labels and sells clean failures (slips, falls, missed grasps, with contact ground truth), even though both verifiers and world models are starving for them. A "failure dataset as a service" for robot learning teams is a small, concrete startup idea. And closer to home: when your humanoid falls in sim, **don't just reset**. Log those rollouts. They're the training data for the fall detector and recovery policy you'll want later.

## Quick hits from the arXiv batch

- **[Pivot-walking a heavy box with a humanoid](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33828)** (Hidaka, Chen, Kawaharazuka). One RL policy handles stepping, balance and manipulation together to "walk" a box that's too heavy to lift, by pivoting it corner to corner. Transfers to hardware without measuring the box's state. A nice example of loco-manipulation as a single policy.
- **[Does initial noise bias VLAs?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33816)** (Lee, Shin, Jeon). Diffusion-action VLAs inherit a directional bias from the starting noise. Fixes: antithetic training (pair each noise sample with its mirror) and subtracting the mean at inference. Cheap, worth knowing.
- **[Why does sensor depth fail learned manipulation policies?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33352)** (Rasool, Pack, Ahmed). It's the *holes* in the depth image, not the noise, that wipe out the benefit of depth for ACT-style policies. Choose cameras by depth coverage.
- **[Fine-tune 1.1% of a world-action model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33963)** (Zhu, Li, Luo). Find the attention heads responsible for grasp targets and motion, fine-tune only those, match full fine-tuning. Same "touch as little as possible" theme as the adapter papers this week.
- **[Image-goal navigation for agile quadrotors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34046)** (Nascivera, Geles, Scaramuzza). From Davide Scaramuzza's group in Zurich: images straight to thrust and body rates, no state estimation, zero-shot to the real world. Good reading for how they distil a privileged teacher into a vision student.

## Outside the bubble: the ice age we interrupted

A study in *Science* this week ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261123005350.htm)) makes a startling claim: Earth's next glacial period may have **already started, about 2,000 years ago, and then global warming stopped it**.

The evidence is on Baffin Island in Canada. The Barnes Ice Cap, roughly the size of Delaware, is the last remnant of the Laurentide Ice Sheet, which once covered much of North America and was more than two miles thick. As the ice cap retreats today, it's uncovering ancient plants that can be radiocarbon-dated. Those dates, from a team led by Nicolás Young at Columbia's Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory, show the ice was at its smallest about 3,000 years ago and **started growing again about 2,000 years ago**. Then summer warming reversed that growth by the 1960s, and the ice has been shrinking since.

"It was just getting started, everything was normal, the next glaciation was about to happen, and yet it stopped," Young says. Getting back to continental size would have taken around 30,000 years, so nobody was about to be buried in ice. Models suggest the Barnes Ice Cap itself could vanish within a few centuries.

The caveat: this conflicts with earlier climate modelling, which put the next glaciation thousands to tens of thousands of years away, so expect pushback.

Why it's worth your two minutes: it's a beautiful piece of detective work using dead plants as a clock, and it reframes "climate change" from "we're nudging the dial" to "we flipped a planetary-scale switch that was moving in the other direction". Systems with long, slow feedback loops are hard to reason about. That's true of climate, and of RL training runs too.
